# Complex Analysis Topic 4.3: Laurent Series

Taylor series represent analytic functions as power series with only non-negative powers: $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n z^n$. But what about functions with singularities? Near an isolated singularity, a function isn't analytic, so it can't have a Taylor series. Yet there's still structure—we can represent the function as a **Laurent series**, which includes negative powers: $\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} a_n z^n$. The positive powers form the **analytic part** (like Taylor series), while the negative powers form the **principal part**, capturing the singular behavior.

Laurent series converge in **annuli** (rings) rather than disks. For instance, $\frac{1}{z(z-1)}$ has singularities at $z=0$ and $z=1$. In the annulus $0 < |z| < 1$, it has a Laurent expansion with both positive and negative powers of $z$. The coefficient $a_{-1}$ (the coefficient of $1/z$) is special—it's called the **residue**, which becomes crucial for integration via the residue theorem. Laurent series are the key to understanding and computing around singularities.

This article develops Laurent series theory from foundations. We'll define Laurent series and their convergence in annuli, compute Laurent expansions using algebraic manipulation and known series, identify principal and analytic parts, understand how the residue encodes singular behavior, and use Laurent series to classify singularities. You'll master finding Laurent expansions, determining convergence regions, and extracting residues—essential tools for complex integration.

## Definition and Convergence

A **Laurent series** centered at $z_0$ is:

$$f(z) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} a_n(z - z_0)^n = \cdots + \frac{a_{-2}}{(z-z_0)^2} + \frac{a_{-1}}{z-z_0} + a_0 + a_1(z-z_0) + \cdots$$

**Principal part**: $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{a_{-n}}{(z-z_0)^n}$ (negative powers)

**Analytic part**: $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n(z-z_0)^n$ (non-negative powers)

**Convergence**: Laurent series converges in an **annulus** $r < |z - z_0| < R$ where:
- Principal part converges for $|z - z_0| > r$
- Analytic part converges for $|z - z_0| < R$

### Example 1: Simple Laurent Series

Identify the principal and analytic parts of $f(z) = \frac{1}{z} + z + z^2$.

**Solution:**

**Principal part**: $\frac{1}{z}$ (only term with negative power)

**Analytic part**: $z + z^2$ (non-negative powers)

**Convergence**: Needs $z \neq 0$ (for principal part). Converges for $0 < |z| < \infty$.

**Answer:** Principal part: $\frac{1}{z}$; analytic part: $z + z^2$

### Example 2: Laurent vs. Taylor

Can $f(z) = \frac{1}{z-1}$ have a Taylor series at $z = 0$?

**Solution:**

$f$ has singularity at $z = 1$.

Taylor series at $z=0$ would converge in disk $|z| < R$ where $R$ = distance to nearest singularity = $|1-0| = 1$.

For $|z| < 1$:
$$\frac{1}{z-1} = -\frac{1}{1-z} = -\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} z^n$$

This is a Taylor series (no negative powers), converges for $|z| < 1$ ✓

**Answer:** Yes, has Taylor series for $|z| < 1$; no singularity at center

### Example 3: Laurent Series Needed

For $f(z) = \frac{1}{z(z-1)}$ at $z = 0$, can we have Taylor series?

**Solution:**

$f$ has singularity **at the center** $z = 0$.

Cannot have Taylor series at $z=0$ (not analytic there).

Need Laurent series to capture behavior near $z=0$.

**Answer:** No Taylor series; singularity at center requires Laurent series

## Computing Laurent Series

### Example 4: Geometric Series Method

Find Laurent series of $\frac{1}{z-2}$ for $|z| > 2$.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{1}{z-2} = \frac{1}{z(1 - 2/z)} = \frac{1}{z} \cdot \frac{1}{1-2/z}$$

Geometric series (converges when $|2/z| < 1$, i.e., $|z| > 2$):
$$\frac{1}{1-2/z} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{2}{z}\right)^n = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{2^n}{z^n}$$

$$\frac{1}{z-2} = \frac{1}{z}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{2^n}{z^n} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{2^n}{z^{n+1}} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{2^{n-1}}{z^n}$$

All negative powers (principal part only).

**Answer:** $\frac{1}{z-2} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{2^{n-1}}{z^n}$ for $|z| > 2$

### Example 5: Partial Fractions

Find Laurent series of $f(z) = \frac{1}{z(z-1)}$ in $0 < |z| < 1$.

**Solution:**

Partial fractions:
$$\frac{1}{z(z-1)} = \frac{A}{z} + \frac{B}{z-1}$$

$$1 = A(z-1) + Bz$$

At $z=0$: $1 = -A \implies A = -1$
At $z=1$: $1 = B$

$$\frac{1}{z(z-1)} = -\frac{1}{z} + \frac{1}{z-1}$$

For $|z| < 1$:
$$\frac{1}{z-1} = -\frac{1}{1-z} = -\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} z^n$$

$$f(z) = -\frac{1}{z} - \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} z^n = -\frac{1}{z} - 1 - z - z^2 - \cdots$$

**Answer:** $f(z) = -\frac{1}{z} - \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} z^n$ for $0 < |z| < 1$

### Example 6: Different Annulus

Find Laurent series of $f(z) = \frac{1}{z(z-1)}$ in $|z| > 1$.

**Solution:**

Start from partial fractions: $f(z) = -\frac{1}{z} + \frac{1}{z-1}$

For $|z| > 1$, expand $\frac{1}{z-1}$ differently:

$$\frac{1}{z-1} = \frac{1}{z(1-1/z)} = \frac{1}{z}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{z^n} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{z^{n+1}} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{z^n}$$

$$f(z) = -\frac{1}{z} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{z^n} = \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} \frac{1}{z^n}$$

All negative powers (principal part only).

**Answer:** $f(z) = \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} \frac{1}{z^n}$ for $|z| > 1$

### Example 7: Using Known Series

Find Laurent series of $e^{1/z}$ for $z \neq 0$.

**Solution:**

Start with Taylor series: $e^w = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{w^n}{n!}$

Substitute $w = 1/z$:

$$e^{1/z} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(1/z)^n}{n!} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n! z^n}$$

$$= 1 + \frac{1}{z} + \frac{1}{2!z^2} + \frac{1}{3!z^3} + \cdots$$

**Principal part**: Infinitely many terms

**Convergence**: $0 < |z| < \infty$

**Answer:** $e^{1/z} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n!z^n}$ for $z \neq 0$

### Example 8: Series with Analytic and Principal Parts

Find Laurent series of $\frac{z}{(z-1)^2}$ for $0 < |z-1| < \infty$.

**Solution:**

Write $w = z - 1$, so $z = w + 1$:

$$\frac{z}{(z-1)^2} = \frac{w+1}{w^2} = \frac{1}{w} + \frac{1}{w^2}$$

In terms of $z$:
$$= \frac{1}{z-1} + \frac{1}{(z-1)^2}$$

**Principal part**: $\frac{1}{z-1} + \frac{1}{(z-1)^2}$

**Analytic part**: $0$ (no non-negative powers)

**Answer:** $\frac{z}{(z-1)^2} = \frac{1}{z-1} + \frac{1}{(z-1)^2}$ for $z \neq 1$

## Coefficient Formula

For Laurent series $\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} a_n(z-z_0)^n$ converging in annulus $r < |z-z_0| < R$:

$$a_n = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C \frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^{n+1}} \, dz$$

where $C$ is any circle $|z-z_0| = \rho$ with $r < \rho < R$.

**Special case** ($n=-1$): The **residue**
$$a_{-1} = \text{Res}(f, z_0) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C f(z) \, dz$$

### Example 9: Finding Residue from Laurent Series

For $f(z) = \frac{e^z}{z^2}$, find the residue at $z=0$.

**Solution:**

Expand $e^z$:
$$e^z = 1 + z + \frac{z^2}{2!} + \frac{z^3}{3!} + \cdots$$

$$\frac{e^z}{z^2} = \frac{1}{z^2}\left(1 + z + \frac{z^2}{2} + \frac{z^3}{6} + \cdots\right)$$
$$= \frac{1}{z^2} + \frac{1}{z} + \frac{1}{2} + \frac{z}{6} + \cdots$$

Coefficient of $\frac{1}{z}$: $a_{-1} = 1$

**Answer:** $\text{Res}(f, 0) = 1$

### Example 10: Multiple Residues

Find residue of $\frac{1}{z^2-1}$ at $z = 1$.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{1}{z^2-1} = \frac{1}{(z-1)(z+1)}$$

Near $z=1$, write $w = z-1$:
$$\frac{1}{(z-1)(z+1)} = \frac{1}{w(w+2)}$$

Partial fractions:
$$\frac{1}{w(w+2)} = \frac{A}{w} + \frac{B}{w+2}$$

$$1 = A(w+2) + Bw$$

At $w=0$: $1 = 2A \implies A = 1/2$

$$\frac{1}{z^2-1} = \frac{1/2}{z-1} + \frac{\text{analytic}}{z-1}$$

Residue = coefficient of $\frac{1}{z-1}$ = $1/2$

**Answer:** $\text{Res}(f, 1) = 1/2$

### Example 11: Residue of Sine Function

Find residue of $\frac{\sin z}{z^4}$ at $z=0$.

**Solution:**

$$\sin z = z - \frac{z^3}{3!} + \frac{z^5}{5!} - \cdots$$

$$\frac{\sin z}{z^4} = \frac{1}{z^4}\left(z - \frac{z^3}{6} + \frac{z^5}{120} - \cdots\right)$$
$$= \frac{1}{z^3} - \frac{1}{6z} + \frac{z}{120} - \cdots$$

Coefficient of $\frac{1}{z}$: $a_{-1} = -\frac{1}{6}$

**Answer:** $\text{Res}(f, 0) = -1/6$

## Uniqueness of Laurent Series

**Uniqueness**: In a given annulus, the Laurent series representation is unique.

Different annuli around the same point may have different Laurent expansions.

### Example 12: Same Function, Different Regions

Compare Laurent series of $\frac{1}{z(z-2)}$ in:
(a) $0 < |z| < 2$
(b) $|z| > 2$

**Solution:**

Partial fractions: $\frac{1}{z(z-2)} = \frac{1/2}{z} - \frac{1/2}{z-2}$

**(a) $0 < |z| < 2$**:

For $|z| < 2$:
$$\frac{1}{z-2} = -\frac{1}{2(1-z/2)} = -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^n$$

$$\frac{1}{z(z-2)} = \frac{1}{2z} + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^n}{2^{n+1}}$$

**(b) $|z| > 2$**:

For $|z| > 2$:
$$\frac{1}{z-2} = \frac{1}{z(1-2/z)} = \frac{1}{z}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{2^n}{z^n}$$

$$\frac{1}{z(z-2)} = \frac{1}{2z} - \frac{1}{2z}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{2^n}{z^n}$$

Different series in different annuli.

**Answer:** Different Laurent expansions depending on region

### Example 13: Verifying Uniqueness

If two Laurent series equal $f$ in same annulus, show coefficients match.

**Solution:**

Suppose $\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} a_n z^n = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} b_n z^n$ in annulus.

Multiply by $\frac{1}{z^{k+1}}$ and integrate around circle in annulus:

$$\oint \frac{1}{z^{k+1}}\sum a_n z^n dz = \oint \frac{1}{z^{k+1}}\sum b_n z^n dz$$

By orthogonality: $\oint z^n dz = 0$ for $n \neq -1$ and $= 2\pi i$ for $n=-1$.

Only $n=k$ term survives: $2\pi i a_k = 2\pi i b_k$

Therefore $a_k = b_k$ for all $k$.

**Answer:** Coefficients must match (uniqueness proven)

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Laurent series $\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} a_n(z-z_0)^n$ converges in annulus $r < |z-z_0| < R$
Ring-shaped region between two circles
Principal part converges for $|z-z_0| > r$
Analytic part converges for $|z-z_0| < R$
Overlap region is annulus

**Problem 2: B**
Principal part = $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{a_{-n}}{(z-z_0)^n}$ (negative powers)
Captures singular behavior
Example: $\frac{1}{z} + \frac{2}{z^2}$ is principal part
Analytic part has non-negative powers
Principal part may be finite or infinite

**Problem 3: B**
Residue = $a_{-1}$ = coefficient of $(z-z_0)^{-1}$
Notation: $\text{Res}(f, z_0) = a_{-1}$
Critical for residue theorem: $\oint_C f = 2\pi i \sum \text{residues}$
Only $1/z$ term contributes to integral
Foundation of complex integration

**Problem 4: B**
$\frac{1}{z^2}$ has singularity at $z=0$ (pole of order 2)
Laurent series: $\frac{1}{z^2}$ (single term)
Only principal part (negative power)
No analytic part (not differentiable at origin)
Cannot be Taylor series (singularity at center)

**Problem 5: B**
Same function, different annuli → different Laurent series
Example: $\frac{1}{z(z-1)}$ has different expansions in $0<|z|<1$ vs $|z|>1$
Each annulus has unique representation
Depends on which singularities enclosed
Series must reflect behavior in specific region
