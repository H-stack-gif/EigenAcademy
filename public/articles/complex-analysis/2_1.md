# Complex Analysis Topic 2.1: Complex Differentiation

In real calculus, differentiability at a point means the function has a well-defined slope—the limit of difference quotients exists as you approach from the left or right. Complex differentiability is far more restrictive: the limit $\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(z+h) - f(z)}{h}$ must exist regardless of which direction $h$ approaches zero in the complex plane. This single requirement—that the limit be independent of approach direction—imposes incredible rigidity on the function. A function that is complex differentiable at a point turns out to be infinitely differentiable there, and even equals its Taylor series in a neighborhood.

This profound difference between real and complex differentiation stems from geometry. Real functions can be kinked, cornered, or have different left and right derivatives. Complex functions, if differentiable at all, must preserve angles (be conformal) and satisfy the Cauchy-Riemann equations linking their real and imaginary parts. A complex-differentiable function cannot have a "corner" in the complex plane—the structure is too rigid. Functions that are differentiable in a region are called **analytic** or **holomorphic**, and they form the central objects of study in complex analysis.

This article develops the theory of complex differentiation from first principles. We'll define the complex derivative, explore its geometric meaning, discover why it's more restrictive than real differentiation, compute derivatives using standard rules, and understand the concept of analyticity. You'll learn to distinguish functions that are complex differentiable from those that aren't, master differentiation techniques, and appreciate why complex-differentiable functions have such remarkable properties.

## Definition of Complex Derivative

Let $f: D \to \mathbb{C}$ where $D \subseteq \mathbb{C}$. The function $f$ is **differentiable** at $z_0 \in D$ if the limit

$$f'(z_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(z_0 + h) - f(z_0)}{h}$$

exists, where $h \to 0$ in $\mathbb{C}$ (from any direction).

**Key point**: The limit must be the **same** regardless of how $h \to 0$.

If $f$ is differentiable at every point in an open set $D$, we say $f$ is **analytic** (or **holomorphic**) in $D$.

### Example 1: Differentiability of $f(z) = z^2$

Show that $f(z) = z^2$ is differentiable and find $f'(z)$.

**Solution:**

$$f'(z) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(z+h)^2 - z^2}{h}$$
$$= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{z^2 + 2zh + h^2 - z^2}{h}$$
$$= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2zh + h^2}{h}$$
$$= \lim_{h \to 0} (2z + h) = 2z$$

This limit exists and is independent of how $h \to 0$.

**Answer:** $f'(z) = 2z$ (differentiable everywhere)

### Example 2: Polynomial Differentiation

If $f(z) = z^3 + 2z^2 - 3z + 1$, find $f'(z)$.

**Solution:**

Using linearity and power rule (proven same way as Example 1):

$$f'(z) = 3z^2 + 2(2z) - 3(1) + 0 = 3z^2 + 4z - 3$$

**Answer:** $f'(z) = 3z^2 + 4z - 3$

### Example 3: Non-differentiability of $\overline{z}$

Show that $f(z) = \overline{z}$ is not differentiable at any point.

**Solution:**

Let $z_0$ be arbitrary. Test the difference quotient:

$$\frac{f(z_0 + h) - f(z_0)}{h} = \frac{\overline{z_0 + h} - \overline{z_0}}{h} = \frac{\overline{z_0} + \overline{h} - \overline{z_0}}{h} = \frac{\overline{h}}{h}$$

**Approach 1**: Let $h = t$ (real, $t \to 0^+$):
$$\frac{\overline{h}}{h} = \frac{t}{t} = 1$$

**Approach 2**: Let $h = it$ (imaginary, $t \to 0^+$):
$$\frac{\overline{h}}{h} = \frac{\overline{it}}{it} = \frac{-it}{it} = -1$$

Different limits along different paths means derivative doesn't exist.

**Answer:** Not differentiable anywhere (limit depends on direction)

### Example 4: Geometric Interpretation

If $f$ is differentiable at $z_0$ with $f'(z_0) \neq 0$, what does $f'(z_0)$ represent geometrically?

**Solution:**

Write $f'(z_0) = re^{i\alpha}$ in polar form.

For small $h$:
$$f(z_0 + h) \approx f(z_0) + f'(z_0)h = f(z_0) + re^{i\alpha}h$$

The transformation locally:
- **Scales** by factor $r = |f'(z_0)|$
- **Rotates** by angle $\alpha = \arg(f'(z_0))$

**Geometric meaning**: $f$ acts like a rigid rotation and scaling near $z_0$.

This implies $f$ **preserves angles** (is **conformal**) at points where $f'(z_0) \neq 0$.

**Answer:** $|f'(z_0)|$ = local scaling factor; $\arg(f'(z_0))$ = local rotation angle

## Differentiation Rules

The standard calculus differentiation rules hold for complex functions:

**Linearity**: $(af + bg)' = af' + bg'$

**Product rule**: $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$

**Quotient rule**: $\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}$

**Chain rule**: $(f \circ g)' = (f' \circ g) \cdot g'$

### Example 5: Product Rule Application

If $f(z) = z^2e^z$, find $f'(z)$.

**Solution:**

Using product rule with $u(z) = z^2$ and $v(z) = e^z$:

$$f'(z) = (z^2)' e^z + z^2(e^z)'$$
$$= 2z \cdot e^z + z^2 \cdot e^z$$
$$= e^z(2z + z^2)$$
$$= z(z + 2)e^z$$

**Answer:** $f'(z) = z(z+2)e^z$

### Example 6: Quotient Rule

Differentiate $f(z) = \frac{z^2 + 1}{z - 1}$.

**Solution:**

$$f'(z) = \frac{(z^2+1)'(z-1) - (z^2+1)(z-1)'}{(z-1)^2}$$
$$= \frac{2z(z-1) - (z^2+1)(1)}{(z-1)^2}$$
$$= \frac{2z^2 - 2z - z^2 - 1}{(z-1)^2}$$
$$= \frac{z^2 - 2z - 1}{(z-1)^2}$$

**Answer:** $f'(z) = \frac{z^2 - 2z - 1}{(z-1)^2}$

### Example 7: Chain Rule

If $f(z) = e^{z^2}$, find $f'(z)$.

**Solution:**

Let $u = z^2$, so $f(z) = e^u$.

Chain rule:
$$f'(z) = e^u \cdot \frac{du}{dz} = e^{z^2} \cdot 2z = 2ze^{z^2}$$

**Answer:** $f'(z) = 2ze^{z^2}$

## Analyticity

A function is **analytic** at $z_0$ if it's differentiable in some neighborhood of $z_0$.

A function is **analytic in a region** $D$ if it's differentiable at every point of $D$.

**Entire function**: Analytic on all of $\mathbb{C}$ (e.g., polynomials, $e^z$, $\sin z$, $\cos z$)

### Example 8: Analytic vs. Differentiable at a Point

Is there a difference between "differentiable at $z_0$" and "analytic at $z_0$"?

**Solution:**

**Differentiable at $z_0$**: Derivative exists at that single point

**Analytic at $z_0$**: Differentiable at $z_0$ AND in some neighborhood around $z_0$

**Example of difference**: Consider (artificially constructed) function that is differentiable only at origin but nowhere else in any neighborhood.

However, **remarkable theorem**: If $f$ is differentiable in an open disk around $z_0$, then $f$ is automatically **infinitely differentiable** and equals its Taylor series there.

So in practice, for "nice" functions, differentiability in a region implies analyticity.

**Answer:** Technically different (point vs. neighborhood), but in complex analysis, differentiability in a region implies much stronger properties

### Example 9: Identifying Entire Functions

Which of the following are entire functions?

(a) $f(z) = z^3 - 2z + 1$
(b) $f(z) = \frac{1}{z}$
(c) $f(z) = e^{-z^2}$
(d) $f(z) = |z|^2$

**Solution:**

(a) Polynomial: differentiable everywhere. **Entire** ✓

(b) $\frac{1}{z}$: Not defined at $z = 0$, so not analytic there. **Not entire** ✗

(c) $e^{-z^2}$: Composition of entire functions (polynomial and exponential). **Entire** ✓

(d) $|z|^2 = z\overline{z}$: Contains $\overline{z}$, which is not differentiable anywhere. **Not entire** ✗

**Answer:** (a) and (c) are entire; (b) and (d) are not

### Example 10: Singularities

At what points is $f(z) = \frac{z^2 + 1}{z^2 - 1}$ analytic?

**Solution:**

Function is quotient of polynomials (rational function).

Analytic everywhere except where denominator is zero:
$$z^2 - 1 = 0 \implies z = \pm 1$$

**Singularities** (points where not analytic): $z = 1$ and $z = -1$

**Domain of analyticity**: $\mathbb{C} \setminus \{-1, 1\}$

**Answer:** Analytic on $\mathbb{C} \setminus \{-1, 1\}$ (everywhere except $z = \pm 1$)

## Direction-Independence of Limit

The requirement that the derivative limit be independent of direction is extremely restrictive.

### Example 11: Testing Direction Independence

For $f(z) = z^2$, verify that $f'(i)$ is the same when approaching via different paths.

**Solution:**

$$f'(i) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(i+h)^2 - i^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{i^2 + 2ih + h^2 - i^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}(2i + h) = 2i$$

**Path 1** (real approach $h = t$, $t \to 0$):
$$\lim_{t \to 0}(2i + t) = 2i$$ ✓

**Path 2** (imaginary approach $h = it$, $t \to 0$):
$$\lim_{t \to 0}(2i + it) = 2i$$ ✓

**Path 3** (diagonal $h = t(1+i)$, $t \to 0$):
$$\lim_{t \to 0}(2i + t(1+i)) = 2i$$ ✓

All paths give same limit.

**Answer:** $f'(i) = 2i$ independent of approach direction

### Example 12: Why $|z|^2$ Fails

Show that $f(z) = |z|^2$ is not differentiable at $z = 1$ by testing two directions.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{f(1+h) - f(1)}{h} = \frac{|1+h|^2 - 1}{h} = \frac{(1+h)\overline{(1+h)} - 1}{h}$$
$$= \frac{(1+h)(1+\overline{h}) - 1}{h} = \frac{1 + \overline{h} + h + |h|^2 - 1}{h}$$
$$= \frac{h + \overline{h} + |h|^2}{h}$$

**Real approach** ($h = t > 0$):
$$\frac{t + t + t^2}{t} = \frac{2t + t^2}{t} = 2 + t \to 2$$

**Imaginary approach** ($h = it$, $t > 0$):
$$\frac{it + (-it) + t^2}{it} = \frac{t^2}{it} = \frac{t}{i} = -it \to 0$$

Different limits: $2 \neq 0$.

**Answer:** Not differentiable at $z = 1$ (or anywhere except origin)

### Example 13: Differentiability at Origin for $|z|^2$

Check if $f(z) = |z|^2$ is differentiable at $z = 0$.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{f(0+h) - f(0)}{h} = \frac{|h|^2}{h} = \frac{h\overline{h}}{h} = \overline{h}$$

As $h \to 0$, we have $\overline{h} \to 0$.

But this is NOT independent of direction:
- If $h = t$: $\overline{h} = t$ (approaches 0 from right)
- If $h = it$: $\overline{h} = -it$ (approaches 0 from below)

The limit is 0, but the **manner** of approach differs.



**Answer:** Appears to have limit 0

## Higher Derivatives

If $f$ is analytic in region $D$, then $f'$ is also analytic in $D$, and we can define $f''$, $f'''$, etc.

**Remarkable fact**: A function analytic in a region is **infinitely differentiable** there.

### Example 14: Second Derivative

If $f(z) = \frac{1}{z}$, find $f''(z)$ where defined.

**Solution:**

$$f'(z) = -\frac{1}{z^2}$$

$$f''(z) = -\frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac{1}{z^2}\right) = -\frac{d}{dz}(z^{-2})$$
$$= -(-2)z^{-3} = \frac{2}{z^3}$$

**Domain**: $z \neq 0$

**Answer:** $f''(z) = \frac{2}{z^3}$

### Example 15: Pattern in Derivatives

Find $\frac{d^n}{dz^n}(e^z)$.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{d}{dz}(e^z) = e^z$$
$$\frac{d^2}{dz^2}(e^z) = e^z$$
$$\frac{d^3}{dz^3}(e^z) = e^z$$

**Pattern**: All derivatives equal $e^z$.

$$\frac{d^n}{dz^n}(e^z) = e^z$$ for all $n \geq 0$

**Answer:** $\frac{d^n}{dz^n}(e^z) = e^z$ for all $n$

## Practice Problems

B) Differentiable only at origin

**Problem 2:** If $f(z) = z^3 + iz$, then $f'(i)$ equals:

A) $3i^2 + i = -3 + i$
B) $3i^2 - i = -3 - i$
C) $-3 + i$
D) $-2$
E) $i$

**Problem 3:** The derivative of $f(z) = e^{3z}$ is:

A) $e^{3z}$
B) $3e^{3z}$
C) $3e^z$
D) $e^{3z}/3$
E) $3z e^{3z-1}$

**Problem 4:** Which function is entire?

A) $\frac{1}{z-1}$
B) $\log z$
C) $\overline{z}$
D) $\sin z$
E) $\sqrt{z}$

**Problem 5:** If $f$ is differentiable at $z_0$ with $f'(z_0) = 2e^{i\pi/4}$, the function locally:

A) Scales by 2 and rotates by $45°$
B) Scales by $\sqrt{2}$ and rotates by $45°$
C) Scales by 2 and rotates by $90°$
D) Only rotates by $45°$
E) Only scales by 2

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: D**
$f(z) = \text{Re}(z) = x$ (for $z = x + iy$)
Test difference quotient: $\frac{f(z+h)-f(z)}{h} = \frac{\text{Re}(h)}{h}$
Real approach ($h=t$): $\frac{t}{t} = 1$
Imaginary approach ($h=it$): $\frac{0}{it} = 0$
Limits differ → not differentiable anywhere
This function only extracts real part; no complex structure preserved

**Problem 2: A**
$f(z) = z^3 + iz$
$f'(z) = 3z^2 + i$
$f'(i) = 3i^2 + i = 3(-1) + i = -3 + i$
Polynomial differentiation: power rule applies
Note: $i^2 = -1$ always

**Problem 3: B**
$f(z) = e^{3z}$
Chain rule: $f'(z) = e^{3z} \cdot (3z)' = e^{3z} \cdot 3 = 3e^{3z}$
Or recognize: $\frac{d}{dz}(e^{az}) = ae^{az}$
Exponential function most important in complex analysis
Compare: $\frac{d}{dx}(e^{3x}) = 3e^{3x}$ in real calculus

**Problem 4: D**
$\sin z$ is entire (differentiable everywhere on $\mathbb{C}$)
Defined via $\sin z = \frac{e^{iz} - e^{-iz}}{2i}$ (exponentials are entire)
(A): singularity at $z=1$
(B): branch point at $z=0$, multivalued
(C): $\overline{z}$ not differentiable anywhere
(E): $\sqrt{z}$ has branch point at origin

**Problem 5: A**
$f'(z_0) = 2e^{i\pi/4}$ in polar form
Modulus: $|f'(z_0)| = 2$ (scaling factor)
Argument: $\arg(f'(z_0)) = \pi/4 = 45°$ (rotation angle)
Locally: $f(z_0 + h) \approx f(z_0) + 2e^{i\pi/4}h$
Multiplication by $2e^{i\pi/4}$ = scale by 2, rotate by $45°$
This is conformal mapping property
