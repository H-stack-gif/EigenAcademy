# Complex Analysis Topic 3.2: Cauchy's Theorem

In vector calculus, the fundamental theorem for line integrals states that if $\vec{F}$ is conservative (has a potential), then $\oint_C \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} = 0$ around any closed loop. Cauchy's theorem is the complex analogue—and far more powerful. It states that if $f$ is analytic in a simply connected domain $D$, then $\oint_C f(z) \, dz = 0$ for any closed contour $C$ in $D$. This simple statement has extraordinary consequences: it means analytic functions have antiderivatives, integrals are path-independent, and the value of an integral depends only on what singularities lie inside the contour.

The theorem's power stems from its topological character. "Simply connected" means the domain has no holes—you can shrink any closed loop to a point without leaving the domain. In such regions, analytic functions behave perfectly: all closed loop integrals vanish. When domains have holes (punctured planes, annuli), integrals can be non-zero, but only when contours encircle the holes. This topological sensitivity makes complex integration fundamentally different from real integration, where such phenomena don't occur.

This article develops Cauchy's theorem and its applications. We'll state the theorem precisely, understand the simply connected requirement, use it to evaluate integrals by deforming contours, discover path independence for analytic functions, and learn when and why the theorem fails. You'll master contour deformation techniques, understand the relationship between topology and integration, and prepare for the Cauchy integral formula that follows.

## Statement of Cauchy's Theorem

**Cauchy's Theorem**: Let $f$ be analytic in a simply connected domain $D$. Then for any closed contour $C$ in $D$:

$$\oint_C f(z) \, dz = 0$$

**Simply connected**: A domain with no "holes"—every closed curve can be continuously shrunk to a point.

**Examples**:
- Simply connected: disk, half-plane, entire plane, interior of rectangle
- Not simply connected: punctured disk, annulus, exterior of circle

### Example 1: Applying Cauchy's Theorem

Evaluate $\oint_C e^z \, dz$ where $C$ is any closed contour.

**Solution:**

$e^z$ is entire (analytic everywhere on $\mathbb{C}$).

$\mathbb{C}$ is simply connected.

By Cauchy's theorem:
$$\oint_C e^z \, dz = 0$$

**Answer:** $0$ for any closed contour

### Example 2: Polynomial Integral

Evaluate $\oint_C (z^3 + 2z - 5) \, dz$ where $C$ is the circle $|z| = 3$.

**Solution:**

$p(z) = z^3 + 2z - 5$ is a polynomial, hence entire (analytic everywhere).

By Cauchy's theorem:
$$\oint_C p(z) \, dz = 0$$

**Answer:** $0$

### Example 3: When Theorem Doesn't Apply

Evaluate $\oint_{|z|=1} \frac{1}{z} \, dz$.

**Solution:**

$f(z) = \frac{1}{z}$ is analytic in $\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$ (not at origin).

The domain $\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$ is **not simply connected** (punctured plane has a hole).

Cauchy's theorem does **not** apply.

Direct calculation (from previous article):
$$\oint_{|z|=1} \frac{dz}{z} = 2\pi i \neq 0$$

**Answer:** $2\pi i$ (Cauchy's theorem doesn't apply; $f$ not analytic at $z=0$ inside contour)

### Example 4: Disk Domain

Evaluate $\oint_C \sin z \, dz$ where $C$ is any closed curve in $|z| < 5$.

**Solution:**

$\sin z$ is entire (analytic everywhere).

The disk $|z| < 5$ is simply connected.

By Cauchy's theorem:
$$\oint_C \sin z \, dz = 0$$

**Answer:** $0$

## Cauchy's Theorem for Multiple Contours

**Extended version**: If $f$ is analytic in region between two closed contours $C_1$ (outer) and $C_2$ (inner, traversed opposite to $C_1$):

$$\oint_{C_1} f(z) \, dz = \oint_{C_2} f(z) \, dz$$

**Interpretation**: Can deform outer contour to inner contour without changing integral value.

### Example 5: Contour Deformation

Let $f(z) = \frac{1}{z-2}$. Show that $\oint_{|z|=3} f(z) \, dz = \oint_{|z-2|=1} f(z) \, dz$.

**Solution:**

$f$ has singularity only at $z = 2$.

$f$ is analytic in the annulus between $|z| = 3$ and $|z-2| = 1$ (both contours enclose $z=2$, but $f$ analytic in region between them).

By Cauchy's theorem for multiply connected domains:
$$\oint_{|z|=3} \frac{dz}{z-2} = \oint_{|z-2|=1} \frac{dz}{z-2}$$

The right integral: standard form $\oint \frac{dz}{z-z_0} = 2\pi i$

$$\oint_{|z|=3} \frac{dz}{z-2} = 2\pi i$$

**Answer:** Both integrals equal $2\pi i$; contours are equivalent (enclose same singularity)

### Example 6: Multiple Holes

Evaluate $\oint_{|z|=3} \frac{dz}{z^2-1}$ by contour deformation.

**Solution:**

$$f(z) = \frac{1}{z^2-1} = \frac{1}{(z-1)(z+1)}$$

Singularities at $z = 1$ and $z = -1$ (both inside $|z| = 3$).

Deform outer contour $|z|=3$ to two small circles around each singularity:
$$\oint_{|z|=3} f = \oint_{|z-1|=\epsilon} f + \oint_{|z+1|=\epsilon} f$$

Use partial fractions:
$$\frac{1}{z^2-1} = \frac{1/2}{z-1} - \frac{1/2}{z+1}$$

$$\oint_{|z-1|=\epsilon} f = \frac{1}{2}\oint \frac{dz}{z-1} = \frac{1}{2}(2\pi i) = \pi i$$

$$\oint_{|z+1|=\epsilon} f = -\frac{1}{2}\oint \frac{dz}{z+1} = -\frac{1}{2}(2\pi i) = -\pi i$$

Total: $\pi i + (-\pi i) = 0$

**Answer:** $0$

### Example 7: Annular Region

If $f$ is analytic in $1 < |z| < 3$, relate integrals on $|z| = 2$ and $|z| = 2.5$.

**Solution:**

Both circles $|z|=2$ and $|z|=2.5$ lie in the annulus where $f$ is analytic.

Since $f$ is analytic in region between them (no singularities):
$$\oint_{|z|=2.5} f(z) \, dz = \oint_{|z|=2} f(z) \, dz$$

**Answer:** Integrals are equal (contour deformation in region where $f$ analytic)

## Path Independence for Analytic Functions

**Corollary of Cauchy's Theorem**: If $f$ is analytic in simply connected domain $D$, then $\int_C f(z) \, dz$ is path-independent for any $C$ in $D$ with fixed endpoints.

**Proof**: Take two paths $C_1$ and $C_2$ from $z_1$ to $z_2$. Form closed contour $C = C_1 - C_2$. By Cauchy: $\oint_C f = 0$, so $\int_{C_1} f = \int_{C_2} f$.

### Example 8: Path Independence Application

Evaluate $\int_C \frac{e^z}{z-3} \, dz$ from $z = 0$ to $z = 1$ along any path not passing through $z = 3$.

**Solution:**

$f(z) = \frac{e^z}{z-3}$ is analytic except at $z = 3$.

Since $z = 3$ is not between $0$ and $1$ (we can choose paths avoiding it), the integral is path-independent in region not containing $z=3$.

However, $f$ doesn't have a simple closed-form antiderivative, so we need other methods.

For this specific case, the integral value depends on the actual path chosen that avoids $z=3$, but all such paths give the same value.

**Answer:** Path-independent for paths avoiding $z=3$; value requires further calculation

### Example 9: Constructing Antiderivative

Use Cauchy's theorem to show that if $f$ is analytic in simply connected $D$, then $f$ has an antiderivative.

**Solution:**

Fix $z_0 \in D$. Define:
$$F(z) = \int_{z_0}^z f(\zeta) \, d\zeta$$

where integral is along any path in $D$ (well-defined by path independence).

To show $F'(z) = f(z)$:
$$F(z + h) - F(z) = \int_z^{z+h} f(\zeta) \, d\zeta$$

For small $h$, approximate $f(\zeta) \approx f(z)$ on path from $z$ to $z+h$:
$$\int_z^{z+h} f(\zeta) \, d\zeta \approx f(z) \cdot h$$

Therefore:
$$\frac{F(z+h) - F(z)}{h} \approx f(z)$$

Taking limit: $F'(z) = f(z)$ ✓

**Answer:** Path independence from Cauchy implies existence of antiderivative

### Example 10: Using Antiderivative

Since $e^z$ is entire, find $\int_0^{1+i} e^z \, dz$.

**Solution:**

$F(z) = e^z$ is an antiderivative of $f(z) = e^z$.

$$\int_0^{1+i} e^z \, dz = [e^z]_0^{1+i} = e^{1+i} - e^0 = e^{1+i} - 1$$

$$e^{1+i} = e \cdot e^i = e(\cos 1 + i\sin 1)$$

$$\int_0^{1+i} e^z \, dz = e\cos 1 - 1 + ie\sin 1$$

**Answer:** $e\cos 1 - 1 + ie\sin 1$

## Cauchy-Goursat Theorem

**Cauchy-Goursat Theorem**: If $f$ is analytic in simply connected domain $D$, then $\oint_C f(z) \, dz = 0$ for any closed contour $C$ in $D$.

This is Cauchy's theorem without requiring $f'$ to be continuous (Goursat's refinement shows analyticity alone suffices).

### Example 11: Subtle Application

Does $f(z) = |z|^2$ satisfy conditions for Cauchy's theorem?

**Solution:**

$f(z) = |z|^2 = z\overline{z}$ involves $\overline{z}$.

We've shown $\overline{z}$ is not analytic anywhere (fails Cauchy-Riemann).

Therefore $|z|^2$ is not analytic (except at $z=0$ as isolated point).

Cauchy's theorem does **not** apply.

Indeed: $\oint_{|z|=1} |z|^2 \, dz = \oint_{|z|=1} 1 \cdot dz \neq 0$ in general.

**Answer:** No; $|z|^2$ not analytic, so Cauchy's theorem doesn't apply

### Example 12: Checking Simply Connected

Is the domain $D = \{z: 1 < |z| < 2\}$ simply connected?

**Solution:**

$D$ is an annulus (ring).

A closed circle $|z| = 1.5$ in $D$ cannot be shrunk to a point without leaving $D$ (would have to pass through $|z| \leq 1$ or $|z| \geq 2$).

**Not simply connected**.

**Consequence**: Even if $f$ analytic in $D$, $\oint_C f$ may not be zero.

**Answer:** No; annulus is not simply connected (has hole)

### Example 13: Rectangle Domain

Is a rectangle (including interior) simply connected?

**Solution:**

Yes. Any closed curve in the rectangle can be continuously shrunk to a point without leaving the rectangle.

**No holes** inside.

If $f$ is analytic in and on rectangle, $\oint_C f(z) \, dz = 0$ for any closed $C$ inside.

**Answer:** Yes; rectangle is simply connected

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
Cauchy's theorem: if $f$ analytic in simply connected $D$, then $\oint_C f = 0$
Both conditions needed: analytic (differentiable in region) + simply connected (no holes)
Continuity not enough (example: $\overline{z}$ continuous but not analytic)
Single point differentiability not enough (needs region)
Simply connected crucial: $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$ has $\oint \frac{dz}{z} = 2\pi i \neq 0$

**Problem 2: C**
$f(z) = z^2 + 3z$ is polynomial → entire (analytic everywhere)
$\mathbb{C}$ is simply connected
By Cauchy's theorem: $\oint f = 0$ for any closed contour
Alternative: $F(z) = z^3/3 + 3z^2/2$ is antiderivative
$\oint f = F(z_0) - F(z_0) = 0$ (closed loop returns to start)

**Problem 3: B**
$\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$ = complex plane with origin removed (punctured plane)
Has a "hole" at origin
Closed circle around origin cannot shrink to point without passing through $z=0$
Not simply connected
Consequence: $\oint_{|z|=1} \frac{dz}{z} = 2\pi i \neq 0$ even though $1/z$ analytic in domain

**Problem 4: D**
$f$ has singularity at $z=1$, which is inside $|z|=3$
$f$ not analytic in interior of $|z|=3$
Cauchy's theorem doesn't apply (requires analyticity throughout interior)
Cannot determine value without more information about nature of singularity
Compare: if singularity outside contour, Cauchy would give $0$

**Problem 5: B**
Cauchy's theorem + path independence → antiderivatives exist
Proof: $F(z) = \int_{z_0}^z f(\zeta) d\zeta$ is well-defined (path-independent)
Can show $F'(z) = f(z)$
Fundamental result: analyticity in simply connected domain → has antiderivative
Not true that analytic functions have no zeros (e.g., $z$ has zero at origin)
