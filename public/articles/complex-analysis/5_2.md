# Complex Analysis Topic 5.2: Residue Theorem

Cauchy's integral formula tells us that $\oint_C \frac{f(z)}{z-z_0} dz = 2\pi i f(z_0)$ when $f$ is analytic. But what if $f$ itself has singularities? The residue theorem generalizes Cauchy's formula to functions with isolated singularities: the integral around a closed contour equals $2\pi i$ times the sum of residues at all singularities inside. This single theorem transforms contour integration from geometric path analysis into algebraic residue computation. Instead of parametrizing curves and computing limits, you identify singularities, calculate residues (often with simple formulas), sum them, and multiply by $2\pi i$.

The residue theorem's power lies in its generality and simplicity. It works for any closed contour and any function with isolated singularities—poles, removable singularities, even essential singularities. The topology of the contour matters only insofar as it determines which singularities lie inside. Deforming the contour without crossing singularities doesn't change the integral. This topological character makes the residue theorem far more than a computational trick—it's a profound statement about how local singular behavior (encoded in residues) determines global integral behavior.

This article develops the residue theorem and its applications to contour integration. We'll state and understand the theorem, apply it to evaluate integrals with various singularities, use contour deformation to simplify computations, handle multiply connected domains, and understand the theorem's topological content. You'll master the art of contour integration via residues, transforming difficult integrals into straightforward calculations and preparing for applications to real integrals.

## Statement of the Residue Theorem

**Residue Theorem**: Let $f$ be analytic in a domain except for isolated singularities at $z_1, z_2, \ldots, z_n$. If $C$ is a simple closed contour (traversed counterclockwise) containing these singularities in its interior:

$$\oint_C f(z) \, dz = 2\pi i \sum_{k=1}^{n} \text{Res}(f, z_k)$$

**Key insight**: The integral depends only on residues at singularities inside $C$, not on the path itself.

### Example 1: Single Residue

Evaluate $\oint_{|z|=2} \frac{e^z}{z} \, dz$.

**Solution:**

Singularity at $z = 0$ (inside $|z| = 2$).

Laurent series: $\frac{e^z}{z} = \frac{1}{z}(1 + z + \frac{z^2}{2!} + \cdots) = \frac{1}{z} + 1 + \frac{z}{2!} + \cdots$

$$\text{Res}\left(\frac{e^z}{z}, 0\right) = 1$$

By residue theorem:
$$\oint_{|z|=2} \frac{e^z}{z} dz = 2\pi i \cdot 1 = 2\pi i$$

**Answer:** $2\pi i$

### Example 2: Two Residues

Evaluate $\oint_{|z|=3} \frac{z}{(z-1)(z-2)} \, dz$.

**Solution:**

Singularities at $z = 1$ and $z = 2$ (both inside $|z| = 3$).

**At $z = 1$** (simple pole):
$$\text{Res}(f, 1) = \lim_{z \to 1}(z-1)\frac{z}{(z-1)(z-2)} = \lim_{z \to 1}\frac{z}{z-2} = \frac{1}{-1} = -1$$

**At $z = 2$** (simple pole):
$$\text{Res}(f, 2) = \lim_{z \to 2}(z-2)\frac{z}{(z-1)(z-2)} = \lim_{z \to 2}\frac{z}{z-1} = \frac{2}{1} = 2$$

$$\oint_{|z|=3} f dz = 2\pi i(-1 + 2) = 2\pi i$$

**Answer:** $2\pi i$

### Example 3: Singularity Outside Contour

Evaluate $\oint_{|z|=1} \frac{dz}{z-3}$.

**Solution:**

Singularity at $z = 3$ (outside $|z| = 1$ since $|3| = 3 > 1$).

Function $\frac{1}{z-3}$ is analytic inside and on $|z| = 1$.

By Cauchy's theorem:
$$\oint_{|z|=1} \frac{dz}{z-3} = 0$$

**Answer:** $0$ (no singularities inside contour)

### Example 4: Verifying with Direct Computation

Verify Example 1 by parametrizing the contour.

**Solution:**

$C$: $z = 2e^{it}$, $dz = 2ie^{it} dt$ for $0 \leq t \leq 2\pi$

$$\oint_C \frac{e^z}{z} dz = \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{e^{2e^{it}}}{2e^{it}} \cdot 2ie^{it} dt = \int_0^{2\pi} ie^{2e^{it}} dt$$

This is difficult to evaluate directly!

Residue theorem gives answer immediately: $2\pi i$ ✓

**Answer:** Residue method much simpler than direct parametrization

## Multiple Singularities

### Example 5: Three Simple Poles

Evaluate $\oint_{|z|=5} \frac{dz}{z(z-1)(z-2)}$.

**Solution:**

Singularities at $z = 0, 1, 2$ (all inside $|z| = 5$).

Partial fractions:
$$\frac{1}{z(z-1)(z-2)} = \frac{A}{z} + \frac{B}{z-1} + \frac{C}{z-2}$$

$$1 = A(z-1)(z-2) + Bz(z-2) + Cz(z-1)$$

At $z=0$: $1 = A(2) \implies A = 1/2$
At $z=1$: $1 = B(-1) \implies B = -1$
At $z=2$: $1 = C(2) \implies C = 1/2$

$$\text{Res}(f, 0) = \frac{1}{2}, \quad \text{Res}(f, 1) = -1, \quad \text{Res}(f, 2) = \frac{1}{2}$$

$$\oint f = 2\pi i\left(\frac{1}{2} - 1 + \frac{1}{2}\right) = 0$$

**Answer:** $0$

### Example 6: Mixed Order Poles

Evaluate $\oint_{|z|=2} \frac{z+1}{z^2(z-1)} \, dz$.

**Solution:**

Singularities: double pole at $z=0$, simple pole at $z=1$ (both inside $|z|=2$).

**At $z = 0$** (order 2):
$$\text{Res}(f, 0) = \lim_{z \to 0}\frac{d}{dz}\left[z^2 \cdot \frac{z+1}{z^2(z-1)}\right]$$
$$= \lim_{z \to 0}\frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac{z+1}{z-1}\right) = \lim_{z \to 0}\frac{(z-1) - (z+1)}{(z-1)^2}$$
$$= \lim_{z \to 0}\frac{-2}{(z-1)^2} = -2$$

**At $z = 1$** (order 1):
$$\text{Res}(f, 1) = \lim_{z \to 1}\frac{z+1}{z^2} = \frac{2}{1} = 2$$

$$\oint f = 2\pi i(-2 + 2) = 0$$

**Answer:** $0$

### Example 7: Essential Singularity

Evaluate $\oint_{|z|=1} e^{1/z} \, dz$.

**Solution:**

Essential singularity at $z = 0$ (inside $|z| = 1$).

Laurent series:
$$e^{1/z} = 1 + \frac{1}{z} + \frac{1}{2!z^2} + \frac{1}{3!z^3} + \cdots$$

Coefficient of $\frac{1}{z}$: $\text{Res}(e^{1/z}, 0) = 1$

$$\oint_{|z|=1} e^{1/z} dz = 2\pi i \cdot 1 = 2\pi i$$

**Answer:** $2\pi i$

## Contour Deformation

If $f$ is analytic in the region between two contours $C_1$ and $C_2$, then:
$$\oint_{C_1} f = \oint_{C_2} f$$

(Both traversed counterclockwise)

### Example 8: Deforming to Smaller Contour

Evaluate $\oint_{|z|=10} \frac{dz}{z^2+1}$ by deforming to smaller contours.

**Solution:**

Singularities at $z = \pm i$ (both inside $|z| = 10$).

Deform large circle to two small circles around $i$ and $-i$:
$$\oint_{|z|=10} f = \oint_{|z-i|=\epsilon} f + \oint_{|z+i|=\epsilon} f$$

$$\text{Res}(f, i) = \lim_{z \to i}(z-i)\frac{1}{(z-i)(z+i)} = \frac{1}{2i}$$

$$\text{Res}(f, -i) = \lim_{z \to -i}(z+i)\frac{1}{(z-i)(z+i)} = \frac{1}{-2i}$$

$$\oint f = 2\pi i\left(\frac{1}{2i} + \frac{1}{-2i}\right) = 2\pi i \cdot 0 = 0$$

**Answer:** $0$

### Example 9: Avoiding Singularities

Evaluate $\oint_{|z|=1} \frac{dz}{z(z-2)}$.

**Solution:**

Singularities at $z = 0$ (inside $|z|=1$) and $z = 2$ (outside).

Only $z=0$ contributes:

$$\text{Res}(f, 0) = \lim_{z \to 0}z \cdot \frac{1}{z(z-2)} = \frac{1}{-2}$$

$$\oint_{|z|=1} f = 2\pi i \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) = -\pi i$$

**Answer:** $-\pi i$

### Example 10: Different Contours, Same Result

Show $\oint_{|z|=3} \frac{dz}{z-1} = \oint_{|z-1|=1/2} \frac{dz}{z-1}$.

**Solution:**

Both contours enclose only $z = 1$.

Function analytic in region between contours.

Both integrals equal $2\pi i \cdot \text{Res}(1/(z-1), 1) = 2\pi i \cdot 1 = 2\pi i$.

**Answer:** Both equal $2\pi i$ (contour deformation)

## Indented Contours

When singularities lie on the intended contour, use **indentations** (small semicircles) to avoid them.

### Example 11: Singularity on Real Axis

For $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{dx}{x(x^2+1)}$, how do we handle singularity at $x=0$?

**Solution:**

Use contour: real axis with small semicircle of radius $\epsilon$ around origin (upper half).

As $\epsilon \to 0$, the semicircle contribution must be analyzed separately.

(Full evaluation requires techniques from next article)

**Answer:** Indent contour around singularity on real axis

### Example 12: Residue at Infinity

For $f(z) = \frac{1}{z^2}$, the residue at $\infty$ is defined as:

$$\text{Res}(f, \infty) = -\text{Res}\left(\frac{1}{z^2}f\left(\frac{1}{z}\right), 0\right)$$

Find it.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{1}{z^2}f\left(\frac{1}{z}\right) = \frac{1}{z^2} \cdot \frac{1}{(1/z)^2} = \frac{1}{z^2} \cdot z^2 = 1$$

This has no singularity at $z=0$ (constant function).

$$\text{Res}(1, 0) = 0$$

$$\text{Res}(f, \infty) = -0 = 0$$

**Answer:** $\text{Res}(1/z^2, \infty) = 0$

### Example 13: Sum of All Residues

For rational function $f(z) = \frac{P(z)}{Q(z)}$ where $\deg Q \geq \deg P + 2$, show sum of all residues (including at $\infty$) equals zero.

**Solution:**

Consider $\oint_C f$ where $C$ is very large circle of radius $R \to \infty$.

For large $|z|$: $|f(z)| \leq \frac{M}{|z|^2}$

$$\left|\oint_C f\right| \leq \frac{M}{R^2} \cdot 2\pi R = \frac{2\pi M}{R} \to 0$$

But also by residue theorem:
$$\oint_C f = 2\pi i \sum_{\text{all finite}} \text{Res}(f, z_k)$$

Therefore: $\sum \text{Res}(f, z_k) = 0$

This equals negative of residue at $\infty$.

**Answer:** Sum of all residues (including $\infty$) is zero for suitable rational functions

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Residue theorem: $\oint_C f(z) dz = 2\pi i \sum_{k} \text{Res}(f, z_k)$
Sum over all singularities inside $C$
Must multiply by $2\pi i$ (not just sum residues)
Works for any simple closed contour
Fundamental theorem of complex integration

**Problem 2: C**
If all singularities outside, $f$ analytic inside and on $C$
Cauchy's theorem: $\oint_C f = 0$
No residues to contribute
Example: $\oint_{|z|=1} \frac{1}{z-5} = 0$ (singularity at $z=5$ outside)
Residue theorem reduces to Cauchy's theorem

**Problem 3: C**
Works for any isolated singularity: removable, pole, essential
Simple poles: use $\lim(z-z_0)f(z)$
Higher order poles: differentiation formula
Essential: extract Laurent coefficient
Must be isolated (not branch points or natural boundaries)

**Problem 4: B**
Both singularities at $z=1$ and $z=2$ inside $|z|=3$
$\oint f = 2\pi i(2 + (-1)) = 2\pi i \cdot 1 = 2\pi i$
Sum residues, then multiply by $2\pi i$
Note: if one residue outside, don't include it
Orientation matters: counterclockwise is positive

**Problem 5: B**
Contour deformation principle
If $f$ analytic in region between two contours, integrals equal
Topology determines integral (which singularities enclosed)
Can shrink contour to small circles around singularities
Powerful technique for simplifying calculations
