# Complex Analysis Topic 5.3: Evaluation of Real Integrals

The residue theorem seems purely complex—it evaluates contour integrals $\oint_C f(z) \, dz$ in the complex plane. Yet it has remarkable power to evaluate **real integrals** that resist standard calculus techniques. The strategy is indirect but elegant: extend a real integral to a complex contour integral, apply the residue theorem, and show that the extra pieces vanish, leaving only the desired real integral. This method handles three major classes: definite integrals of trigonometric rational functions, improper integrals over the entire real line, and Fourier-type integrals with exponential factors.

For trigonometric integrals $\int_0^{2\pi} R(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) \, d\theta$, the substitution $z = e^{i\theta}$ transforms the integral to $\oint_{|z|=1}$ of a rational function—purely algebraic. For improper integrals $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x) \, dx$, close the contour with a semicircle in the upper or lower half-plane and show the semicircular arc contributes zero (Jordan's lemma). For Fourier integrals with $e^{iax}$, similar semicircular contours work, exploiting exponential decay in the imaginary direction.

This article develops techniques for evaluating real integrals using complex methods. We'll master the trigonometric substitution, learn when and how to close contours with semicircles, apply Jordan's lemma to handle exponential factors, and understand when each technique applies. You'll transform difficult real calculus problems into straightforward residue calculations, showcasing the unexpected power of complex analysis.

## Type 1: Trigonometric Integrals

For $\int_0^{2\pi} R(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) \, d\theta$ where $R$ is rational:

**Method**:
1. Substitute $z = e^{i\theta}$
2. $\cos\theta = \frac{z + z^{-1}}{2}$, $\sin\theta = \frac{z - z^{-1}}{2i}$
3. $d\theta = \frac{dz}{iz}$
4. Integral becomes $\oint_{|z|=1}$ of rational function
5. Evaluate using residues

### Example 1: Basic Trigonometric Integral

Evaluate $\int_0^{2\pi} \frac{d\theta}{2 + \cos\theta}$.

**Solution:**

Let $z = e^{i\theta}$:
$$\cos\theta = \frac{z + z^{-1}}{2}, \quad d\theta = \frac{dz}{iz}$$

$$\int_0^{2\pi} \frac{d\theta}{2 + \cos\theta} = \oint_{|z|=1} \frac{1}{2 + (z+z^{-1})/2} \cdot \frac{dz}{iz}$$

$$= \oint_{|z|=1} \frac{1}{(4 + z + z^{-1})/2} \cdot \frac{dz}{iz} = \oint_{|z|=1} \frac{2}{4 + z + z^{-1}} \cdot \frac{dz}{iz}$$

$$= \oint_{|z|=1} \frac{2}{iz(4 + z + z^{-1})} \, dz = \oint_{|z|=1} \frac{2}{i(4z + z^2 + 1)} \, dz$$

$$= \oint_{|z|=1} \frac{2}{i(z^2 + 4z + 1)} \, dz$$

Find poles: $z^2 + 4z + 1 = 0$

$$z = \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{16-4}}{2} = \frac{-4 \pm 2\sqrt{3}}{2} = -2 \pm \sqrt{3}$$

$z_1 = -2 + \sqrt{3} \approx -0.27$ (inside $|z|=1$)
$z_2 = -2 - \sqrt{3} \approx -3.73$ (outside $|z|=1$)

At $z_1$: Using residue formula for $\frac{g(z)}{h(z)}$:
$$\text{Res}\left(\frac{2/i}{z^2+4z+1}, z_1\right) = \frac{2/i}{2z_1+4} = \frac{2/i}{2(-2+\sqrt{3})+4} = \frac{2/i}{2\sqrt{3}} = \frac{1}{i\sqrt{3}}$$

$$\int_0^{2\pi} = 2\pi i \cdot \frac{1}{i\sqrt{3}} = \frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{3}}$$

**Answer:** $\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{2\pi\sqrt{3}}{3}$

### Example 2: Sine and Cosine Together

Evaluate $\int_0^{2\pi} \frac{\cos\theta}{5 + 4\sin\theta} \, d\theta$.

**Solution:**

Let $z = e^{i\theta}$:
$$\cos\theta = \frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}, \quad \sin\theta = \frac{z-z^{-1}}{2i}, \quad d\theta = \frac{dz}{iz}$$

$$\int = \oint_{|z|=1} \frac{(z+z^{-1})/2}{5 + 4(z-z^{-1})/(2i)} \cdot \frac{dz}{iz}$$

Simplify denominator:
$$5 + \frac{4(z-z^{-1})}{2i} = 5 + \frac{2(z-z^{-1})}{i} = 5 - 2i(z-z^{-1}) = 5 - 2iz + 2iz^{-1}$$

This becomes complex. The integral equals **zero** by symmetry (can verify with residues).

**Answer:** $0$

## Type 2: Improper Integrals

For $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x) \, dx$ where $f$ is rational:

**Method**:
1. Extend to complex: $\int_{-R}^{R} f(x) \, dx$
2. Close contour with semicircle $C_R$ in upper half-plane
3. Apply residue theorem to closed contour
4. Show $\int_{C_R} f \to 0$ as $R \to \infty$
5. Integral equals $2\pi i \sum \text{residues in upper half-plane}$

**Condition**: Need $|f(z)| \to 0$ fast enough as $|z| \to \infty$

### Example 4: Basic Improper Integral

Evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{dx}{1+x^2}$.

**Solution:**

Consider $f(z) = \frac{1}{1+z^2}$. Poles at $z = \pm i$.

Close with semicircle in upper half-plane (contains $z=i$).

$$\text{Res}\left(\frac{1}{1+z^2}, i\right) = \lim_{z \to i}(z-i)\frac{1}{(z-i)(z+i)} = \frac{1}{2i}$$

On semicircle $C_R$: $|z| = R$, so $|z^2| \approx R^2$, giving $|f| \leq \frac{1}{R^2-1}$

Arc length $\pi R$, so $|\int_{C_R} f| \leq \frac{\pi R}{R^2-1} \to 0$ as $R \to \infty$

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{dx}{1+x^2} = 2\pi i \cdot \frac{1}{2i} = \pi$$

**Answer:** $\pi$

### Example 5: Higher Degree Denominator

Evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{x^2}{(x^2+1)(x^2+4)} \, dx$.

**Solution:**

Poles at $z = \pm i$ and $z = \pm 2i$. Upper half-plane: $i$ and $2i$.

**At $z = i$**:
$$\text{Res}(f, i) = \lim_{z \to i}(z-i)\frac{z^2}{(z-i)(z+i)(z^2+4)} = \frac{-1}{2i(3)} = \frac{-1}{6i}$$

**At $z = 2i$**:
$$\text{Res}(f, 2i) = \lim_{z \to 2i}(z-2i)\frac{z^2}{(z^2+1)(z-2i)(z+2i)} = \frac{-4}{(-3)(4i)} = \frac{1}{3i}$$

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} = 2\pi i\left(\frac{-1}{6i} + \frac{1}{3i}\right) = 2\pi i \cdot \frac{1}{6i} = \frac{\pi}{3}$$

**Answer:** $\frac{\pi}{3}$

### Example 6: When Semicircle Doesn't Vanish

Why can't we evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{x^3}{1+x^2} \, dx$ this way?

**Solution:**

On semicircle: $|f(z)| \approx \frac{|z|^3}{|z|^2} = |z| = R$

$$\left|\int_{C_R} f\right| \leq R \cdot \pi R = \pi R^2 \to \infty$$

Semicircle contribution doesn't vanish!

Actually, the integral doesn't converge (odd function, but not fast enough decay).

**Answer:** Fails because $f$ doesn't decay fast enough at infinity

## Type 3: Fourier Integrals

For $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)e^{iax} \, dx$ with $a > 0$:

**Method**: Same as Type 2, but exponential factor helps convergence on semicircle (Jordan's lemma).

**Jordan's Lemma**: If $|f(z)| \to 0$ uniformly on semicircle and $a > 0$, then
$$\int_{C_R} f(z)e^{iaz} \, dz \to 0$$ as $R \to \infty$

### Example 7: Fourier Integral

Evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\cos x}{1+x^2} \, dx$.

**Solution:**

Write $\cos x = \text{Re}(e^{ix})$, so compute:
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{e^{ix}}{1+x^2} \, dx$$

and take real part.

Close in upper half-plane (pole at $z=i$):

$$\text{Res}\left(\frac{e^{iz}}{1+z^2}, i\right) = \frac{e^{i \cdot i}}{2i} = \frac{e^{-1}}{2i}$$

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{e^{ix}}{1+x^2} dx = 2\pi i \cdot \frac{e^{-1}}{2i} = \pi e^{-1}$$

Real part: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\cos x}{1+x^2} dx = \pi e^{-1} = \frac{\pi}{e}$

**Answer:** $\frac{\pi}{e}$

### Example 8: Sine Integral

Evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{x\sin x}{x^2+4} \, dx$.

**Solution:**

$$\sin x = \text{Im}(e^{ix})$$

Compute $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{xe^{ix}}{x^2+4} dx$ and take imaginary part.

Poles at $z = \pm 2i$; upper half-plane: $2i$

$$\text{Res}(f, 2i) = \lim_{z \to 2i}\frac{ze^{iz}}{z+2i} = \frac{2ie^{-2}}{4i} = \frac{e^{-2}}{2}$$

$$\int = 2\pi i \cdot \frac{e^{-2}}{2} = \pi ie^{-2}$$

Imaginary part: $\pi e^{-2}$

**Answer:** $\pi e^{-2} = \frac{\pi}{e^2}$

### Example 9: Choosing Half-Plane

For $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)e^{-iax} dx$ with $a > 0$, which half-plane?

**Solution:**

$e^{-iax} = e^{-ia(x+iy)} = e^{-iax + ay} = e^{ay}e^{-iax}$

For $y > 0$ (upper half-plane): $e^{ay} \to \infty$ (bad)

For $y < 0$ (lower half-plane): $e^{ay} = e^{-a|y|} \to 0$ (good)

Use **lower** half-plane when exponent has $-iax$ with $a > 0$.

**Answer:** Lower half-plane for $e^{-iax}$ ($a > 0$)

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
Substitution $z = e^{i\theta}$ for trigonometric integrals
Converts $\cos\theta = (z+1/z)/2$, $\sin\theta = (z-1/z)/(2i)$
$d\theta = dz/(iz)$
Transforms $\int_0^{2\pi}$ to $\oint_{|z|=1}$
Rational function on unit circle → residues

**Problem 2: B**
Upper half-plane contains pole at $z=i$
$\text{Res}(1/(z^2+1), i) = 1/(2i)$
Semicircle vanishes as $R \to \infty$
Integral $= 2\pi i \cdot (1/2i) = \pi$
Choose half-plane containing relevant poles

**Problem 3: B**
Need $f(z) \to 0$ as $|z| \to \infty$ on semicircle
More precisely: $|f| \leq M/|z|^k$ for $k \geq 1$
Then $\int_{C_R} f \leq M \cdot \pi R / R^k \to 0$
Example: $1/(1+z^2) \sim 1/z^2$ works
Example: $z/(1+z^2) \sim 1/z$ doesn't work well

**Problem 4: B**
Jordan's lemma: for $\int f(z)e^{iaz} dz$ with $a > 0$
Exponential $e^{iaz} = e^{-ay}e^{iax}$ decays in upper half-plane ($y > 0$)
Allows weaker decay condition on $f$
Essential for Fourier integrals
Use upper half-plane for $e^{iax}$, lower for $e^{-iax}$

**Problem 5: D**
Pole at $z = i$ (upper half-plane)
$\text{Res}(e^{iz}/(1+z^2), i) = e^{-1}/(2i)$
Integral $= 2\pi i \cdot e^{-1}/(2i) = \pi e^{-1} = \pi/e$
This is complex answer; real part gives $\int \cos x/(1+x^2)$
Imaginary part gives $\int \sin x/(1+x^2)$
