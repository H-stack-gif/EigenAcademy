# Partial Differential Equations Topic 1.3: Well-Posed Problems

Imagine trying to predict where a vibrating drumhead will be at $t = 1$ second, but you're only told its position now—not its velocity. Or solving for steady-state temperature in a room without knowing the wall temperatures. These problems are **ill-posed**: they lack sufficient information for a unique solution. Well-posedness is the mathematical requirement that a problem makes physical sense and has exactly one solution that depends continuously on the data.

The concept of well-posedness, formalized by Jacques Hadamard in the early 1900s, distinguishes meaningful physical problems from mathematical curiosities. A **well-posed problem** must satisfy three criteria: a solution exists, the solution is unique, and the solution depends continuously on the initial/boundary data (small changes in data produce small changes in solution). If any criterion fails, the problem is ill-posed and likely unphysical.

This article explores what makes PDE problems well-posed. We'll examine different types of boundary conditions (Dirichlet, Neumann, Robin), understand why certain PDEs require specific conditions, and see how well-posedness connects to physical reality. The right conditions transform an underdetermined PDE into a solvable problem with physical meaning.

## The Three Criteria for Well-Posedness

A problem is **well-posed** (in the sense of Hadamard) if:

1. **Existence**: A solution exists
2. **Uniqueness**: The solution is unique
3. **Stability**: The solution depends continuously on data

If any criterion fails, the problem is **ill-posed**.

### Example 1: Why Three Criteria?

Why isn't existence alone sufficient for a well-posed problem?

**Solution:**

**Existence without uniqueness**: Useless for prediction (multiple possible futures)

Example: $u_{xx} = 0$ with $u(0) = 0$ and $u(1) = 0$

Solutions: $u = 0$ AND $u = c\sin(n\pi x)$ for any $n$ (infinitely many!)

**Existence and uniqueness without stability**: Tiny measurement errors could produce completely wrong answers

Example: Backward heat equation (ill-posed)

**Answer:** Need all three for physically meaningful problems

### Example 2: Continuous Dependence

For heat equation $u_t = ku_{xx}$ with $u(x, 0) = f(x)$, what does "continuous dependence on data" mean?

**Solution:**

If initial data $f_1(x)$ and $f_2(x)$ are close (small $\|f_1 - f_2\|$), then solutions $u_1(x, t)$ and $u_2(x, t)$ remain close for all $t > 0$.

Mathematically: For any $\epsilon > 0$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that
$$\|f_1 - f_2\| < \delta \implies \|u_1(x, t) - u_2(x, t)\| < \epsilon$$

**Physical meaning**: Small measurement errors don't drastically change predictions.

**Answer:** Small changes in initial temperature produce small changes in future temperature

## Types of Boundary Conditions

Three main types of boundary conditions appear in PDEs:

**Dirichlet**: Specify value of $u$ on boundary
$$u|_{\partial \Omega} = g$$

**Neumann**: Specify normal derivative on boundary
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\bigg|_{\partial \Omega} = g$$

**Robin (Mixed)**: Linear combination of $u$ and $\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}$
$$\alpha u + \beta\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\bigg|_{\partial \Omega} = g$$

### Example 3: Physical Interpretation of Boundary Conditions

For heat equation in a rod, interpret each boundary condition type:

**Solution:**

**Dirichlet**: $u(0, t) = T_0$ — Fixed temperature at end (e.g., end in ice bath)

**Neumann**: $u_x(0, t) = 0$ — Insulated end (no heat flux: $q = -ku_x = 0$)

**Robin**: $u_x(L, t) + hu(L, t) = 0$ — Convective cooling (Newton's law: heat flux proportional to temperature difference)

**Answer:** Dirichlet = fixed value, Neumann = insulated, Robin = convection

### Example 4: Counting Conditions

For $u_t = ku_{xx}$ on $0 \leq x \leq L$, $t \geq 0$:

How many boundary conditions in space?
How many initial conditions in time?

**Solution:**

**Spatial**: Second-order in $x$ → need 2 boundary conditions (one at $x = 0$, one at $x = L$)

**Temporal**: First-order in $t$ → need 1 initial condition at $t = 0$

Total: 3 conditions

**Answer:** 2 boundary + 1 initial condition

### Example 5: Wave Equation Conditions

For $u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx}$ on $0 \leq x \leq L$:

**Solution:**

**Spatial**: Second-order in $x$ → 2 boundary conditions

**Temporal**: Second-order in $t$ → 2 initial conditions
- $u(x, 0) = f(x)$ (initial displacement)
- $u_t(x, 0) = g(x)$ (initial velocity)

Total: 4 conditions

**Answer:** 2 boundary + 2 initial conditions (position and velocity)

## Well-Posed Problems for Each PDE Type

### Example 6: Laplace's Equation (Elliptic)

**Dirichlet problem**: $\nabla^2 u = 0$ in $\Omega$, $u = g$ on $\partial\Omega$

Is this well-posed?

**Solution:**

**Existence**: Yes (proven by various methods: Perron, potential theory)

**Uniqueness**: Yes (maximum principle: if two solutions $u_1, u_2$ exist, then $w = u_1 - u_2$ satisfies $\nabla^2 w = 0$ with $w = 0$ on boundary, so $w = 0$ everywhere)

**Stability**: Yes (small changes in boundary data produce small changes in interior)

**Answer:** Well-posed ✓

### Example 7: Heat Equation (Parabolic)

**Initial-boundary value problem**:
$$u_t = ku_{xx}, \quad 0 < x < L, \quad t > 0$$
$$u(x, 0) = f(x)$$
$$u(0, t) = u(L, t) = 0$$

Is this well-posed?

**Solution:**

**Existence**: Yes (separation of variables, Fourier series)

**Uniqueness**: Yes (energy method: if two solutions exist, their difference satisfies problem with zero data, so difference is zero)

**Stability**: Yes (maximum principle; also Fourier series shows exponential decay)

**Answer:** Well-posed ✓

### Example 8: Backward Heat Equation (Ill-Posed)

Solve $u_t = -ku_{xx}$ (negative sign!) backward from $t = T$ to $t = 0$.

Why is this ill-posed?

**Solution:**

**Existence**: Solutions may not exist (require too much regularity)

**Stability**: FAILS catastrophically

Small high-frequency perturbations in final data $u(x, T)$ grow exponentially as we go backward:

If $u(x, T) = \epsilon\sin(nx/L)$, then 
$$u(x, 0) = \epsilon e^{kn^2\pi^2T/L^2}\sin(nx/L)$$

For large $n$ (high frequency), this is huge even for tiny $\epsilon$!

**Answer:** Ill-posed: unstable (exponential growth backward in time)

### Example 9: Wave Equation Initial Value Problem

**Cauchy problem** on infinite domain:
$$u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx}, \quad -\infty < x < \infty, \quad t > 0$$
$$u(x, 0) = f(x), \quad u_t(x, 0) = g(x)$$

Is this well-posed?

**Solution:**

**Existence**: Yes (d'Alembert's formula gives explicit solution)

**Uniqueness**: Yes (d'Alembert gives unique answer)

**Stability**: Yes (d'Alembert shows solution bounded by initial data)

**Answer:** Well-posed ✓ (d'Alembert's formula proves all three)

## Maximum Principles and Uniqueness

### Example 10: Maximum Principle for Laplace

If $u$ is harmonic ($\nabla^2 u = 0$) in region $\Omega$, then maximum and minimum occur on boundary $\partial\Omega$.

Use this to prove uniqueness for Dirichlet problem.

**Solution:**

Suppose $u_1$ and $u_2$ both solve $\nabla^2 u = 0$ with $u = g$ on $\partial\Omega$.

Let $w = u_1 - u_2$. Then:
- $\nabla^2 w = 0$ (linear PDE)
- $w = 0$ on $\partial\Omega$ (both have same boundary values)

By maximum principle: $\max w$ and $\min w$ occur on boundary.

Since $w = 0$ on boundary: $0 \leq w \leq 0$ everywhere.

Therefore $w = 0$, so $u_1 = u_2$ ✓

**Answer:** Uniqueness proven via maximum principle

### Example 11: Heat Equation Maximum Principle

For $u_t = ku_{xx}$ in rectangle $0 \leq x \leq L$, $0 \leq t \leq T$:

Maximum occurs on:
- Initial time: $t = 0$ (entire interval)
- Spatial boundary: $x = 0$ or $x = L$

Use this for uniqueness.

**Solution:**

If $u_1, u_2$ both solve problem with same initial/boundary data:

$w = u_1 - u_2$ satisfies:
- $w_t = kw_{xx}$
- $w(x, 0) = 0$
- $w(0, t) = w(L, t) = 0$

Maximum principle: $\max |w|$ occurs on parabolic boundary (initial time or spatial boundary).

Since $w = 0$ there: $w = 0$ everywhere.

**Answer:** Uniqueness via maximum principle (for parabolic PDEs)

### Example 12: Energy Method for Wave Equation

Define energy: $E(t) = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^L [u_t^2 + c^2u_x^2] \, dx$

Show $E(t) = \text{constant}$ for wave equation with fixed ends.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{dE}{dt} = \int_0^L [u_tu_{tt} + c^2u_xu_{xt}] \, dx$$

Using $u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx}$:
$$= \int_0^L [u_t \cdot c^2u_{xx} + c^2u_xu_{xt}] \, dx$$
$$= c^2\int_0^L [u_tu_{xx} + u_xu_{xt}] \, dx$$
$$= c^2\int_0^L [u_tu_{xx} + u_xu_{tx}] \, dx$$
$$= c^2[u_tu_x]_0^L = 0$$ (since $u(0,t) = u(L,t) = 0$ means $u_t = 0$ at ends)

**Answer:** Energy conserved; proves uniqueness

### Example 13: Overspecified Problem

For Laplace's equation in square, what if we specify:
- $u = g_1$ on top edge
- $u = g_2$ on right edge  
- $u = g_3$ on bottom edge
- $u = g_4$ on left edge
- AND $u_x = h$ on top edge

Is this well-posed?

**Solution:**

**Overspecified**: Too many conditions (5 conditions for 4 sides)

Conditions may be inconsistent (no solution exists)

If $g_1$ and $h$ are incompatible, problem has no solution.

**Answer:** Ill-posed: overspecified (too many conditions may conflict)

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Hadamard's definition of well-posedness:
1. Existence: solution exists
2. Uniqueness: only one solution
3. Stability: continuous dependence on data
All three required for physical meaningfulness
Missing any criterion makes problem ill-posed

**Problem 2: B**
Dirichlet: specify $u|_{\text{boundary}} = g$
Neumann: specify $\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}|_{\text{boundary}} = g$
Robin: specify $\alpha u + \beta\frac{\partial u}{\partial n} = g$
Example: fixed temperature at wall

**Problem 3: C**
Second-order in $x$ requires 2 boundary conditions
One at each spatial endpoint
Plus 1 initial condition (first-order in $t$)
Total: 3 conditions for well-posed problem

**Problem 4: B**
$u_t = -ku_{xx}$ (wrong sign)
Runs time backward (anti-diffusion)
Unstable: small high-frequency errors grow exponentially
Physically unrealistic (violates second law)
Compare forward heat equation (well-posed)

**Problem 5: B**
Harmonic functions ($\nabla^2 u = 0$) achieve max/min on boundary
Interior values are averages (mean value property)
Cannot have interior extrema
Used to prove uniqueness
Fundamental property of elliptic PDEs
