# Partial Differential Equations Topic 3.4: Gibbs Phenomenon and Generalized Fourier Series

Compute a Fourier series for a square wave and plot the partial sums—something strange happens near the discontinuities. No matter how many terms you include, the approximation **overshoots** by about 9% above and below the jump. The oscillations get narrower as you add more terms, but the overshoot persists. This is the **Gibbs phenomenon**, discovered in 1899 and named after Josiah Willard Gibbs.

The Gibbs phenomenon isn't an error—it's a fundamental property of Fourier approximation at discontinuities. The overshoot approaches $\frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi} \frac{\sin t}{t} dt - 1 \approx 0.089$ (8.9%) as the number of terms increases. Understanding this phenomenon is crucial when applying Fourier methods to real-world problems: don't be alarmed by ringing artifacts near sharp edges!

This article explores the Gibbs phenomenon in depth and extends to **generalized Fourier series** with other orthogonal function families. We'll see the overshoot behavior, understand why it occurs, learn how to mitigate it (windowing, smoothing), and discover that sines and cosines aren't the only game in town—Legendre polynomials, Chebyshev polynomials, and other orthogonal functions also generate Fourier-like expansions.

## The Gibbs Phenomenon

### Example 1: Partial Sum for Square Wave

For square wave $f(x) = \text{sgn}(x)$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$:
$$S_N(x) = \frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{N} \frac{\sin((2k+1)x)}{2k+1}$$

Evaluate $S_5(x)$ near $x = 0$.

**Solution:**

$$S_5(x) = \frac{4}{\pi}\left[\sin x + \frac{\sin 3x}{3} + \frac{\sin 5x}{5} + \frac{\sin 7x}{7} + \frac{\sin 9x}{9} + \frac{\sin 11x}{11}\right]$$

At $x = 0.5$:
$$S_5(0.5) \approx 1.089$$

But $f(0.5) = 1$!

**Overshoot**: $\approx 8.9\%$ above the true value.

**Answer:** Partial sum overshoots by ~9% near discontinuity

### Example 2: Overshoot Doesn't Disappear

Does overshoot decrease as $N \to \infty$?

**Solution:**

**No!** As $N$ increases:
- Overshoot **height** stays $\approx 0.089$ (8.9%)
- Overshoot **width** shrinks as $\propto 1/N$
- Oscillations concentrate near discontinuity

**Limiting behavior**:
$$\max_{x} S_N(x) \to 1 + \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi} \frac{\sin t}{t} dt - 1 \approx 1.089$$

**Answer:** Overshoot persists at 8.9%, but narrows with increasing $N$

### Example 3: Gibbs Constant

The exact overshoot is:
$$G = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi} \frac{\sin t}{t} dt - 1$$

Compute this integral.

**Solution:**

The overshoot **amplitude** is:
$$\frac{2}{\pi} Si(\pi) - 1 \approx 0.089$$ times the **jump size**.

For jump from $-1$ to $+1$ (size 2), overshoot is $0.089 \times 2 = 0.178 \approx 17.9\%$ of half-jump.

Or **8.9% of the full function range**.

**Answer:** Gibbs overshoot is about 9% of jump size

### Example 4: Physical Interpretation

Why does Gibbs phenomenon occur?

**Solution:**

**Intuition**: Fourier series is trying to approximate discontinuity using *smooth* functions (sines/cosines).

**Analogy**: Approximating a step with smooth curves necessarily creates overshoot (like a polynomial trying to fit a vertical line).

**Mathematical**: Truncated series has **finite bandwidth**. Cannot represent infinite frequency content of discontinuity.

**Ringing** near edges is fundamental to band-limited approximations.

**Answer:** Smooth approximations to discontinuities necessarily overshoot

## Reducing Gibbs Phenomenon

### Example 5: Windowing (Lanczos Sigma Factor)

Multiply Fourier coefficients by:
$$\sigma_n = \frac{\sin(n\pi/N)}{n\pi/N}$$

**Solution:**

Modified partial sum:
$$\tilde{S}_N(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \sigma_n b_n \sin(nx)$$

**Effect**: Reduces overshoot significantly (to ~1-2%).

**Trade-off**: Transition becomes less sharp (wider "blur").

**Applications**: Image processing, signal reconstruction.

**Answer:** Windowing reduces overshoot but broadens transition

### Example 6: Smoothing the Function

Add small smooth transition region near discontinuity.

**Solution:**

Instead of $f(x) = \text{sgn}(x)$, use:
$$f_{\epsilon}(x) = \begin{cases} -1 & x < -\epsilon \\ x/\epsilon & -\epsilon \leq x \leq \epsilon \\ 1 & x > \epsilon \end{cases}$$

**Result**: Gibbs phenomenon disappears (function now continuous).

**Trade-off**: Not modeling the original discontinuous function.

**Answer:** Smoothing eliminates Gibbs but changes problem

## Generalized Fourier Series

Not just sines and cosines—any **orthogonal set** of functions generates Fourier-like series.

### Example 7: Orthogonal Function Set

Functions $\{\phi_n(x)\}$ are orthogonal on $[a, b]$ with weight $w(x)$ if:
$$\int_a^b w(x)\phi_m(x)\phi_n(x) \, dx = \begin{cases} 0 & m \neq n \\ \|\phi_n\|^2 & m = n \end{cases}$$

**Solution:**

**Examples:**
- Sines/cosines on $[-L, L]$ with $w(x) = 1$
- Legendre polynomials on $[-1, 1]$ with $w(x) = 1$
- Chebyshev polynomials on $[-1, 1]$ with $w(x) = 1/\sqrt{1-x^2}$
- Hermite polynomials on $(-\infty, \infty)$ with $w(x) = e^{-x^2}$

**Answer:** Many orthogonal function families exist

### Example 8: Generalized Fourier Coefficients

Expand $f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n \phi_n(x)$.

Find $c_n$.

**Solution:**

Multiply by $\phi_m$ and integrate with weight:
$$\int_a^b w(x)f(x)\phi_m(x) \, dx = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n \int_a^b w(x)\phi_n(x)\phi_m(x) \, dx$$

By orthogonality, only $n = m$ term survives:
$$c_m = \frac{\int_a^b w(x)f(x)\phi_m(x) \, dx}{\int_a^b w(x)\phi_m^2(x) \, dx} = \frac{\langle f, \phi_m \rangle}{\|\phi_m\|^2}$$

**Answer:** $c_n = \frac{\langle f, \phi_n \rangle}{\|\phi_n\|^2}$ (generalized Fourier coefficients)

### Example 9: Legendre Polynomial Expansion

Legendre polynomials $P_n(x)$ on $[-1, 1]$:
$$P_0 = 1, \quad P_1 = x, \quad P_2 = \frac{1}{2}(3x^2 - 1), \quad P_3 = \frac{1}{2}(5x^3 - 3x), \ldots$$

Expand $f(x) = x^2$.

**Solution:**

Orthogonality: $\int_{-1}^{1} P_m(x)P_n(x) \, dx = \frac{2}{2n+1}\delta_{mn}$

Need to express $x^2$ in terms of $P_n$:
$$x^2 = \frac{1}{3}(2P_2(x) + 1)$$

Check:
$$\frac{1}{3}[2 \cdot \frac{1}{2}(3x^2-1) + 1] = \frac{1}{3}[3x^2 - 1 + 1] = x^2$$ ✓

Generalized Fourier series:
$$x^2 = \frac{1}{3}P_0(x) + \frac{2}{3}P_2(x)$$

**Only two terms!** (Finite expansion for polynomial)

**Answer:** $x^2 = \frac{1}{3}P_0 + \frac{2}{3}P_2$ (finite sum)

### Example 10: Why Different Bases?

When would you use Legendre vs. Fourier basis?

**Solution:**

**Fourier (trigonometric):**
- Periodic functions
- Time series, signals
- PDE with periodic boundary conditions

**Legendre:**
- Non-periodic functions on finite interval
- Polynomial approximation
- Angular problems (spherical harmonics)

**Chebyshev:**
- Minimax approximation
- Numerical methods (spectral methods)
- Reduces oscillation near endpoints

**Answer:** Different bases optimize for different problem types

### Example 11: Completeness

Can any $L^2$ function be represented by Legendre series?

**Solution:**

**Yes!** Legendre polynomials form **complete orthogonal set** on $L^2[-1, 1]$.

$$f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n P_n(x)$$

converges in $L^2$ sense for any square-integrable $f$.

**Parseval-type identity**:
$$\int_{-1}^{1} |f(x)|^2 \, dx = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{2c_n^2}{2n+1}$$

**Answer:** Legendre basis is complete in $L^2[-1,1]$

### Example 12: Bessel Function Orthogonality

For circular domains, Bessel functions $J_n(\alpha_{nk} r)$ are orthogonal:
$$\int_0^1 r J_n(\alpha_{nm} r) J_n(\alpha_{nk} r) \, dr = 0 \quad (m \neq k)$$

where $\alpha_{nk}$ are zeros of $J_n$.

**Solution:**

These arise naturally in separation of variables for circular membrane.

**Fourier-Bessel series**:
$$f(r) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} c_k J_n(\alpha_{nk} r)$$

$$c_k = \frac{\int_0^1 r f(r) J_n(\alpha_{nk} r) \, dr}{\int_0^1 r J_n^2(\alpha_{nk} r) \, dr}$$

**Answer:** Bessel functions provide radial orthogonal basis

### Example 13: Sturm-Liouville Theory Preview

All these orthogonal sets arise from **Sturm-Liouville problems**:
$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[p(x)\frac{dy}{dx}\right] + [\lambda w(x) - q(x)]y = 0$$

**Solution:**

**Examples:**
- Fourier: $y'' + \lambda y = 0$ (constant $p = 1$)
- Legendre: $(1-x^2)y'' - 2xy' + n(n+1)y = 0$
- Bessel: $x^2y'' + xy' + (x^2 - n^2)y = 0$

**General theory** (Unit 8) shows eigenfunctions are orthogonal and complete.

**Answer:** Sturm-Liouville theory unifies all orthogonal expansions

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
Gibbs constant: overshoot $\approx 8.9\%$ of jump size
Exact: $\frac{2}{\pi} Si(\pi) - 1 \approx 0.089$
Occurs at every discontinuity
Fundamental to band-limited approximation
Cannot be eliminated by adding terms

**Problem 2: B**
Overshoot height: stays $\approx 0.089$ (constant)
Overshoot width: shrinks as $\propto 1/N$
Total oscillatory energy → 0
But peak magnitude persists
Ringing concentrates near discontinuity

**Problem 3: B**
Legendre $P_n(x)$ on $[-1, 1]$
Weight function $w(x) = 1$
Orthogonality: $\int_{-1}^{1} P_m P_n = \frac{2}{2n+1}\delta_{mn}$
Arise in spherical harmonics
Used for non-periodic functions

**Problem 4: B**
$c_n = \frac{\langle f, \phi_n \rangle}{\|\phi_n\|^2} = \frac{\int w(x)f(x)\phi_n(x) dx}{\int w(x)\phi_n^2(x) dx}$
Inner product with basis function
Orthogonality isolates coefficient
Same principle as Fourier $a_n$, $b_n$
Works for any orthogonal basis

**Problem 5: C**
Windowing (e.g., Lanczos $\sigma$-factor)
Modifies coefficients: $\tilde{c}_n = \sigma_n c_n$
Reduces overshoot to 1-2%
Trade-off: broader transition region
Common in signal processing
