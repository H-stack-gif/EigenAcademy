# Partial Differential Equations Topic 3.1: Fourier Series Fundamentals

Strike a piano key and listen to the rich sound. That single note contains not just the fundamental frequency, but a whole spectrum of overtones—harmonics at integer multiples of the base frequency. Your ear performs a remarkable feat: it decomposes the complex waveform into its constituent pure tones. **Fourier series** accomplish this mathematically, expressing any periodic function as an infinite sum of sines and cosines.

Jean-Baptiste Joseph Fourier introduced this idea in 1807 while studying heat conduction. His radical claim: *any* periodic function, no matter how irregular, can be represented as a sum of simple trigonometric functions. The mathematical establishment initially rejected this—surely discontinuous functions couldn't be expressed by smooth sines and cosines! Yet Fourier was right, and his series became one of the most powerful tools in mathematics and engineering.

This article develops Fourier series from scratch. We'll discover why sines and cosines are special (orthogonality), derive formulas for the coefficients, compute Fourier series for standard functions, and understand the physical meaning. These series are the key to solving partial differential equations with separation of variables—the foundation for the heat equation, wave equation, and beyond.

## Periodic Functions and Fourier Series

A function $f(x)$ is **periodic** with period $2L$ if $f(x + 2L) = f(x)$ for all $x$.

### Example 1: Periodic Functions

Which functions are periodic?

**Solution:**

**Periodic:**
- $\sin(x)$ with period $2\pi$
- $\cos(3x)$ with period $2\pi/3$
- $|x|$ on $[-1, 1]$ extended periodically
- Square wave (alternating +1 and -1)

**Not periodic:**
- $x$ (grows without bound)
- $e^x$ (exponential growth)
- $x^2$ (unbounded)

**Answer:** Repeating patterns are periodic; growing functions are not

### Example 2: Fourier Series Definition

For periodic function $f(x)$ with period $2L$:

$$f(x) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left[a_n\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) + b_n\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\right]$$

What are the $a_n$ and $b_n$?

**Solution:**

These are **Fourier coefficients**, to be determined.

**Physical meaning:**
- $a_0/2$ is the average value (DC component)
- $a_n\cos(n\pi x/L)$ are even harmonics
- $b_n\sin(n\pi x/L)$ are odd harmonics
- Frequency increases with $n$ (higher harmonics)

**Answer:** Coefficients determined by orthogonality (next examples)

## Orthogonality of Trigonometric Functions

### Example 3: Orthogonality of Sines

Show $\int_{-L}^{L} \sin(m\pi x/L)\sin(n\pi x/L) \, dx = 0$ for $m \neq n$.

**Solution:**

Use product-to-sum formula:
$$\sin A \sin B = \frac{1}{2}[\cos(A-B) - \cos(A+B)]$$

$$\int_{-L}^{L} \sin(m\pi x/L)\sin(n\pi x/L) \, dx$$
$$= \frac{1}{2}\int_{-L}^{L} \left[\cos\left(\frac{(m-n)\pi x}{L}\right) - \cos\left(\frac{(m+n)\pi x}{L}\right)\right] dx$$

For $m \neq n$, both integrals of cosines over full periods are zero.

**For $m = n$**:
$$\int_{-L}^{L} \sin^2(n\pi x/L) \, dx = L$$

**Answer:** $\int_{-L}^{L} \sin(m\pi x/L)\sin(n\pi x/L) \, dx = \begin{cases} L & m = n \\ 0 & m \neq n \end{cases}$

### Example 4: Orthogonality of Cosines

Similarly for cosines:

**Solution:**

$$\int_{-L}^{L} \cos(m\pi x/L)\cos(n\pi x/L) \, dx = \begin{cases} 2L & m = n = 0 \\ L & m = n \neq 0 \\ 0 & m \neq n \end{cases}$$

**Answer:** Cosines also form orthogonal set

### Example 5: Sines and Cosines are Orthogonal

Show $\int_{-L}^{L} \sin(m\pi x/L)\cos(n\pi x/L) \, dx = 0$ for all $m, n$.

**Solution:**

$$\sin A \cos B = \frac{1}{2}[\sin(A+B) + \sin(A-B)]$$

$$\int_{-L}^{L} \sin(m\pi x/L)\cos(n\pi x/L) \, dx$$
$$= \frac{1}{2}\int_{-L}^{L} \left[\sin\left(\frac{(m+n)\pi x}{L}\right) + \sin\left(\frac{(m-n)\pi x}{L}\right)\right] dx$$

Both integrals are zero (sine over symmetric interval).

**Answer:** Sines and cosines are mutually orthogonal

## Computing Fourier Coefficients

### Example 6: Formula for $a_0$

To find $a_0$, integrate $f(x) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum a_n\cos(...) + \sum b_n\sin(...)$ over $[-L, L]$.

**Solution:**

$$\int_{-L}^{L} f(x) \, dx = \int_{-L}^{L} \frac{a_0}{2} \, dx + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n \int_{-L}^{L} \cos(n\pi x/L) \, dx + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n \int_{-L}^{L} \sin(n\pi x/L) \, dx$$

Cosine and sine integrals are zero:
$$\int_{-L}^{L} f(x) \, dx = \frac{a_0}{2} \cdot 2L = a_0 L$$

$$a_0 = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x) \, dx$$

**Answer:** $a_0$ is twice the average value of $f$

### Example 7: Formula for $a_n$

Multiply both sides by $\cos(n\pi x/L)$ and integrate:

**Solution:**

$$\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\cos(n\pi x/L) \, dx$$
$$= \int_{-L}^{L} \frac{a_0}{2}\cos(n\pi x/L) \, dx + \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} a_m \int_{-L}^{L} \cos(m\pi x/L)\cos(n\pi x/L) \, dx + \cdots$$

By orthogonality, only $m = n$ term survives:
$$\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\cos(n\pi x/L) \, dx = a_n \cdot L$$

$$a_n = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) dx$$

**Answer:** $a_n = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\cos(n\pi x/L) \, dx$

### Example 8: Formula for $b_n$

Similarly, multiply by $\sin(n\pi x/L)$ and integrate:

**Solution:**

$$b_n = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) dx$$

**Answer:** $b_n = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\sin(n\pi x/L) \, dx$

## Examples of Fourier Series

### Example 9: Constant Function

Find Fourier series for $f(x) = 1$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$.

**Solution:**

Here $L = \pi$.

$$a_0 = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} 1 \, dx = \frac{2\pi}{\pi} = 2$$

$$a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \cos(nx) \, dx = 0$$

$$b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \sin(nx) \, dx = 0$$

Fourier series: $f(x) = 1$ (just the constant!)

**Answer:** Constant function is its own Fourier series

### Example 10: Square Wave

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & 0 < x < \pi \\ -1 & -\pi < x < 0 \end{cases}$$

Extended periodically with period $2\pi$.

**Solution:**

$L = \pi$. Function is odd: $f(-x) = -f(x)$, so $a_n = 0$ (all cosine coefficients zero).

$$b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\sin(nx) \, dx = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \sin(nx) \, dx$$

$$= \frac{2}{\pi} \cdot \left[-\frac{\cos(nx)}{n}\right]_0^{\pi} = \frac{2}{n\pi}[1 - \cos(n\pi)]$$

$$= \frac{2}{n\pi}[1 - (-1)^n] = \begin{cases} \frac{4}{n\pi} & n \text{ odd} \\ 0 & n \text{ even} \end{cases}$$

Fourier series:
$$f(x) = \frac{4}{\pi}\left[\sin x + \frac{\sin 3x}{3} + \frac{\sin 5x}{5} + \cdots\right] = \frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{\sin(2k+1)x}{2k+1}$$

**Answer:** $f(x) = \frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{\sin(2k+1)x}{2k+1}$

### Example 11: Triangular Wave

$$f(x) = |x|$$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$, extended periodically.

**Solution:**

Function is even: $f(-x) = f(x)$, so $b_n = 0$ (all sine coefficients zero).

$$a_0 = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} |x| \, dx = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} x \, dx = \frac{2}{\pi} \cdot \frac{\pi^2}{2} = \pi$$

$$a_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} x\cos(nx) \, dx$$

Integration by parts:
$$= \frac{2}{\pi}\left[\frac{x\sin(nx)}{n}\Big|_0^{\pi} - \int_0^{\pi} \frac{\sin(nx)}{n} \, dx\right]$$

$$= \frac{2}{\pi}\left[0 + \frac{\cos(nx)}{n^2}\Big|_0^{\pi}\right] = \frac{2}{n^2\pi}[\cos(n\pi) - 1]$$

$$= \frac{2}{n^2\pi}[(-1)^n - 1] = \begin{cases} -\frac{4}{n^2\pi} & n \text{ odd} \\ 0 & n \text{ even} \end{cases}$$

Fourier series:
$$f(x) = \frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{4}{\pi}\left[\cos x + \frac{\cos 3x}{9} + \frac{\cos 5x}{25} + \cdots\right]$$

**Answer:** $f(x) = \frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{\cos(2k+1)x}{(2k+1)^2}$

## Physical Interpretation

### Example 12: Harmonics and Overtones

For square wave, why only odd harmonics?

**Solution:**

Square wave has **half-wave symmetry**: $f(x + \pi) = -f(x)$.

This forces all even harmonics to zero.

**Musical analogy**:
- Clarinet produces mostly odd harmonics (cylindrical bore)
- Flute produces both even and odd harmonics (open pipe)
- Square wave sounds "hollow" like clarinet

**Answer:** Symmetry properties determine which harmonics appear

### Example 13: Energy Distribution (Parseval's Identity)

For normalized function:
$$\frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} |f(x)|^2 \, dx = \frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (a_n^2 + b_n^2)$$

For square wave $f(x) = \pm 1$:

**Solution:**

$$\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} 1^2 \, dx = 2$$

From Fourier series: $b_n = \frac{4}{n\pi}$ for odd $n$, zero for even $n$.

$$\sum_{n=1,3,5,\ldots}^{\infty} \left(\frac{4}{n\pi}\right)^2 = \frac{16}{\pi^2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{(2k+1)^2} = \frac{16}{\pi^2} \cdot \frac{\pi^2}{8} = 2$$ ✓

**Answer:** Total energy equals sum of energies in each harmonic

## Practice Problems

A) Amplitude
B) Frequency

**Problem 4:** Orthogonality means:

A) Functions perpendicular
B) $\int \sin(mx)\sin(nx) = 0$ for $m \neq n$
C) Functions equal
D) Zero everywhere

**Problem 5:** For square wave, $b_n \propto$:

A) $1/n^2$
B) $1/n$ for odd $n$
C) $e^{-n}$
D) $n$
E) Constant

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Harmonics: $\cos(n\pi x/L)$ and $\sin(n\pi x/L)$
Angular frequency: $\omega_n = n\pi/L$ (spatial frequency)
Wavelength: $\lambda_n = 2L/n$
Fundamental ($n=1$): $\omega_1 = \pi/L$
First overtone ($n=2$): $\omega_2 = 2\pi/L$

**Problem 2: A**
Odd function: $f(-x) = -f(x)$
Cosines are even: $\cos(-x) = \cos(x)$
Product $f(x)\cos(nx)$ is odd
Integral over symmetric interval: zero
Therefore all $a_n = 0$ (including $a_0$)
Only sine terms (odd functions) survive

**Problem 3: C**
$a_0 = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x) \, dx = \frac{2}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x) \, dx / 2$
Average value: $\bar{f} = \frac{1}{2L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x) \, dx$
Therefore: $a_0 = 2\bar{f}$
Series form: $f(x) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum ...$
DC component in electrical engineering

**Problem 4: B**
Orthogonality: inner product is zero
$\langle f, g \rangle = \int_{-L}^{L} f(x)g(x) \, dx = 0$
Trigonometric functions form orthogonal set
Allows unique determination of coefficients
Geometric analogy: perpendicular vectors have dot product zero

**Problem 5: B**
Square wave: $b_n = \frac{4}{n\pi}$ for $n$ odd, $b_n = 0$ for $n$ even
Decay rate: $1/n$ (slow decay)
Sharp discontinuities → slow coefficient decay
Compare triangular wave: $a_n \propto 1/n^2$ (faster decay, continuous)
Smoother functions → faster coefficient decay
