# Partial Differential Equations Topic 4.4: Maximum Principle and Fundamental Solution

Imagine heating a metal rod and then letting it cool. Where does the maximum temperature occur at any time? Intuitively, heat flows from hot to cold, so temperature shouldn't spontaneously increase anywhere inside—the hottest point must be either at the boundaries or in the initial condition. This physical intuition is captured mathematically by the **maximum principle**: for the heat equation, the maximum temperature occurs on the **parabolic boundary** (initial time or spatial boundary).

The maximum principle is more than just intuition—it's a powerful tool for proving uniqueness, establishing bounds, and understanding qualitative behavior without solving explicitly. If two solutions have the same boundary and initial data, their difference satisfies the heat equation with zero data, so by the maximum principle this difference is zero everywhere. This proves uniqueness without finding the solution! The maximum principle reveals the deep connection between PDE structure and physical constraints.

This article explores the maximum principle and the **fundamental solution** (heat kernel) for infinite domains. We'll prove the maximum principle, use it for uniqueness, understand the heat kernel as impulse response, and see how solutions on infinite domains spread via convolution. The fundamental solution is the Green's function for heat equation, enabling us to write solutions as integrals rather than Fourier series.

## The Maximum Principle

**Theorem (Maximum Principle)**: Let $u$ satisfy $u_t = ku_{xx}$ in rectangle $0 \leq x \leq L$, $0 \leq t \leq T$. Then:

$$\max_{0 \leq x \leq L, 0 \leq t \leq T} u(x,t) = \max_{\text{parabolic boundary}} u$$

**Parabolic boundary**: Initial time ($t = 0$, all $x$) and spatial boundaries ($x = 0$ or $x = L$, all $t$).

### Example 1: Identifying Maximum Location

For heat equation on $[0, 1] \times [0, 5]$, where can maximum occur?

**Solution:**

**Parabolic boundary consists of**:
1. Bottom: $t = 0$, $0 \leq x \leq 1$ (initial condition)
2. Left: $x = 0$, $0 \leq t \leq 5$ (left boundary)
3. Right: $x = 1$, $0 \leq t \leq 5$ (right boundary)

Maximum CANNOT occur in interior ($0 < x < 1$, $0 < t \leq 5$).

**Physical meaning**: Heat doesn't spontaneously concentrate; hottest point is where you applied heat initially or at boundaries.

**Answer:** Maximum on initial time or spatial boundaries only

### Example 2: Why Not Top Boundary?

Why doesn't maximum occur at $t = T$ (final time)?

**Solution:**

Heat equation **dissipates** energy (increases entropy).

Temperature smooths out over time; extrema don't increase.

For wave equation (time-reversible), would need to check top boundary too.

**Heat vs. Wave**: Heat dissipative, wave conservative.

**Answer:** Heat equation is irreversible; maximum can't increase with time

### Example 3: Proof Sketch of Maximum Principle

Suppose maximum occurs at interior point $(x_0, t_0)$ with $0 < x_0 < L$, $0 < t_0 \leq T$.

**Solution:**

At maximum: $u_t \geq 0$ (increasing or stationary in time)

At spatial maximum: $u_x = 0$ and $u_{xx} \leq 0$ (concave down)

But heat equation: $u_t = ku_{xx}$

$$u_t = ku_{xx} \leq 0$$ (since $u_{xx} \leq 0$ and $k > 0$)

Contradiction with $u_t \geq 0$ unless $u_t = 0$ and $u_{xx} = 0$ (constant).

**Conclusion**: Maximum cannot occur in interior (unless $u$ is constant).

**Answer:** Interior maximum leads to contradiction with heat equation

## Applications of Maximum Principle

### Example 4: Uniqueness Proof

Prove uniqueness for heat equation with given boundary and initial data.

**Solution:**

Suppose $u_1$ and $u_2$ both solve problem with same data.

Let $w = u_1 - u_2$. Then:
- $w_t = kw_{xx}$ (linearity)
- $w(x, 0) = 0$ (same initial condition)
- $w(0, t) = w(L, t) = 0$ (same boundary conditions)

By maximum principle: $\max w = 0$ (occurs on parabolic boundary where $w = 0$)

Similarly, $\min w = 0$.

Therefore $w \equiv 0$, so $u_1 \equiv u_2$.

**Answer:** Uniqueness proven via maximum principle

### Example 5: Comparison Theorem

If $u$ and $v$ satisfy heat equation with $u \leq v$ on parabolic boundary, show $u \leq v$ everywhere.

**Solution:**

Let $w = v - u$. Then:
- $w_t = kw_{xx}$ (difference of solutions)
- $w \geq 0$ on parabolic boundary (given)

By maximum principle: $\min w$ occurs on parabolic boundary.

Since $w \geq 0$ there: $w \geq 0$ everywhere.

Therefore $v \geq u$ everywhere.

**Answer:** Order preserved by heat equation (comparison principle)

### Example 6: Bounded Initial Data

If $|u(x, 0)| \leq M$ and boundaries held at zero, show $|u(x, t)| \leq M$ for all $t$.

**Solution:**

On parabolic boundary:
- $|u(x, 0)| \leq M$ (initial condition)
- $u(0, t) = u(L, t) = 0 \leq M$ (boundaries)

So $|u| \leq M$ on parabolic boundary.

By maximum principle: $u \leq M$ everywhere.
By minimum principle: $u \geq -M$ everywhere.

**Answer:** $|u(x,t)| \leq M$ for all $(x,t)$ (maximum norm non-increasing)

## The Fundamental Solution (Heat Kernel)

For heat equation on infinite domain $-\infty < x < \infty$:

$$u_t = ku_{xx}$$

with initial condition $u(x, 0) = \delta(x)$ (Dirac delta at origin).

**Fundamental solution** (heat kernel):

$$G(x, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi kt}} e^{-x^2/(4kt)}$$

### Example 7: Verifying Fundamental Solution

Show $G(x,t)$ satisfies heat equation for $t > 0$.

**Solution:**

$$G = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi kt}}e^{-x^2/(4kt)}$$

$$G_t = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi kt}}e^{-x^2/(4kt)}\right]$$

Using product rule and chain rule:
$$G_t = -\frac{1}{2t}G + G \cdot \frac{x^2}{4kt^2} = G\left(\frac{x^2}{4kt^2} - \frac{1}{2t}\right)$$

$$G_x = G \cdot \left(-\frac{2x}{4kt}\right) = -\frac{x}{2kt}G$$

$$G_{xx} = -\frac{1}{2kt}G - \frac{x}{2kt}G_x = -\frac{1}{2kt}G + \frac{x^2}{4k^2t^2}G$$

$$kG_{xx} = G\left(\frac{x^2}{4kt^2} - \frac{1}{2t}\right) = G_t$$ ✓

**Answer:** Heat kernel satisfies $G_t = kG_{xx}$

### Example 8: Properties of Heat Kernel

Show $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} G(x, t) \, dx = 1$ for all $t > 0$.

**Solution:**

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi kt}}e^{-x^2/(4kt)} \, dx$$

Let $\xi = \frac{x}{\sqrt{4kt}}$, so $dx = \sqrt{4kt} \, d\xi$:

$$= \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\xi^2} \, d\xi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} \cdot \sqrt{\pi} = 1$$

**Physical meaning**: Total "heat" (integral) conserved (no boundaries to lose heat through).

**Answer:** $\int G \, dx = 1$ (normalized)

### Example 9: Spreading of Heat Kernel

How does $G(x, t)$ evolve as $t$ increases?

**Solution:**

- **Height**: $G(0, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi kt}} \sim t^{-1/2}$ (decreases)
- **Width**: $\sigma = \sqrt{2kt}$ (standard deviation increases)
- **Area**: $\int G \, dx = 1$ (conserved)

As $t \to \infty$: peak flattens, width increases, but area stays 1.

**Answer:** Gaussian spreads: height decreases as $t^{-1/2}$, width increases as $\sqrt{t}$

### Example 10: Solution via Convolution

General solution with initial condition $u(x, 0) = f(x)$:

$$u(x, t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} G(x - \xi, t) f(\xi) \, d\xi$$

This is **convolution** of $G$ with $f$.

**Solution:**

**Physical interpretation**: 
- Each point $\xi$ has initial temperature $f(\xi)$
- Treat as delta function with amplitude $f(\xi)$
- Heat from $\xi$ spreads as $f(\xi) \cdot G(x - \xi, t)$
- Total: sum (integrate) contributions from all $\xi$

**Answer:** Solution is superposition of heat kernels centered at each point

### Example 11: Gaussian Initial Data

Solve $u_t = ku_{xx}$ with $u(x, 0) = e^{-x^2/a^2}$.

**Solution:**

$$u(x, t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi kt}}e^{-(x-\xi)^2/(4kt)} \cdot e^{-\xi^2/a^2} \, d\xi$$

Completing the square in exponent (Gaussian integral):

$$u(x, t) = \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 + 4kt}} \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{a^2 + 4kt}\right)$$

**Physical meaning**: Gaussian initial profile stays Gaussian, but spreads: variance grows from $a^2/2$ to $(a^2 + 4kt)/2$.

**Answer:** $u(x,t) = \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 + 4kt}}e^{-x^2/(a^2 + 4kt)}$

### Example 12: Asymptotic Behavior

For fixed $x \neq 0$, what happens as $t \to \infty$?

**Solution:**

$$G(x, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi kt}}e^{-x^2/(4kt)}$$

As $t \to \infty$: exponent $\to 0$, but factor $t^{-1/2} \to 0$ faster.

$$G(x, t) \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi kt}} \to 0$$

Heat spreads over entire line; temperature at any fixed point goes to zero.

**Answer:** $G(x,t) \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$ for fixed $x$

### Example 13: Finite vs. Infinite Domain

Compare long-time behavior on finite vs. infinite domain:

**Solution:**

**Finite domain** $[0, L]$ with $u(0,t) = u(L,t) = 0$:
- Fundamental mode: $e^{-\pi^2 kt/L^2}\sin(\pi x/L)$
- Decays exponentially: $u \to 0$

**Infinite domain** $-\infty < x < \infty$:
- Heat kernel: $G \sim t^{-1/2}$
- Decays algebraically (power law): slower!
- Total heat conserved (spreads over infinite space)

**Answer:** Finite domain has exponential decay; infinite domain has power-law decay

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
Parabolic boundary: $t = 0$ (initial) and $x = 0, L$ (spatial boundaries)
Maximum cannot occur in interior
Physical: heat doesn't spontaneously concentrate
Contrast wave equation (need all four sides)
Used for uniqueness proofs

**Problem 2: C**
Heat kernel: $G(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi kt}}e^{-x^2/(4kt)}$
Fundamental solution (impulse response)
Gaussian with variance $2kt$
Satisfies $G_t = kG_{xx}$
Initial condition: $G(x,0) = \delta(x)$

**Problem 3: C**
$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} G(x,t) \, dx = 1$ for all $t > 0$
Normalized (total heat conserved)
Gaussian integral: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/\sigma^2} \, dx = \sigma\sqrt{\pi}$
Physical: no boundaries, so no heat loss
Probability interpretation (diffusion = random walk)

**Problem 4: C**
Height: $G(0,t) = (4\pi kt)^{-1/2} \to 0$
Width: $\sigma = \sqrt{2kt} \to \infty$
Peak flattens, distribution spreads
Total area (integral) stays 1
Self-similar spreading

**Problem 5: B**
Maximum principle proves uniqueness
If $u_1, u_2$ solve same problem: $w = u_1 - u_2$ has max 0
So $w \leq 0$; similarly $w \geq 0$
Therefore $w = 0$, $u_1 = u_2$
Also: comparison theorems, bounds on solutions
