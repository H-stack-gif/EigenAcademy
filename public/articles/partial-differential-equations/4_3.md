# Partial Differential Equations Topic 4.3: Fourier Series Solutions

We've found infinitely many solutions to the heat equation—one for each normal mode $u_n(x,t) = \sin(n\pi x/L)e^{-n^2\pi^2 kt/L^2}$. But how do we solve the problem with specific initial temperature $u(x, 0) = f(x)$? The answer lies in **superposition**: by combining normal modes with appropriate coefficients, we can match any initial condition. This is the power of **Fourier series**—representing arbitrary functions as infinite sums of sines and cosines.

Joseph Fourier's revolutionary 1822 insight was that *any* reasonable function can be written as a trigonometric series. This seemed impossible to his contemporaries (how could a discontinuous function equal a sum of smooth sines?), but Fourier was right. The normal modes form a **complete orthogonal basis**: they're mutually perpendicular (orthogonal) and span the entire function space. Finding Fourier coefficients is just projecting the initial temperature onto each basis function.

This article develops Fourier series solutions to the heat equation. We'll learn to compute Fourier coefficients, understand orthogonality, solve specific initial value problems, and interpret solutions physically. The method transforms heat equation problems from impossible to routine: expand initial condition, multiply each term by its decay factor, done. This is the standard technique for solving PDEs on finite domains.

## General Solution by Superposition

By superposition (linearity), if each $u_n$ satisfies the heat equation, so does:

$$u(x, t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_n \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) e^{-n^2\pi^2 kt/L^2}$$

### Example 1: Matching Initial Condition

For $u(x, 0) = f(x)$:

**Solution:**

At $t = 0$:
$$f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_n \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$$

This is a **Fourier sine series** for $f(x)$.

**Question**: How to find coefficients $c_n$?

**Answer**: Use orthogonality of sine functions

### Example 2: Orthogonality of Sines

Show that $\int_0^L \sin(m\pi x/L)\sin(n\pi x/L) \, dx = 0$ for $m \neq n$.

**Solution:**

Use product-to-sum formula:
$$\sin A \sin B = \frac{1}{2}[\cos(A-B) - \cos(A+B)]$$

$$\int_0^L \sin(m\pi x/L)\sin(n\pi x/L) \, dx$$
$$= \frac{1}{2}\int_0^L \left[\cos\left(\frac{(m-n)\pi x}{L}\right) - \cos\left(\frac{(m+n)\pi x}{L}\right)\right] dx$$

For $m \neq n$, both cosines integrate to zero over full period.

For $m = n$:
$$\int_0^L \sin^2(n\pi x/L) \, dx = \frac{L}{2}$$

**Answer:** $\int_0^L \sin(m\pi x/L)\sin(n\pi x/L) \, dx = \begin{cases} L/2 & m = n \\ 0 & m \neq n \end{cases}$

### Example 3: Finding Fourier Coefficients

From $f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_n \sin(n\pi x/L)$, multiply both sides by $\sin(m\pi x/L)$ and integrate:

**Solution:**

$$\int_0^L f(x)\sin(m\pi x/L) \, dx = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_n \int_0^L \sin(n\pi x/L)\sin(m\pi x/L) \, dx$$

By orthogonality, only $n = m$ term survives:
$$\int_0^L f(x)\sin(m\pi x/L) \, dx = c_m \cdot \frac{L}{2}$$

Therefore:
$$c_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L f(x)\sin(n\pi x/L) \, dx$$

**Answer:** Fourier coefficients $c_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L f(x)\sin(n\pi x/L) \, dx$

### Example 4: Constant Initial Temperature

Solve $u_t = ku_{xx}$ with $u(0,t) = u(1,t) = 0$ and $u(x,0) = 1$ for $0 < x < 1$.

**Solution:**

$L = 1$, $f(x) = 1$.

$$c_n = 2\int_0^1 1 \cdot \sin(n\pi x) \, dx = 2 \cdot \frac{-\cos(n\pi x)}{n\pi}\bigg|_0^1$$
$$= \frac{2}{n\pi}[-\cos(n\pi) + \cos(0)] = \frac{2}{n\pi}[1 - (-1)^n]$$

For $n$ even: $c_n = 0$
For $n$ odd: $c_n = \frac{4}{n\pi}$

Solution:
$$u(x,t) = \frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{n=1,3,5,\ldots}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n}\sin(n\pi x)e^{-n^2\pi^2 kt}$$

**Answer:** $u = \frac{4}{\pi}\left[\sin(\pi x)e^{-\pi^2 kt} + \frac{1}{3}\sin(3\pi x)e^{-9\pi^2 kt} + \cdots\right]$

### Example 5: Triangular Initial Temperature

Solve with $u(x, 0) = x$ for $0 < x < 1$.

**Solution:**

$$c_n = 2\int_0^1 x \sin(n\pi x) \, dx$$

Integration by parts: $u = x$, $dv = \sin(n\pi x)dx$
$$= 2\left[-\frac{x\cos(n\pi x)}{n\pi}\bigg|_0^1 + \frac{1}{n\pi}\int_0^1 \cos(n\pi x) \, dx\right]$$
$$= 2\left[-\frac{\cos(n\pi)}{n\pi} + 0\right] = \frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi}$$

For $n$ even: $c_n = \frac{2}{n\pi}$
For $n$ odd: $c_n = -\frac{2}{n\pi}$

**Answer:** $c_n = \frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi}$

### Example 6: Verification at $t = 0$

Does $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi}\sin(n\pi x)$ actually equal $x$?

**Solution:**

This is Fourier series for $f(x) = x$ on $[0, 1]$.

At $x = 1/2$:
$$\frac{1}{2} = \frac{2}{\pi}\left[1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{7} + \cdots\right]$$

This is Leibniz series: $\frac{\pi}{4} = 1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \cdots$ ✓

**Answer:** Yes, series converges to $x$ for $0 < x < 1$

## Physical Interpretation

### Example 7: Energy Decay

Total thermal energy: $E(t) = \int_0^L u^2(x,t) \, dx$

How does it decay?

**Solution:**

By Parseval's identity:
$$E(t) = \int_0^L u^2 \, dx = \frac{L}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_n^2 e^{-2n^2\pi^2 kt/L^2}$$

Each mode contributes $c_n^2 e^{-2n^2\pi^2 kt/L^2}$ to energy.

Higher modes decay faster (exponentially in $n^2$).

**Answer:** Energy decays exponentially; higher modes contribute less over time

### Example 8: Approach to Equilibrium

For Example 4 ($u(x,0) = 1$), what is steady-state solution?

**Solution:**

With $u(0,t) = u(1,t) = 0$ (fixed at 0):

Steady state satisfies $u_{xx} = 0$ with $u(0) = u(1) = 0$.

Only solution: $u_{\infty} \equiv 0$

As $t \to \infty$, all modes decay: $u \to 0$ everywhere.

**Physical**: Heat escapes through boundaries held at zero.

**Answer:** $u \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$ (equilibrates to boundary temperature)

### Example 9: Insulated Ends

If instead $u_x(0,t) = u_x(L,t) = 0$ (insulated), what changes?

**Solution:**

Eigenvalue problem: $X'' + \lambda X = 0$ with $X'(0) = X'(L) = 0$

Solutions: $X_0 = 1$ (constant), $X_n = \cos(n\pi x/L)$ for $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$

Eigenvalues: $\lambda_0 = 0$, $\lambda_n = (n\pi/L)^2$

General solution:
$$u(x,t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n \cos(n\pi x/L)e^{-n^2\pi^2 kt/L^2}$$

As $t \to \infty$: $u \to a_0/2 = \frac{1}{L}\int_0^L f(x) \, dx$ (average temperature)

**Answer:** Fourier cosine series; equilibrates to average initial temperature

### Example 10: Dominant Mode

For large $t$, which mode dominates?

**Solution:**

$$u(x,t) \approx c_1 \sin(\pi x/L)e^{-\pi^2 kt/L^2}$$

All higher modes exponentially smaller.

**Time for dominance**: When $e^{-4\pi^2 kt/L^2} \ll e^{-\pi^2 kt/L^2}$

Approximately $t \gtrsim L^2/(k\pi^2)$.

**Answer:** Fundamental mode ($n = 1$) dominates for $t \gg L^2/k$

### Example 11: Half-Life of Modes

How long for mode $n$ to decay to half its initial amplitude?

**Solution:**

$$e^{-n^2\pi^2 kt_{1/2}/L^2} = \frac{1}{2}$$

$$t_{1/2} = \frac{L^2 \ln 2}{n^2\pi^2 k}$$

Mode $n = 2$ has one-quarter the half-life of $n = 1$.

**Answer:** $t_{1/2}^{(n)} = \frac{L^2\ln 2}{n^2\pi^2 k}$ (decreases as $1/n^2$)

### Example 12: Initial Discontinuity

If $u(x,0) = \begin{cases} 1 & 0 < x < L/2 \\ 0 & L/2 < x < L \end{cases}$, what happens immediately?

**Solution:**

At $t = 0^+$ (just after $t = 0$):
- Solution becomes $C^\infty$ smooth (smoothing property)
- Discontinuity at $x = L/2$ instantly becomes continuous
- Fourier series converges to average at jump: $(1+0)/2 = 1/2$

For $t > 0$: infinitely differentiable everywhere.

**Answer:** Discontinuity immediately smooths out; solution smooth for $t > 0$

### Example 13: Gibbs Phenomenon

Near discontinuity in initial data, Fourier series "overshoots" by ~9%. Does this appear in solution for $t > 0$?

**Solution:**

**At $t = 0$**: Gibbs phenomenon present (overshoot in finite partial sums)

**For $t > 0$**: No overshoot!
- High-frequency modes (responsible for overshoot) decay rapidly
- After short time, only smooth modes remain
- Solution immediately becomes smooth

**Answer:** Gibbs phenomenon only at $t = 0$; vanishes immediately for $t > 0$

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
Fourier sine series: $f(x) = \sum c_n \sin(n\pi x/L)$
Multiply by $\sin(m\pi x/L)$ and integrate
Orthogonality: only $n = m$ term survives
$\int_0^L f \sin(m\pi x/L) \, dx = c_m (L/2)$
Therefore: $c_m = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L f \sin(m\pi x/L) \, dx$

**Problem 2: B**
Orthogonality of functions: inner product is zero
$\langle \sin(m\pi x/L), \sin(n\pi x/L) \rangle = \int_0^L \sin(m\pi x/L)\sin(n\pi x/L) \, dx = 0$
Like perpendicular vectors: $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} = 0$
Enables unique coefficient determination
Foundation of Fourier analysis

**Problem 3: C**
$c_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L 1 \cdot \sin(n\pi x/L) \, dx = \frac{2}{n\pi}[1 - (-1)^n]$
For $n$ even: $(-1)^n = 1$, so $c_n = 0$
For $n$ odd: $(-1)^n = -1$, so $c_n = \frac{4}{n\pi}$
Only odd harmonics present (symmetry)

**Problem 4: A**
Boundaries held at $u = 0$ (Dirichlet)
All modes decay: $e^{-n^2\pi^2 kt/L^2} \to 0$
Solution: $u = \sum c_n \sin(n\pi x/L)e^{-n^2\pi^2 kt/L^2} \to 0$
Equilibrates to boundary temperature
Heat escapes through boundaries

**Problem 5: B**
Mode $n$ decays as $e^{-n^2\pi^2 kt/L^2}$
Decay rate proportional to $n^2$
Mode $n = 10$: decays 100× faster than $n = 1$
Physical: rapid oscillations smooth quickly
Long-term: only fundamental mode significant
