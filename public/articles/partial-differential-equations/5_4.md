# Partial Differential Equations Topic 5.4: Energy Methods and Higher Dimensions

Drop a pebble in a pond and watch circular ripples spread outward. Pluck a drumhead and hear it vibrate with a complex mixture of tones. Strike a bell and listen to its distinctive, non-harmonic ring. These phenomena extend wave motion beyond one dimension, governed by multi-dimensional wave equations. The mathematics becomes richer: circular and spherical waves, Bessel functions, and more complex mode shapes. But fundamental principles persist: energy conservation, finite propagation speed, and superposition.

**Energy methods** provide powerful tools for analyzing wave equations. The total energy—kinetic plus potential—is conserved for conservative systems (no damping). This conservation law proves uniqueness: if two solutions have the same initial and boundary conditions, their energies are equal, so they must be identical. Energy arguments also establish stability estimates and long-time behavior without solving explicitly.

This article explores energy conservation for waves and extends to higher dimensions. We'll prove energy conservation, use it for uniqueness, understand circular membranes and Bessel functions, and see how dimensionality affects wave propagation. Energy methods reveal the deep structure underlying wave phenomena across all spatial dimensions.

## Energy in One Dimension

For wave equation $u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx}$, define **energy density**:

$$e(x, t) = \frac{1}{2}[u_t^2 + c^2u_x^2]$$

**Total energy**: $E(t) = \int_a^b e(x, t) \, dx$

### Example 1: Kinetic and Potential Energy

Interpret $u_t^2$ and $c^2u_x^2$ physically.

**Solution:**

**Kinetic energy density**: $\frac{1}{2}u_t^2$
- $u_t$ is velocity of string element
- Kinetic energy proportional to velocity squared

**Potential energy density**: $\frac{1}{2}c^2u_x^2$
- $u_x$ is slope (strain)
- Potential energy from stretching (elastic deformation)
- Factor $c^2 = T/\rho$ relates to tension

**Answer:** $u_t^2$ = kinetic, $c^2u_x^2$ = potential (elastic)

### Example 2: Energy Conservation

Show $\frac{dE}{dt} = 0$ for string with fixed ends.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{dE}{dt} = \int_0^L \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[\frac{1}{2}u_t^2 + \frac{1}{2}c^2u_x^2\right] dx$$

$$= \int_0^L [u_tu_{tt} + c^2u_xu_{xt}] \, dx$$

Using $u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx}$:
$$= \int_0^L [u_t \cdot c^2u_{xx} + c^2u_xu_{xt}] \, dx$$

$$= c^2\int_0^L [u_tu_{xx} + u_xu_{tx}] \, dx$$

$$= c^2\int_0^L \frac{\partial}{\partial x}[u_tu_x] \, dx$$

$$= c^2[u_tu_x]_0^L$$

For fixed ends: $u(0,t) = u(L,t) = 0 \implies u_t(0,t) = u_t(L,t) = 0$

Therefore: $\frac{dE}{dt} = 0$

**Answer:** Energy conserved for fixed-end string

### Example 3: Energy for Normal Mode

For $u_n = A\sin(n\pi x/L)\cos(n\pi ct/L)$, compute total energy.

**Solution:**

$$u_t = -A\frac{n\pi c}{L}\sin(n\pi x/L)\sin(n\pi ct/L)$$

$$u_x = A\frac{n\pi}{L}\cos(n\pi x/L)\cos(n\pi ct/L)$$

$$E = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^L \left[A^2\frac{n^2\pi^2c^2}{L^2}\sin^2(n\pi x/L)\sin^2(n\pi ct/L) + c^2A^2\frac{n^2\pi^2}{L^2}\cos^2(n\pi x/L)\cos^2(n\pi ct/L)\right] dx$$

Using $\int_0^L \sin^2(n\pi x/L) \, dx = \int_0^L \cos^2(n\pi x/L) \, dx = L/2$:

$$E = \frac{A^2n^2\pi^2c^2}{4L}[\sin^2(\omega_n t) + \cos^2(\omega_n t)] = \frac{A^2n^2\pi^2c^2}{4L}$$

**Answer:** $E_n = \frac{A^2n^2\pi^2c^2}{4L}$ (constant in time, proportional to $n^2$)

## Uniqueness via Energy Method

### Example 4: Uniqueness Proof

Prove uniqueness of solution to wave equation with given initial and boundary conditions.

**Solution:**

Suppose $u_1$ and $u_2$ both solve the problem.

Let $w = u_1 - u_2$. Then:
- $w_{tt} = c^2w_{xx}$ (linearity)
- $w(x, 0) = 0$ and $w_t(x, 0) = 0$ (same initial conditions)
- $w(0, t) = w(L, t) = 0$ (same boundary conditions)

Energy of $w$:
$$E_w(0) = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^L [w_t(x, 0)^2 + c^2w_x(x, 0)^2] \, dx = 0$$

Since energy conserved: $E_w(t) = 0$ for all $t$.

Therefore: $w_t = 0$ and $w_x = 0$ everywhere.

So $w$ is constant, and from $w(x, 0) = 0$: $w \equiv 0$.

Thus $u_1 \equiv u_2$.

**Answer:** Uniqueness proven via energy conservation

### Example 5: Continuous Dependence on Data

If initial data change slightly, does solution change slightly?

**Solution:**

Let $u$ and $v$ be solutions with slightly different initial conditions.

$w = u - v$ satisfies homogeneous PDE with initial data $w(x, 0)$ and $w_t(x, 0)$.

Energy:
$$E_w(t) = E_w(0) = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^L [w_t(x, 0)^2 + c^2w_x(x, 0)^2] \, dx$$

If initial data close: $E_w(0)$ small.

Since $E_w$ conserved: $E_w(t)$ stays small for all $t$.

**Answer:** Solution depends continuously on initial data (stable)

## Two-Dimensional Wave Equation

For vibrating membrane (drumhead): $u_{tt} = c^2(u_{xx} + u_{yy}) = c^2\nabla^2u$

### Example 6: Circular Membrane

For circular drum of radius $a$, use **polar coordinates** $(r, \theta)$:

$$u_{tt} = c^2\left[\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial r^2} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial u}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial\theta^2}\right]$$

Separate variables: $u(r, \theta, t) = R(r)\Theta(\theta)T(t)$

**Solution:**

After separation:
- $\Theta'' + m^2\Theta = 0$ (periodicity: $m = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$)
- $T'' + \omega^2T = 0$ (temporal oscillation)
- **Bessel's equation**: $r^2R'' + rR' + (\lambda r^2 - m^2)R = 0$

Solutions: $R(r) = J_m(\sqrt{\lambda}r)$ (Bessel function of first kind)

Boundary condition $u(a, \theta, t) = 0$: $J_m(\sqrt{\lambda}a) = 0$

**Eigenvalues**: $\sqrt{\lambda_{mn}}a = z_{mn}$ (zeros of $J_m$)

**Answer:** Normal modes involve Bessel functions $J_m$

### Example 7: Bessel Function Zeros

For $J_0$ (axisymmetric modes), first few zeros are:
$$z_{01} \approx 2.405, \quad z_{02} \approx 5.520, \quad z_{03} \approx 8.654$$

Find frequencies for drum of radius $a = 0.2$ m with $c = 200$ m/s.

**Solution:**

$$\omega_{0n} = \frac{cz_{0n}}{a}$$

$$f_{01} = \frac{\omega_{01}}{2\pi} = \frac{200 \cdot 2.405}{2\pi \cdot 0.2} \approx 383 \text{ Hz}$$

$$f_{02} = \frac{200 \cdot 5.520}{2\pi \cdot 0.2} \approx 880 \text{ Hz}$$

$$f_{03} = \frac{200 \cdot 8.654}{2\pi \cdot 0.2} \approx 1378 \text{ Hz}$$

**Non-harmonic**: $f_{02}/f_{01} \approx 2.3$ (not integer ratio!)

**Answer:** $f_{01} \approx 383$ Hz, $f_{02} \approx 880$ Hz (non-harmonic overtones)

### Example 8: Nodal Lines

For circular drum, nodal lines are circles (radial nodes) and diameters (angular nodes).

Mode $(m, n)$ has:
- $m$ angular nodes (diameters)
- $n - 1$ circular nodes (interior)

**Solution:**

Mode $(0, 1)$: No nodal lines (fundamental)

Mode $(0, 2)$: One circular nodal line

Mode $(1, 1)$: One diameter (two semicircles oscillate out of phase)

Mode $(2, 1)$: Two diameters (four quadrants)

**Answer:** Nodal patterns more complex than 1D string

## Three-Dimensional Wave Equation

For acoustic waves: $u_{tt} = c^2\nabla^2u$ where $u$ is pressure.

### Example 9: Spherical Waves

In spherical coordinates for radially symmetric solutions:

$$u_{tt} = c^2\left[\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial r^2} + \frac{2}{r}\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}\right]$$

Let $v = ru$:

**Solution:**

$$v_{tt} = c^2v_{rr}$$

This is 1D wave equation!

General solution: $v(r, t) = f(r - ct) + g(r + ct)$

Therefore:
$$u(r, t) = \frac{f(r - ct)}{r} + \frac{g(r + ct)}{r}$$

**Physical meaning**: Spherical wave with amplitude decreasing as $1/r$.

**Answer:** $u = \frac{f(r - ct)}{r}$ (outgoing spherical wave)

### Example 10: Point Source

For point source at origin radiating energy $E$:

$$u(r, t) = \frac{A}{r}\delta(r - ct)$$

Why $1/r$ factor?

**Solution:**

Energy spreads over spherical surface of area $4\pi r^2$.

Intensity (energy per unit area) $\propto 1/r^2$.

Amplitude (pressure) $\propto 1/r$ (since intensity $\propto$ amplitude$^2$).

**Inverse square law** for intensity.

**Answer:** Amplitude decreases as $1/r$ to conserve energy

### Example 11: Huygens' Principle

In 3D, sharp wavefront stays sharp (unlike 2D where wake persists).

**Solution:**

**1D**: Sharp pulse splits but stays sharp.

**2D**: Wavefront followed by decaying tail (ripples continue after main wave).

**3D**: Clean separation—once wavefront passes, silence follows.

**Odd vs. even dimensions**: Different behavior!

**Answer:** 3D has Huygens' principle (clean wavefronts); 2D doesn't

### Example 12: Energy in Higher Dimensions

For 2D membrane with area $A$:

$$E = \iint_A \frac{1}{2}[u_t^2 + c^2(u_x^2 + u_y^2)] \, dA$$

Conservation still holds?

**Solution:**

$$\frac{dE}{dt} = \iint_A [u_tu_{tt} + c^2(u_xu_{xt} + u_yu_{yt})] \, dA$$

Using $u_{tt} = c^2(u_{xx} + u_{yy})$:

$$= c^2\iint_A [u_tu_{xx} + u_tu_{yy} + u_xu_{xt} + u_yu_{yt}] \, dA$$

$$= c^2\iint_A \nabla \cdot (u_t\nabla u) \, dA$$

By divergence theorem:
$$= c^2\oint_{\partial A} u_t \frac{\partial u}{\partial n} \, ds$$

For fixed boundary ($u = 0$): $u_t = 0$, so $\frac{dE}{dt} = 0$.

**Answer:** Energy conserved in all dimensions (for fixed boundaries)

### Example 13: Wave Speed in Different Media

Sound in air: $c \approx 343$ m/s

Sound in water: $c \approx 1480$ m/s

Sound in steel: $c \approx 5960$ m/s

Why variation?

**Solution:**

Wave speed: $c = \sqrt{\frac{\text{stiffness}}{\text{density}}}$

**Air**: Low density, low stiffness → moderate $c$

**Water**: Higher density, but much higher bulk modulus → higher $c$

**Steel**: Very high elastic modulus → very high $c$

**General**: $c = \sqrt{E/\rho}$ where $E$ is elastic modulus.

**Answer:** Stiffer materials and lower density → higher wave speed

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
Energy density: $e = \frac{1}{2}(u_t^2 + c^2u_x^2)$
Kinetic: $u_t^2$ (velocity squared)
Potential: $c^2u_x^2$ (strain squared)
Total: $E(t) = \int_0^L e \, dx$
Conserved for conservative systems

**Problem 2: C**
$\frac{dE}{dt} = c^2[u_tu_x]_0^L$
Fixed ends: $u(0,t) = u(L,t) = 0 \implies u_t = 0$ at ends
Therefore: $\frac{dE}{dt} = 0$
Energy sloshes between kinetic and potential
No dissipation in ideal string

**Problem 3: B**
Circular symmetry → Bessel's equation
$r^2R'' + rR' + (\lambda r^2 - m^2)R = 0$
Solutions: $J_m(\sqrt{\lambda}r)$ (Bessel functions)
Zeros determine eigenvalues
Non-harmonic overtones (unlike string)

**Problem 4: B**
Spherical wave: $u \sim \frac{1}{r}e^{i(kr - \omega t)}$
Energy conserved → spreads over area $4\pi r^2$
Intensity $\propto \frac{1}{r^2}$ (inverse square law)
Amplitude $\propto \frac{1}{r}$ (since intensity $\propto$ amplitude$^2$)
Point source radiation pattern

**Problem 5: B**
Huygens' principle in odd dimensions (1D, 3D)
After wavefront passes, complete silence
No wake or tail
2D (even): persistent oscillations after main wave
Fundamental difference: odd vs. even dimensions
