# Partial Differential Equations Topic 1.1: What are PDEs?

When you touch a hot stove, heat flows from the metal into your hand. The temperature at each point on the stove's surface changes over time as energy diffuses through the material. To describe this process mathematically, you need a function of *multiple variables*—temperature depends on position (x, y, z) and time (t). The rate of temperature change involves **partial derivatives** with respect to each variable, leading to a **partial differential equation** (PDE).

PDEs are the language of continuous phenomena in physics and engineering. While ordinary differential equations (ODEs) describe processes depending on a single variable—like a spring's position over time—PDEs describe how quantities vary across space and time simultaneously. The heat equation governs temperature distribution. The wave equation describes vibrating strings and electromagnetic radiation. Laplace's equation determines electric potential, gravitational fields, and fluid flow.

This article introduces partial differential equations from the ground up. We'll define PDEs precisely, distinguish them from ODEs, classify them by order and linearity, and explore fundamental examples from physics. You'll learn to recognize PDE types, understand their physical origins, and appreciate why PDEs require fundamentally different solution techniques than ODEs.

## Definition and Basic Concepts

A **partial differential equation** is an equation involving a function of two or more independent variables and its partial derivatives.

**General form**: $F(x, y, u, u_x, u_y, u_{xx}, u_{xy}, u_{yy}, \ldots) = 0$

where $u = u(x, y)$ is the unknown function, and subscripts denote partial derivatives: $u_x = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}$, etc.

**Order**: The order of the highest derivative appearing in the equation.

**Linearity**: A PDE is **linear** if it can be written as a linear combination of the unknown function and its derivatives. No products or nonlinear functions of $u$ and its derivatives.

### Example 1: Identifying PDEs

Which of the following are partial differential equations?

(a) $\frac{dy}{dx} + y = x$ 

(b) $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = 0$

(c) $\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = u^2$

**Solution:**

(a) This is an **ODE** (only one independent variable $x$)

(b) This is a **PDE** (two independent variables $x$ and $y$)

(c) This is a **PDE** (partial derivative, though nonlinear in $u$)

**Answer:** (b) and (c) are PDEs

### Example 2: Determining Order

Find the order of each PDE:

(a) $u_x + u_y = xy$

(b) $u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0$

(c) $u_{xxx} + u_t = 0$

**Solution:**

(a) First-order (highest derivative is first)

(b) Second-order (highest derivative is second)

(c) Third-order (highest derivative is $u_{xxx}$)

**Answer:** (a) first-order, (b) second-order, (c) third-order

### Example 3: Linear vs. Nonlinear

Classify each PDE as linear or nonlinear:

(a) $u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0$

(b) $u_t = u_{xx} + u^2$

(c) $u_x + 2u_y = e^x$

**Solution:**

(a) **Linear**: $u$ and derivatives appear linearly (no products, powers, or nonlinear functions)

(b) **Nonlinear**: $u^2$ term makes it nonlinear

(c) **Linear**: $e^x$ doesn't involve $u$, so equation is linear in $u$

**Answer:** (a) and (c) linear; (b) nonlinear

## The Classical PDEs of Physics

Three fundamental PDEs govern most physical phenomena:

### Example 4: The Heat Equation

The **heat equation** (or **diffusion equation**) describes how temperature evolves:

$$u_t = k u_{xx}$$

where $u(x, t)$ is temperature, $t$ is time, $x$ is position, and $k$ is thermal diffusivity.

**Physical meaning**: Temperature changes proportionally to the second spatial derivative (curvature). Heat flows from hot to cold regions, smoothing out temperature variations.

**Answer:** First-order in time, second-order in space; linear; parabolic PDE

### Example 5: The Wave Equation

The **wave equation** describes vibrations and wave propagation:

$$u_{tt} = c^2 u_{xx}$$

where $u(x, t)$ is displacement, and $c$ is wave speed.

**Physical meaning**: Acceleration equals restoring force (proportional to curvature). Models vibrating strings, sound waves, and electromagnetic waves.

**Answer:** Second-order in time and space; linear; hyperbolic PDE

### Example 6: Laplace's Equation

**Laplace's equation** describes steady-state phenomena (no time dependence):

$$u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0$$

or in 3D: $u_{xx} + u_{yy} + u_{zz} = 0$

**Physical meaning**: At equilibrium, the average value of $u$ around any point equals the value at that point. Describes electric potential, gravitational fields, and steady-state heat distribution.

**Answer:** Second-order; linear; elliptic PDE

## Solutions to PDEs

Unlike ODEs, which have general solutions containing arbitrary constants, PDEs have general solutions containing **arbitrary functions**.

### Example 7: Verifying a Solution

Verify that $u(x, y) = x^2 - y^2$ satisfies Laplace's equation $u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0$.

**Solution:**

$$u_x = 2x \implies u_{xx} = 2$$
$$u_y = -2y \implies u_{yy} = -2$$

$$u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 2 + (-2) = 0$$ ✓

**Answer:** Verified; $u = x^2 - y^2$ is a solution

### Example 8: General Solution Form

Show that $u(x, t) = f(x - ct)$ is a solution to the wave equation $u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx}$ for any twice-differentiable function $f$.

**Solution:**

Let $\xi = x - ct$. Then $u = f(\xi)$.

$$u_t = f'(\xi) \cdot (-c) = -cf'(\xi)$$
$$u_{tt} = -c \cdot f''(\xi) \cdot (-c) = c^2f''(\xi)$$

$$u_x = f'(\xi) \cdot 1 = f'(\xi)$$
$$u_{xx} = f''(\xi)$$

$$c^2u_{xx} = c^2f''(\xi) = u_{tt}$$ ✓

**Answer:** $u = f(x - ct)$ satisfies wave equation for any $f$

### Example 9: Product Solutions

Find a solution to $u_{xy} = 0$ of the form $u(x, y) = X(x)Y(y)$.

**Solution:**

$$u = XY$$
$$u_x = X'Y, \quad u_{xy} = X'Y'$$

For $u_{xy} = 0$: $X'Y' = 0$

This requires either $X' = 0$ or $Y' = 0$.

If $X' = 0$: $X = c_1$ (constant)
If $Y' = 0$: $Y = c_2$ (constant)

**Answer:** $u(x, y) = c_1Y(y)$ or $u(x, y) = X(x)c_2$ (separable into functions of one variable)

## Initial and Boundary Conditions

PDEs require additional conditions to determine unique solutions:

- **Initial conditions**: Specify $u$ (and possibly derivatives) at $t = 0$
- **Boundary conditions**: Specify $u$ (or derivatives) at spatial boundaries

### Example 10: Heat Equation with Conditions

For the heat equation $u_t = u_{xx}$ on $0 \leq x \leq L$:

**Initial condition**: $u(x, 0) = f(x)$ (initial temperature distribution)

**Boundary conditions**: $u(0, t) = 0$ and $u(L, t) = 0$ (fixed temperatures at ends)

How many conditions are needed for unique solution?

**Solution:**

- Heat equation: first-order in $t$, second-order in $x$
- Need 1 initial condition (first-order in time)
- Need 2 boundary conditions (second-order in space)

Total: 3 conditions needed

**Answer:** 1 initial + 2 boundary conditions determine unique solution

### Example 11: Wave Equation Conditions

For wave equation $u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx}$:

**Initial conditions**: $u(x, 0) = f(x)$ (initial displacement) and $u_t(x, 0) = g(x)$ (initial velocity)

**Boundary conditions**: $u(0, t) = 0$ and $u(L, t) = 0$ (fixed endpoints)

Why are two initial conditions needed?

**Solution:**

Wave equation is **second-order in time**, so we need:
1. Initial position: $u(x, 0)$
2. Initial velocity: $u_t(x, 0)$

(Compare: ODEs $y'' = f(t, y, y')$ need two initial conditions $y(0)$ and $y'(0)$)

**Answer:** Second-order in time requires position and velocity initially

### Example 12: Poisson's Equation

Poisson's equation with source term: $u_{xx} + u_{yy} = f(x, y)$

This is an **elliptic PDE** (no time dependence). What conditions are needed?

**Solution:**

- No time variable, so no initial conditions needed
- Need boundary conditions on closed curve enclosing region
- One condition per boundary point (Dirichlet: specify $u$, or Neumann: specify $u_n$)

**Answer:** Only boundary conditions (no initial conditions for time-independent problems)

## Physical Interpretation

### Example 13: Temperature Diffusion

A metal rod of length $L$ is initially at temperature $u(x, 0) = \sin(\pi x/L)$. The ends are held at $0°$. The temperature evolves by $u_t = ku_{xx}$.

Qualitatively, what happens as $t \to \infty$?

**Solution:**

- Heat equation smooths out temperature variations
- Ends fixed at $0°$
- No internal heat source ($u_{xx}$ term redistributes existing heat)
- Energy dissipates to boundaries

As $t \to \infty$: $u(x, t) \to 0$ (entire rod reaches equilibrium at boundary temperature)

**Physical interpretation**: Heat flows from hot regions to cold until equilibrium is reached.

**Answer:** $u \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$ (exponential decay to equilibrium)

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
$u_t + uu_x = 0$ (Burgers' equation without viscosity)
Product $u \cdot u_x$ makes it nonlinear
Highest derivative is first-order
Important in fluid dynamics and shock waves

**Problem 2: B**
$u_t = ku_{xx}$ is heat/diffusion equation
First-order in time, second-order in space
Models temperature, concentration, probability
$k$ is diffusivity constant
Parabolic PDE

**Problem 3: C**
Wave equation is second-order in time
Need initial position: $u(x, 0) = f(x)$
Need initial velocity: $u_t(x, 0) = g(x)$
Two conditions required (like ODEs with $y''$)

**Problem 4: B**
PDEs have infinite degrees of freedom
General solution contains arbitrary functions (not just constants)
Example: $u = f(x-ct) + g(x+ct)$ for wave equation
Much richer solution space than ODEs
Boundary/initial conditions determine specific functions

**Problem 5: C**
Laplace's equation: $\nabla^2 u = 0$ (no time variable)
Describes equilibrium, steady-state
Examples: electric potential, fluid flow, steady heat
Solutions are harmonic functions
Elliptic PDE
