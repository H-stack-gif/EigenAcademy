# Partial Differential Equations Topic 3.3: Convergence Theory

A Fourier series is an infinite sum—does it actually converge to the function? For smooth, continuous functions, the series converges beautifully at every point. But what about functions with corners, jumps, or discontinuities? Real-world problems often involve step functions, impulses, or piecewise definitions. Understanding **when and how** Fourier series converge is crucial for applying them correctly to PDEs.

There are different notions of convergence: **pointwise** (convergence at each individual point), **uniform** (convergence everywhere with single error bound), and **mean-square** (convergence in $L^2$ norm). Each has different requirements and implications. The **Dirichlet conditions** provide sufficient (though not necessary) criteria for pointwise convergence, covering most functions encountered in practice.

This article explores convergence rigorously but intuitively. We'll understand pointwise convergence at discontinuities (converges to average), uniform convergence for smooth functions, mean-square convergence (always works), and the practical implications for PDE solutions. This theory ensures that our Fourier series solutions actually represent the physical phenomena we're modeling.

## Pointwise Convergence

### Example 1: Dirichlet Conditions

A function $f$ has convergent Fourier series if:
1. $f$ is periodic
2. $f$ is piecewise continuous
3. $f$ has finitely many maxima/minima in one period
4. $f$ has finite number of discontinuities

**Solution:**

**These conditions are sufficient**, not necessary.

**Examples satisfying conditions:**
- Continuous functions
- Piecewise smooth functions
- Square waves, sawtooth waves
- Functions with jump discontinuities

**Examples NOT satisfying:**
- $f(x) = 1/x$ near $x = 0$ (infinite discontinuity)
- Infinitely oscillating functions

**Answer:** Most physical functions satisfy Dirichlet conditions

### Example 2: Convergence at Continuous Points

If $f$ is continuous at $x_0$, the Fourier series converges to $f(x_0)$.

**Solution:**

For smooth, continuous $f$:
$$\lim_{N \to \infty} S_N(x_0) = f(x_0)$$

where $S_N$ is the $N$-th partial sum.

**Example:** $f(x) = x^2$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$ is continuous everywhere.

Fourier series converges to $x^2$ at every point.

**Answer:** At continuous points, series equals function value

### Example 3: Convergence at Jump Discontinuities

If $f$ has jump discontinuity at $x_0$, series converges to:
$$\frac{f(x_0^-) + f(x_0^+)}{2}$$

(average of left and right limits)

**Solution:**

**Example:** Square wave at $x = 0$:
- $f(0^-) = -1$ (approaching from left)
- $f(0^+) = 1$ (approaching from right)

Fourier series at $x = 0$:
$$S(0) = \frac{-1 + 1}{2} = 0$$

**Physical meaning**: Series "splits the difference" at jumps.

**Answer:** At discontinuities, series converges to midpoint

### Example 4: Rate of Convergence

For square wave: $b_n = \frac{4}{n\pi}$ for odd $n$.

How many terms needed for 1% accuracy?

**Solution:**

Error after $N$ terms:
$$|f(x) - S_N(x)| \approx |b_{N+1}| = \frac{4}{(N+1)\pi}$$

For 1% error at maximum (where $f = 1$):
$$\frac{4}{(N+1)\pi} < 0.01$$

$$N + 1 > \frac{4}{0.01\pi} \approx 127$$

Need about **127 terms** for 1% accuracy.

**Answer:** ~127 terms needed (slow convergence for discontinuous functions)

### Example 5: Smoother Functions Converge Faster

For triangular wave: $a_n = \frac{-4}{n^2\pi}$ for odd $n$.

**Solution:**

Coefficients decay as $1/n^2$ (vs. $1/n$ for square wave).

Error: $|a_N| \approx \frac{4}{N^2\pi}$

For 1% error:
$$\frac{4}{N^2\pi} < 0.01 \implies N > \sqrt{\frac{4}{0.01\pi}} \approx 11$$

**Only 11 terms needed!**

**General rule**: Smoother functions (continuous derivatives) → faster convergence.

**Answer:** Continuous functions converge much faster than discontinuous

## Uniform Convergence

### Example 6: Definition of Uniform Convergence

Series converges **uniformly** if for any $\epsilon > 0$, there exists $N$ such that:
$$|f(x) - S_N(x)| < \epsilon \text{ for all } x$$

(Same $N$ works for all $x$)

**Solution:**

**Pointwise**: Different $N$ for different $x$.

**Uniform**: Single $N$ works everywhere.

**Consequence**: Can interchange limit and integral/derivative.

**Answer:** Uniform convergence means single error bound works everywhere

### Example 7: When Is Convergence Uniform?

Fourier series converges uniformly if:
- $f$ is continuous
- $f'$ is piecewise continuous

**Solution:**

**Theorem**: If $f$ and $f'$ are piecewise continuous, series converges uniformly to $f$.

**Example**: $f(x) = x^2$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$.
- $f$ continuous ✓
- $f' = 2x$ continuous ✓
- Convergence is uniform

**Counterexample**: Square wave.
- $f$ has jumps
- Not uniformly convergent (Gibbs phenomenon at jumps)

**Answer:** Continuous functions with piecewise continuous derivatives converge uniformly

### Example 8: Term-by-Term Differentiation

When can we differentiate Fourier series term by term?

**Solution:**

**Safe if:** $f'$ exists and is piecewise continuous.

**Example**: $f(x) = x$ on $[0, \pi]$.
$$f(x) = 2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\sin(nx)$$

Differentiate:
$$f'(x) = 2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{n+1}\cos(nx)$$

But $f'(x) = 1$ everywhere! Does series equal 1?

**Issue**: At $x = 0, \pi$, series doesn't converge (coefficients don't decay).

**Answer:** Can differentiate term-by-term ONLY if $f'$ is continuous

### Example 9: Term-by-Term Integration

Can always integrate Fourier series term by term.

**Solution:**

$$\int_a^b f(x) \, dx = \int_a^b \left[\frac{a_0}{2} + \sum a_n\cos(nx) + \sum b_n\sin(nx)\right] dx$$

$$= \frac{a_0}{2}(b-a) + \sum a_n \int_a^b \cos(nx) \, dx + \sum b_n \int_a^b \sin(nx) \, dx$$

**Always valid**, even if $f$ discontinuous!

Integration improves convergence.

**Answer:** Integration term-by-term always valid (unlike differentiation)

## Mean-Square Convergence

### Example 10: $L^2$ Norm and Mean-Square Error

**Mean-square error**:
$$E_N = \int_{-L}^{L} |f(x) - S_N(x)|^2 \, dx$$

**Solution:**

Series converges in **mean-square** (or $L^2$) if:
$$\lim_{N \to \infty} E_N = 0$$

**Key result**: Fourier series **always** converges in $L^2$ for square-integrable $f$.

Even if pointwise convergence fails, mean-square convergence works!

**Answer:** Mean-square convergence holds for any square-integrable function

### Example 11: Parseval's Identity

$$\frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} |f(x)|^2 \, dx = \frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (a_n^2 + b_n^2)$$

**Solution:**

**Physical meaning**: Total energy equals sum of energies in each mode.

**Completeness**: Fourier basis is complete in $L^2$.

**Example**: For $f(x) = x$ on $[0, \pi]$:
$$\int_0^{\pi} x^2 \, dx = \frac{\pi^3}{3}$$

From Fourier series: $b_n = \frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n}$

$$\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n^2 = \frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{4}{n^2} = 2\pi \cdot \frac{\pi^2}{6} = \frac{\pi^3}{3}$$ ✓

**Answer:** Parseval's identity: energy conservation in Fourier space

### Example 12: Best Approximation Property

Among all trigonometric polynomials of degree $N$, the $N$-th partial sum of Fourier series minimizes mean-square error.

**Solution:**

$$S_N(x) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{N} [a_n\cos(nx) + b_n\sin(nx)]$$

is the **best $L^2$ approximation** to $f$ using $2N+1$ terms.

**No other choice of coefficients gives smaller** $E_N$.

**Answer:** Fourier coefficients are optimal in mean-square sense

### Example 13: Completeness

Can every square-integrable function be represented by Fourier series?

**Solution:**

**Yes!** Fourier basis $\{1, \cos(nx), \sin(nx)\}$ is **complete** in $L^2[-L, L]$.

**Meaning**: For any $f \in L^2$, can find Fourier coefficients such that:
$$\lim_{N \to \infty} \int |f - S_N|^2 = 0$$

**Analogy**: Like spanning a vector space—can represent any vector as linear combination of basis vectors.

**Answer:** Fourier basis is complete; can represent any $L^2$ function

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
Dirichlet theorem: at discontinuity $x_0$
Series converges to $\frac{f(x_0^-) + f(x_0^+)}{2}$
Average of left and right limits
Example: square wave at $x=0$: $\frac{-1+1}{2} = 0$
Cannot equal both limits simultaneously

**Problem 2: B**
If $f$ continuous and $f'$ piecewise continuous:
Convergence is uniform
Single error bound $|f - S_N| < \epsilon$ works for all $x$
Can interchange limit and integral/derivative
Example: $f(x) = x^2$ converges uniformly

**Problem 3: B**
FALSE - Can differentiate only if $f'$ continuous
Example: $f(x) = |x|$ has Fourier series
But $f'$ discontinuous at $x=0$
Differentiated series doesn't converge to $f'$
Integration always safe; differentiation dangerous

**Problem 4: B**
$\frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} |f(x)|^2 dx = \frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(a_n^2 + b_n^2)$
Energy conservation: total = sum of modal energies
Completeness of Fourier basis
Used to find $\sum 1/n^2 = \pi^2/6$ (Basel problem)

**Problem 5: C**
$L^2$ (mean-square) convergence:
$\lim_{N\to\infty}\int|f - S_N|^2 = 0$
Works for ANY square-integrable function
No continuity required
Weaker than pointwise/uniform convergence
Most general notion
