# Partial Differential Equations Topic 4.1: Derivation and Physical Interpretation

Touch a cold metal spoon that's been sitting in hot coffee—within seconds, the handle warms up. Heat flows from the hot end to the cold end, spreading through the metal until eventually the entire spoon reaches a uniform temperature. This natural process of **heat diffusion** is governed by one of the most fundamental PDEs in mathematical physics: the **heat equation**. Understanding where it comes from and what it means transforms an abstract PDE into a powerful tool for describing thermal phenomena.

The heat equation emerges from two physical principles: **Fourier's law** (heat flows from hot to cold, proportionally to temperature gradient) and **conservation of energy** (heat accumulating in a region equals net heat flowing in). These combine to produce $u_t = k u_{xx}$, a parabolic PDE describing how temperature $u(x,t)$ evolves. The constant $k$ (thermal diffusivity) determines how quickly heat spreads—high for metals, low for insulators.

This article derives the heat equation from first principles and explores its physical meaning. We'll see why heat diffusion smooths out temperature variations, why information propagates infinitely fast (though exponentially weakly), and how boundary conditions model different physical scenarios. Understanding the physics behind the mathematics makes solving the heat equation intuitive rather than mechanical.

## Fourier's Law of Heat Conduction

**Fourier's Law** (1822): Heat flux is proportional to temperature gradient:

$$q = -\kappa \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}$$

where:
- $q$ = heat flux (energy per unit area per unit time)
- $\kappa$ = thermal conductivity (material property)
- $u$ = temperature
- Negative sign: heat flows from hot to cold (down the gradient)

### Example 1: Understanding Fourier's Law

A metal rod has temperature $u(x) = 100 - 2x$ (°C). If $\kappa = 400$ W/(m·K), find heat flux.

**Solution:**

$$u_x = \frac{d}{dx}(100 - 2x) = -2 \text{ K/m}$$

$$q = -\kappa u_x = -400 \cdot (-2) = 800 \text{ W/m}^2$$

**Physical meaning**: Heat flows in positive $x$ direction (from hot to cold) at rate 800 W/m².

**Answer:** $q = 800$ W/m² (rightward)

### Example 2: Zero Flux Condition

If rod is insulated at $x = 0$, what boundary condition applies?

**Solution:**

Insulated means no heat flux through boundary:
$$q = -\kappa u_x = 0$$

Therefore: $u_x(0, t) = 0$ (**Neumann boundary condition**)

**Physical meaning**: Temperature gradient perpendicular to insulated boundary is zero.

**Answer:** $u_x(0, t) = 0$

### Example 3: Steady State

For steady-state (time-independent), $u_t = 0$, so heat equation becomes:

$$0 = \kappa u_{xx}$$

Solve for $u(x)$ on $0 \leq x \leq L$ with $u(0) = T_1$, $u(L) = T_2$.

**Solution:**

$u_{xx} = 0 \implies u_x = c_1 \implies u = c_1 x + c_2$

Boundary conditions:
- $u(0) = c_2 = T_1$
- $u(L) = c_1 L + T_1 = T_2$

So $c_1 = (T_2 - T_1)/L$

**Answer:** $u(x) = T_1 + \frac{T_2 - T_1}{L} x$ (linear temperature profile)

## Derivation of Heat Equation

Consider thin rod along $x$-axis with cross-sectional area $A$ and density $\rho$.

**Energy balance**: Rate of change of heat energy = Net heat flux in

### Example 4: Energy Balance in Segment

For segment $[x, x + \Delta x]$:

Heat energy: $E = c_p \rho A \Delta x \cdot u$ (where $c_p$ = specific heat)

Rate of change: $\frac{\partial E}{\partial t} = c_p \rho A \Delta x \cdot u_t$

Heat in at $x$: $q(x) \cdot A$
Heat out at $x + \Delta x$: $q(x + \Delta x) \cdot A$

Net flux: $A[q(x) - q(x + \Delta x)]$

**Solution:**

$$c_p \rho A \Delta x \cdot u_t = A[q(x) - q(x + \Delta x)]$$

Divide by $A \Delta x$:
$$c_p \rho u_t = -\frac{q(x + \Delta x) - q(x)}{\Delta x}$$

Take limit $\Delta x \to 0$:
$$c_p \rho u_t = -\frac{\partial q}{\partial x}$$

Using Fourier's law $q = -\kappa u_x$:
$$c_p \rho u_t = -\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(-\kappa u_x) = \kappa u_{xx}$$

**Answer:** $c_p \rho u_t = \kappa u_{xx}$

### Example 5: The Heat Equation

Define **thermal diffusivity**: $k = \frac{\kappa}{c_p \rho}$

Then:
$$u_t = k u_{xx}$$

This is the **one-dimensional heat equation**.

What are typical values of $k$?

**Solution:**

**Copper**: $k \approx 1.1 \times 10^{-4}$ m²/s (high—good conductor)
**Water**: $k \approx 1.4 \times 10^{-7}$ m²/s
**Wood**: $k \approx 8 \times 10^{-8}$ m²/s (low—insulator)

Higher $k$ → faster heat diffusion

**Answer:** Metals have large $k$, insulators have small $k$

### Example 6: Dimensions and Units

Verify that $k$ has dimensions of length²/time.

**Solution:**

From $u_t = k u_{xx}$:
$$[k] = \frac{[u_t]}{[u_{xx}]} = \frac{T/\text{time}}{T/\text{length}^2} = \frac{\text{length}^2}{\text{time}}$$

SI units: m²/s

**Physical meaning**: $\sqrt{kt}$ is characteristic diffusion distance in time $t$.

**Answer:** $[k] = L^2/T$ (m²/s)

## Physical Interpretation

The heat equation reveals fundamental properties of diffusion.

### Example 7: Diffusion is Irreversible

Why can't you "un-mix" cream in coffee?

**Solution:**

Heat equation is **irreversible**: 
- Forward in time: smooth initial data → smoother solution
- Backward in time: ill-posed (exponential instability)

Second law of thermodynamics: entropy increases.

Heat equation smooths out gradients, increases entropy.

**Answer:** Diffusion increases entropy; reverse process violates second law

### Example 8: Infinite Propagation Speed

At $t = 0$, temperature is nonzero only at $x = 0$. What happens for $t > 0$?

**Solution:**

Heat equation has **infinite propagation speed**: 
- Any temperature change at origin instantly affects entire domain
- Effects exponentially small at large distances, but nonzero

Contrast with wave equation (finite speed $c$).

**Physical**: microscopic collisions propagate info at molecular speeds, but macroscopic effect diffuses slowly.

**Answer:** Temperature becomes nonzero everywhere instantaneously (though exponentially small far away)

### Example 9: Smoothing Property

Initial temperature $u(x, 0)$ has discontinuity at $x = 0$. Is solution smooth for $t > 0$?

**Solution:**

Yes! Heat equation has **smoothing property**:
- Discontinuous initial data → $C^\infty$ smooth for $t > 0$
- All derivatives exist for $t > 0$

Heat diffusion instantly "rounds off" corners and discontinuities.

**Answer:** Solution is infinitely differentiable for all $t > 0$

## Boundary Conditions

Three main types model different physical scenarios:

### Example 10: Dirichlet Boundary Conditions

**Fixed temperature**: $u(0, t) = T_0$, $u(L, t) = T_L$

When is this appropriate?

**Solution:**

- Ends in contact with large heat reservoirs (e.g., ice baths)
- Reservoir so large that its temperature doesn't change
- Example: rod with ends in boiling water and ice

**Answer:** Ends in thermal contact with constant-temperature reservoirs

### Example 11: Neumann Boundary Conditions

**Insulated ends**: $u_x(0, t) = 0$, $u_x(L, t) = 0$

What happens as $t \to \infty$?

**Solution:**

No heat escapes, so total energy is conserved:
$$\int_0^L u(x, t) \, dx = \text{constant}$$

System approaches uniform temperature (thermal equilibrium):
$$u(x, t) \to u_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{L}\int_0^L u(x, 0) \, dx$$

**Answer:** Approaches average initial temperature uniformly

### Example 12: Robin Boundary Conditions

**Convective cooling** (Newton's law): $-\kappa u_x(0, t) = h[u(0, t) - u_{\text{ambient}}]$

What does this mean physically?

**Solution:**

Heat flux through boundary proportional to temperature difference:
- $h$ = heat transfer coefficient
- $u_{\text{ambient}}$ = surrounding temperature
- Combines conduction (left side) with convection (right side)

Example: rod cooling in air or water

**Answer:** Heat loss proportional to temperature difference (convection)

### Example 13: Initial Condition

Heat equation is first-order in time, so need one initial condition:

$$u(x, 0) = f(x)$$

Why only one (unlike wave equation which needs two)?

**Solution:**

**Heat equation**: $u_t = ku_{xx}$ (first-order in $t$)
- Specify $u$ at $t = 0$
- Evolution determined by PDE

**Wave equation**: $u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx}$ (second-order in $t$)
- Need $u(x, 0)$ (position) and $u_t(x, 0)$ (velocity)

**Answer:** First-order in time requires only initial position, not velocity

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
$q = -\kappa u_x$ with $\kappa > 0$
If $u_x > 0$ (temperature increases with $x$): $q < 0$ (heat flows left)
If $u_x < 0$ (temperature decreases with $x$): $q > 0$ (heat flows right)
Heat flows down temperature gradient (hot → cold)
Fundamental to second law of thermodynamics

**Problem 2: C**
From $u_t = ku_{xx}$: $[k] = [u_t]/[u_{xx}] = (T/t)/(T/L^2) = L^2/t$
SI units: m²/s
Characteristic diffusion distance: $\sqrt{kt}$
Higher $k$ → faster diffusion
Metals: $k \sim 10^{-4}$ m²/s; insulators: $k \sim 10^{-7}$ m²/s

**Problem 3: B**
Insulated: no heat flux $q = 0$
Fourier's law: $q = -\kappa u_x = 0$
Therefore: $u_x = 0$ (Neumann condition)
Temperature gradient perpendicular to boundary is zero
Contrast Dirichlet: $u = \text{const}$ (fixed temperature)

**Problem 4: B**
Heat equation has infinite propagation speed
Localized temperature change instantly affects entire domain
Effects exponentially small at distance, but nonzero
Contrast wave equation (finite speed)
Mathematical: smoothing property makes $u$ analytic for $t > 0$

**Problem 5: C**
Insulated: no heat loss, energy conserved
Total heat: $\int_0^L u \, dx = \text{const}$
Entropy increases until maximum (uniform state)
Equilibrium: $u(x,t) \to u_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{L}\int u(x,0) \, dx$
Rate of approach: exponential with slowest decay mode
