# Partial Differential Equations Topic 6.4: Maximum Principle and Green's Identities

Harmonic functions—solutions to Laplace's equation—possess extraordinary properties that set them apart from solutions to other PDEs. The **maximum principle** states that a harmonic function on a bounded domain achieves its maximum and minimum values on the boundary, never in the interior. This seemingly simple fact has profound implications: it guarantees uniqueness of solutions, provides comparison principles, and reveals the "averaging" nature of harmonic functions.

**Green's identities** are integral formulas relating volume integrals to surface integrals, generalizing integration by parts to multiple dimensions. These identities provide powerful tools for proving uniqueness theorems, deriving integral representations (Green's functions), and establishing energy methods. Together with the maximum principle, they form the foundation for the theory of elliptic PDEs.

This article explores these fundamental properties of harmonic functions. We'll prove the maximum principle, understand its physical meaning (no hot spots in interior), derive Green's identities, use them to prove uniqueness, and discover the mean value property (harmonic function equals its average over any sphere). These results reveal the deep structure underlying Laplace's equation.

## The Maximum Principle

### Example 1: Statement of Maximum Principle

**Theorem**: If $u$ is harmonic on bounded domain $\Omega$ and continuous on $\bar{\Omega} = \Omega \cup \partial\Omega$, then:
$$\max_{\bar{\Omega}} u = \max_{\partial\Omega} u$$

**Solution:**

**In words**: Maximum occurs on boundary, not interior.

**Corollary** (minimum principle): $\min_{\bar{\Omega}} u = \min_{\partial\Omega} u$

**Physical interpretation**:
- Steady-state temperature: hottest point is on boundary
- No interior heat sources can create local maxima
- Heat flows from hot to cold, so interior can't be hottest

**Answer:** Maximum and minimum of harmonic function occur on boundary

### Example 2: Proof Sketch

Suppose maximum occurs at interior point $x_0 \in \Omega$.

**Solution:**

At maximum: $u(x_0) \geq u(x)$ for all $x$ near $x_0$.

Therefore: $u_{xx}(x_0) \leq 0$, $u_{yy}(x_0) \leq 0$ (second derivatives non-positive).

But $u$ harmonic: $u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0$ at $x_0$.

$$u_{xx}(x_0) + u_{yy}(x_0) = 0$$

Both $\leq 0$, sum = 0 → both must equal zero.

This means $u$ is constant in neighborhood of $x_0$ (from calculus).

By connectivity, $u$ constant throughout $\Omega$.

**Conclusion**: If maximum in interior, then $u$ is constant.

**Answer:** Interior maximum implies $u$ is constant

### Example 3: Physical Interpretation

Why can't steady-state temperature have hot spot in interior?

**Solution:**

Suppose $T(x_0) > T(x)$ for all nearby $x$ (interior hot spot).

Heat flows from hot to cold: $\vec{q} = -k\nabla T$

Heat flows **outward** from $x_0$ in all directions.

**Divergence**: $\nabla \cdot \vec{q} = -k\nabla^2 T < 0$ (heat leaving)

But steady state: $\nabla^2 T = 0$ (no sources/sinks).

**Contradiction!**

**Answer:** Heat conservation forbids interior hot spots

### Example 4: Comparison Principle

If $u$ and $v$ harmonic on $\Omega$ with $u \leq v$ on $\partial\Omega$, then $u \leq v$ in $\Omega$.

**Solution:**

Define $w = u - v$.

$w$ is harmonic: $\nabla^2 w = \nabla^2 u - \nabla^2 v = 0 - 0 = 0$

On boundary: $w = u - v \leq 0$

By maximum principle: $\max w$ occurs on boundary.

Therefore: $w \leq 0$ throughout $\Omega$.

Thus: $u \leq v$ in $\Omega$.

**Answer:** Boundary ordering extends to interior

### Example 5: Uniqueness via Maximum Principle

Prove uniqueness for Dirichlet problem.

**Solution:**

Suppose $u_1$ and $u_2$ both solve:
$$\nabla^2 u = 0 \text{ in } \Omega, \quad u = f \text{ on } \partial\Omega$$

Let $w = u_1 - u_2$.

$w$ is harmonic: $\nabla^2 w = 0$

On boundary: $w = f - f = 0$

By maximum principle: $\max w = 0$ and $\min w = 0$

Therefore: $w = 0$ everywhere.

Thus: $u_1 = u_2$ (uniqueness!).

**Answer:** Maximum principle proves uniqueness

## Mean Value Property

### Example 6: Mean Value Theorem

For harmonic $u$, value at center equals average over any sphere/circle:
$$u(x_0) = \frac{1}{|\partial B_r|}\int_{\partial B_r} u \, dS$$

**Solution:**

**2D** (circle of radius $r$ centered at origin):
$$u(0, 0) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} u(r, \theta) \, d\theta$$

**3D** (sphere of radius $r$):
$$u(0, 0, 0) = \frac{1}{4\pi r^2}\int_{\text{sphere}} u \, dS$$

**Physical**: Steady-state temperature at point equals average of surroundings.

**Answer:** Harmonic function equals its average over concentric spheres

### Example 7: Converse

If $u$ satisfies mean value property, is $u$ harmonic?

**Solution:**

**Yes!** Mean value property **characterizes** harmonic functions.

If $u$ continuous and satisfies:
$$u(x_0) = \text{average over every sphere centered at } x_0$$

then $u$ is harmonic.

**Deep result**: Mean value property ↔ Laplace's equation.

**Answer:** Mean value property is equivalent to harmonicity

### Example 8: Proof of Mean Value Property

For 2D, use polar coordinates.

**Solution:**

Harmonic: $u_{rr} + \frac{1}{r}u_r + \frac{1}{r^2}u_{\theta\theta} = 0$

Integrate over $\theta$:
$$\int_0^{2\pi} \left[u_{rr} + \frac{1}{r}u_r + \frac{1}{r^2}u_{\theta\theta}\right] d\theta = 0$$

$$\frac{d}{dr}\left(r\frac{d}{dr}\int_0^{2\pi} u \, d\theta\right) = 0$$

Define: $\phi(r) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} u(r, \theta) \, d\theta$ (average on circle)

$$\frac{d}{dr}\left(r\frac{d\phi}{dr}\right) = 0$$

$$\phi(r) = A + B\ln r$$

Bounded at origin: $\phi(r) = A = \phi(0) = u(0,0)$

**Answer:** Average $\phi(r)$ is constant, equals $u(0,0)$

## Green's Identities

### Example 9: Green's First Identity

For smooth functions $u$ and $v$:
$$\int_{\Omega} (u\nabla^2 v + \nabla u \cdot \nabla v) \, dV = \int_{\partial\Omega} u\frac{\partial v}{\partial n} \, dS$$

**Solution:**

**Derivation**: Apply divergence theorem to $\vec{F} = u\nabla v$:
$$\int_{\Omega} \nabla \cdot (u\nabla v) \, dV = \int_{\partial\Omega} u\nabla v \cdot \hat{n} \, dS$$

$$\int_{\Omega} [\nabla u \cdot \nabla v + u\nabla^2 v] \, dV = \int_{\partial\Omega} u\frac{\partial v}{\partial n} \, dS$$

**Generalization of integration by parts** to multiple dimensions!

**Answer:** Volume integral = surface integral (divergence theorem)

### Example 10: Green's Second Identity

Subtract two applications of first identity:

**Solution:**

Apply first identity twice:
$$\int_{\Omega} (u\nabla^2 v + \nabla u \cdot \nabla v) = \int_{\partial\Omega} u\frac{\partial v}{\partial n}$$
$$\int_{\Omega} (v\nabla^2 u + \nabla v \cdot \nabla u) = \int_{\partial\Omega} v\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}$$

Subtract:
$$\int_{\Omega} (u\nabla^2 v - v\nabla^2 u) \, dV = \int_{\partial\Omega} \left(u\frac{\partial v}{\partial n} - v\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\right) dS$$

**Green's Second Identity** (symmetric in $u$ and $v$).

**Answer:** $\int (u\nabla^2 v - v\nabla^2 u) = \int_{\partial\Omega} (u v_n - v u_n)$

### Example 11: Uniqueness for Neumann Problem

Show solution to $\nabla^2 u = 0$ with $\frac{\partial u}{\partial n} = g$ on $\partial\Omega$ is unique up to constant.

**Solution:**

Suppose $u_1$ and $u_2$ both solve problem.

Let $w = u_1 - u_2$.

$w$ is harmonic, and $\frac{\partial w}{\partial n} = g - g = 0$ on boundary.

Use Green's first identity with $u = v = w$:
$$\int_{\Omega} (w\nabla^2 w + |\nabla w|^2) = \int_{\partial\Omega} w\frac{\partial w}{\partial n}$$

$$\int_{\Omega} |\nabla w|^2 = 0$$ (both terms on left zero, right side zero)

Therefore: $\nabla w = 0$ everywhere.

Thus: $w = \text{constant}$.

**Answer:** Solution unique up to additive constant

### Example 12: Energy Method

Define energy: $E = \frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega} |\nabla u|^2 \, dV$

**Solution:**

For harmonic $u$:
$$E = \frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega} |\nabla u|^2$$

By Green's first identity:
$$\int_{\Omega} |\nabla u|^2 = \int_{\partial\Omega} u\frac{\partial u}{\partial n} - \int_{\Omega} u\nabla^2 u$$

For harmonic $u$: $\nabla^2 u = 0$

$$E = \frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega} u\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}$$

**Energy depends only on boundary values!**

**Minimum energy**: Among all functions with given boundary values, harmonic function minimizes $\int |\nabla u|^2$.

**Answer:** Harmonic function minimizes Dirichlet energy

### Example 13: Representation Formula

For harmonic $u$ in $\Omega$:

**Solution:**

Apply Green's second identity with $v = G(x, y)$ (Green's function):
$$u(x) = \int_{\partial\Omega} \left[G(x,y)\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}(y) - u(y)\frac{\partial G}{\partial n}(x,y)\right] dS(y)$$

where $G$ satisfies:
- $\nabla^2 G(x, y) = \delta(x - y)$ (fundamental solution)
- Appropriate boundary conditions

**Representation formula** expresses $u$ as integral over boundary.

**Green's function method**: Powerful technique for solving PDEs.

**Answer:** Solution represented as boundary integral via Green's function
