# QUANTUM MECHANICS 2 ANSWER KEY

## Multiple Choice Solutions

**1. C** - The first-order transition amplitude in the interaction picture is:
$$c_f^{(1)}(t) = -\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^t \langle f|\hat{V}_I(t')|i\rangle dt' = -\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^t \langle f|\hat{V}(t')|i\rangle e^{i\omega_{fi}t'} dt'$$
where $\omega_{fi} = (E_f - E_i)/\hbar$ is the transition frequency. The exponential factor $e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}$ comes from transforming to the interaction picture and accounts for the energy difference between initial and final states. This integral must be evaluated for specific forms of $\hat{V}(t)$ to obtain the transition amplitude.

**2. B** - Fermi's golden rule states:
$$\Gamma = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}|\langle f|\hat{V}|i\rangle|^2\rho(E_f)$$
where $\rho(E_f)$ is the density of final states at energy $E_f = E_i$ (energy conservation). The factor of $2\pi/\hbar$ (not $\pi/\hbar$) comes from the long-time limit of the transition probability and the integration over the energy-conserving delta function. This formula is valid for transitions to a continuum when $t \gg \hbar/|E_f - E_i|$ and describes the constant rate of irreversible transitions.

**3. B** - Electric dipole selection rules arise from the angular momentum carried by the photon and parity conservation. Since the photon has spin-1 and the dipole operator $\mathbf{r}$ has odd parity, we get:
- $\Delta l = \pm 1$ (orbital angular momentum changes by one unit)
- $\Delta m = 0, \pm 1$ (magnetic quantum number changes by at most one)
- Parity must change: $\pi_f = -\pi_i$

Additionally, for total angular momentum: $\Delta j = 0, \pm 1$ (but $j=0 \not\to j'=0$). These rules dramatically reduce the number of allowed transitions in atomic spectra.

**4. C** - The spontaneous emission coefficient (Einstein A coefficient) is:
$$A = \frac{\omega^3|\mathbf{d}_{fi}|^2}{3\pi\epsilon_0\hbar c^3}$$
showing the crucial $\omega^3$ dependence. This means higher energy transitions (UV, X-ray) occur much faster than lower energy transitions (IR, microwave). This scaling arises from the phase space density of photons and explains why atomic excited states have nanosecond lifetimes while molecular rotational transitions can have millisecond lifetimes. The inverse $\tau = 1/A$ gives the lifetime.

**5. C** - Berry's phase is a **geometric phase** acquired during adiabatic evolution around a closed loop in parameter space:
$$\gamma_n(C) = i\oint_C \langle n(\mathbf{R})|\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}|n(\mathbf{R})\rangle \cdot d\mathbf{R}$$
It is gauge-invariant and depends only on the geometry of the path, not the rate of traversal (unlike dynamical phases which are $\propto \int E \, dt$). Berry's phase is topological in nature and can be expressed as a surface integral of Berry curvature (Stokes theorem). Examples include the $\gamma = -\Omega/2$ phase for spin-1/2 in a rotating magnetic field, where $\Omega$ is the solid angle.

**6. C** - The differential cross section (probability per unit solid angle) is the absolute square of the scattering amplitude:
$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = |f(\theta,\phi)|^2$$
The scattering amplitude $f$ has dimensions of length, so its square has dimensions of area per solid angle. The asymptotic scattered wave has the form $\psi_{sc} \sim f(\theta,\phi)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}$, and the probability flux through a solid angle element gives $d\sigma/d\Omega$. The total cross section is $\sigma_{tot} = \int |f|^2 d\Omega$.

**7. B** - In the Born approximation (first-order perturbation), the scattering amplitude is:
$$f(\theta) = -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int V(\mathbf{r})e^{i\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{r}}d^3r = -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\tilde{V}(\mathbf{q})$$
where $\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{k} - \mathbf{k}'$ is the momentum transfer with $|\mathbf{q}| = 2k\sin(\theta/2)$. This shows that scattering probes the Fourier transform of the potential: high-energy scattering (large $k$) probes short-distance structure (large $\mathbf{q}$). The Born approximation is valid for weak potentials or high energies where the incident wave is only slightly perturbed.

**8. B** - The phase shift $\delta_l$ measures how much the potential shifts the phase of the radial wavefunction relative to the free-particle solution. For no potential, $\delta_l = 0$ and $u_l \sim \sin(kr - l\pi/2)$. With potential, $u_l \sim \sin(kr - l\pi/2 + \delta_l)$. A positive (negative) phase shift indicates the potential is effectively attractive (repulsive) in that partial wave. The magnitude of $\delta_l$ determines the strength of scattering in the $l$-channel, with $\sigma_l \propto \sin^2\delta_l$.

**9. C** - A resonance occurs when $\delta_l(E_r) = \pi/2$ (or more generally $\delta_l = n\pi + \pi/2$). At resonance:
- The partial cross section $\sigma_l = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l$ reaches its maximum value $\frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)$ (unitarity limit)
- The scattering amplitude has maximum imaginary part
- The phase shift passes rapidly through $\pi/2$ as energy varies

Physically, this corresponds to a quasi-bound state: the system temporarily forms a bound state before decaying to the continuum. The width $\Gamma$ of the resonance is inversely related to the lifetime: $\Gamma = \hbar/\tau$.

**10. B** - For identical fermions, the wavefunction must be antisymmetric under particle exchange. The scattering amplitude becomes:
$$f_{total}(\theta) = f(\theta) - f(\pi - \theta)$$
At $\theta = 90°$, we have $f(\pi/2) - f(\pi/2) = 0$, giving zero differential cross section. This is a purely quantum mechanical effect with no classical analog—the indistinguishability and Pauli exclusion principle create destructive interference at $90°$. This "Pauli dip" is observed in electron-electron and proton-proton scattering.

**11. C** - The variational principle (Rayleigh-Ritz) states that for any normalized trial wavefunction:
$$E_{trial} = \langle\psi_{trial}|\hat{H}|\psi_{trial}\rangle \geq E_0$$
where $E_0$ is the true ground state energy. The expectation value of the Hamiltonian provides an **upper bound** on the ground state energy. Equality holds only when $|\psi_{trial}\rangle$ is exactly the ground state. This principle is the basis for variational methods in quantum chemistry and provides a systematic way to improve trial wavefunctions by minimizing $\langle\hat{H}\rangle$.

**12. B** - The WKB (Wentzel-Kramers-Brillouin) approximation is valid when the potential varies slowly on the scale of the de Broglie wavelength:
$$\lambda_{dB} = \frac{2\pi\hbar}{p} \ll L$$
where $L$ is the characteristic length scale of potential variation. Equivalently, $\hbar$ should be "effectively small" or the classical action should be large compared to $\hbar$. When this condition holds, the semi-classical approximation $\psi \sim e^{iS/\hbar}$ is accurate. The approximation breaks down near classical turning points where $p \to 0$.

**13. B** - The WKB tunneling transmission coefficient through a barrier is:
$$T \approx \exp\left[-\frac{2}{\hbar}\int_{x_1}^{x_2}|p(x)|dx\right]$$
where the integral is over the classically forbidden region and $p(x) = \sqrt{2m(V(x) - E)}$ (imaginary momentum). The factor of 2 comes from the exponential decay going in and coming out. This is called the Gamow factor and explains alpha decay, scanning tunneling microscopy, and field emission. The exponential sensitivity to barrier width and height is characteristic of quantum tunneling.

**14. C** - A pure state has $\text{Tr}(\hat{\rho}^2) = 1$. For a pure state $\hat{\rho} = |\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$:
$$\text{Tr}(\hat{\rho}^2) = \text{Tr}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|) = \langle\psi|\psi\rangle\text{Tr}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|) = 1$$
For a mixed state $\hat{\rho} = \sum_i p_i|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|$ with $0 < p_i < 1$:
$$\text{Tr}(\hat{\rho}^2) = \sum_i p_i^2 < \left(\sum_i p_i\right)^2 = 1$$
The purity $\text{Tr}(\hat{\rho}^2)$ ranges from $1/N$ (maximally mixed) to 1 (pure). Note that $\text{Tr}(\hat{\rho}) = 1$ is always true (normalization), not just for pure states.

**15. B** - The Klein-Gordon equation is the relativistic wave equation for **spin-0** particles (scalar bosons):
$$\left(\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2 + \frac{m^2c^2}{\hbar^2}\right)\phi = 0$$
or in covariant form: $(\Box + m^2)\phi = 0$ where $\Box = \partial_\mu\partial^\mu$. It correctly describes pions, kaons, and the Higgs boson. Spin-1/2 particles (electrons, quarks) require the Dirac equation. The Klein-Gordon equation arises from the relativistic energy-momentum relation $E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4$.

**16. C** - The Klein-Gordon equation has the problem that the conserved current gives a probability density:
$$\rho = \frac{i\hbar}{2mc^2}(\phi^*\dot{\phi} - \phi\dot{\phi}^*)$$
which **can be negative** for certain superpositions. This violates the probabilistic interpretation of quantum mechanics. Additionally, both positive and negative energy solutions $E = \pm\sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}$ exist, making single-particle interpretation problematic. The resolution requires second quantization and interpreting the Klein-Gordon equation as a field equation, with negative energy solutions corresponding to antiparticles.

**17. B** - The Dirac equation naturally predicts the electron's g-factor to be exactly **g = 2** (in tree-level QED). The magnetic moment is:
$$\boldsymbol{\mu} = -g\frac{e}{2m}\mathbf{S}$$
where $\mathbf{S}$ is the spin. This was revolutionary because:
- Pauli had postulated $g \approx 2$ empirically
- Dirac equation derived it from first principles
- No ad hoc assumption needed

QED radiative corrections give $g = 2(1 + \alpha/2\pi + \ldots) \approx 2.002319$, which matches experiments to 12 decimal places—one of the most precise predictions in physics.

**18. B** - The Dirac gamma matrices satisfy the **anticommutation relation** (Clifford algebra):
$$\{\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu\} = \gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu + \gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu = 2\eta^{\mu\nu}$$
where $\eta^{\mu\nu} = \text{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$ is the Minkowski metric. This gives:
- $(\gamma^0)^2 = 1$
- $(\gamma^i)^2 = -1$ for $i = 1,2,3$
- $\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu = -\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu$ for $\mu \neq \nu$

These relations ensure the Dirac equation is Lorentz covariant and first-order in time derivatives, avoiding the negative probability problem of Klein-Gordon.

**19. D** - All three interpretations of antiparticles are valid and equivalent:
- **Feynman-Stueckelberg**: Negative energy particle moving backward in time = positive energy antiparticle moving forward
- **Dirac sea**: The vacuum is filled with negative energy electrons; a hole (missing electron) appears as a positron
- **Modern QFT**: Both particles and antiparticles are positive energy excitations of quantum fields

The Dirac sea interpretation, while physically intuitive for fermions, is less fundamental. QFT provides the most complete description where particle-antiparticle pairs are created/annihilated, with CPT symmetry relating them.

**20. C** - Fine structure in hydrogen arises from two relativistic corrections of order $\alpha^2$:
1. **Relativistic kinetic energy**: $\Delta E_{kin} = -\frac{p^4}{8m^3c^2}$ correction to $p^2/2m$
2. **Spin-orbit coupling**: $\Delta E_{SO} = \frac{1}{2m^2c^2}\frac{1}{r}\frac{dV}{dr}\mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{S}$

Together they give the fine structure formula $E_{nj} \sim -\frac{13.6 \text{ eV}}{n^2}[1 + \frac{\alpha^2}{n^2}(\ldots)]$. The Lamb shift is a higher-order QED correction ($\alpha^3$ or smaller) that splits states with the same $j$ but different $l$ (like $2s_{1/2}$ and $2p_{1/2}$), which Dirac theory predicts to be degenerate.

**21. B** - Each mode of the electromagnetic field is quantized as a **harmonic oscillator**:
$$\hat{H} = \sum_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\hbar\omega_k(\hat{a}^\dagger_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k},\lambda} + \frac{1}{2})$$
where $\omega_k = c|\mathbf{k}|$ and $\lambda = 1,2$ labels the two polarization states. The operators $\hat{a}^\dagger$, $\hat{a}$ create and annihilate photons with momentum $\hbar\mathbf{k}$ and energy $\hbar\omega_k$. The zero-point energy $\sum \frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega_k$ per mode leads to vacuum fluctuations and the Casimir effect. This is the foundation of quantum electrodynamics.

**22. B** - A coherent state $|\alpha\rangle$ is defined as an **eigenstate of the annihilation operator**:
$$\hat{a}|\alpha\rangle = \alpha|\alpha\rangle$$
where $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$. Properties:
- Minimum uncertainty: $\Delta E \Delta \phi = 1/2$ (most classical quantum state)
- Poisson photon statistics: $P(n) = e^{-|\alpha|^2}|\alpha|^{2n}/n!$
- Time evolution: $|\alpha(t)\rangle = |e^{-i\omega t}\alpha\rangle$ (classical oscillation)
- Displacement operator: $|\alpha\rangle = \hat{D}(\alpha)|0\rangle$ where $\hat{D}(\alpha) = e^{\alpha\hat{a}^\dagger - \alpha^*\hat{a}}$

Lasers produce coherent states, making them "quantum but classical-like."

**23. C** - The Lamb shift is a QED radiative correction arising from:
1. **Self-energy**: Virtual photon emission and reabsorption by the electron
2. **Vacuum polarization**: Virtual electron-positron pairs screening the nuclear charge

These effects shift energy levels by $\sim \alpha^3 mc^2$, splitting the $2s_{1/2}$ and $2p_{1/2}$ levels in hydrogen by ~1057 MHz (measured by Lamb and Retherford in 1947). This cannot be explained by the Dirac equation alone and was crucial evidence for QED. The Lamb shift is larger for s-states because their wavefunctions penetrate the nucleus more.

**24. B** - In Feynman's path integral formulation, the quantum amplitude to go from $(x_i, t_i)$ to $(x_f, t_f)$ is:
$$K(x_f,t_f;x_i,t_i) = \int \mathcal{D}[x(t)] \exp\left[\frac{i}{\hbar}S[x(t)]\right]$$
where $S = \int L \, dt$ is the classical action and the integral is over **all possible paths** (not just classical ones). In the classical limit $\hbar \to 0$, stationary phase gives dominance to the path that extremizes the action (Euler-Lagrange equations). This formulation is equivalent to the Schrödinger and Heisenberg pictures but provides deep insights into quantum-classical correspondence and is essential for QFT.

**25. B** - The Aharonov-Bohm effect shows that the **gauge potential $\mathbf{A}$ is physically observable**, not just the fields $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$. A charged particle passing through a region where $\mathbf{B} = 0$ but $\mathbf{A} \neq 0$ (e.g., outside a solenoid) acquires a phase:
$$\Delta\phi = \frac{e}{\hbar}\oint \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \frac{e\Phi_B}{\hbar}$$
where $\Phi_B = \int \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$ is the enclosed magnetic flux. This phase is observable in interference experiments, demonstrating that potentials (not just fields) are fundamental in quantum mechanics. This is a topological effect and was confirmed experimentally in the 1960s.

**(b)** Resonant case and Rabi oscillations:

For $\omega \approx \omega_0$, the term with denominator $(\omega_0 - \omega)$ dominates because:

- $\omega_0 - \omega \approx 0$ (small, near-resonant)

$$c_e^{(1)}(t) \approx \frac{V_{eg}}{2\hbar}\frac{e^{i(\omega_0 - \omega)t} - 1}{\omega_0 - \omega}$$

$$= \frac{iV_{eg}}{\hbar\delta}e^{i\delta t/2}\sin(\delta t/2)$$

$$P_{g \to e}(t) = \sin^2(\Omega_R t/2)$$

where the **Rabi frequency** is:
$$\boxed{\Omega_R = \frac{|V_{eg}|}{\hbar}}$$

The system oscillates between ground and excited states with period $T = 2\pi/\Omega_R$.

**(c)** Physical explanation for off-resonant case:

**Physical reasons:**
1. **Energy mismatch**: The photon energy $\hbar\omega$ doesn't match the atomic transition energy $\hbar\omega_0 = E_e - E_g$. Energy conservation cannot be satisfied for a real transition.

3. **Oscillating probability**: The transition probability oscillates in time with frequency $\delta$, but remains small with amplitude $\propto 1/\delta^2$. The atom is "shaken" by the non-resonant field but doesn't efficiently absorb energy.

4. **Adiabatic following**: For very slow changes (small $\omega$), the system adiabatically follows the instantaneous eigenstate without transitions.

The maximum probability occurs at resonance where energy conservation is satisfied and the amplitude builds coherently over time.

**(d)** Fermi's golden rule derivation:

For a continuum of final states with density $\rho(E)$, we need to sum the transition rate over all accessible final states.

Starting from the transition probability to a single state $|f\rangle$:
$$P_{i \to f}(t) = |c_f^{(1)}(t)|^2 \approx \frac{4|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2}\frac{\sin^2[(\omega_{fi} - \omega)t/2]}{(\omega_{fi} - \omega)^2}$$

As $t \to \infty$, the function $\frac{\sin^2(\delta t/2)}{\delta^2}$ becomes sharply peaked at $\delta = 0$ and approaches $(\pi t/2)\delta(\delta)$:
$$\lim_{t \to \infty}\frac{\sin^2(\delta t/2)}{\delta^2} = \frac{\pi t}{2}\delta(\delta)$$

The transition rate to a single state is:
$$\Gamma_f = \frac{dP_{i \to f}}{dt} \approx \frac{2\pi}{\hbar^2}|V_{fi}|^2\delta(E_f - E_i - \hbar\omega)$$

Summing over the continuum of final states:
$$\Gamma_{total} = \int \Gamma_f \rho(E_f) dE_f$$

$$= \int \frac{2\pi}{\hbar^2}|V_{fi}|^2\delta(E_f - E_i - \hbar\omega)\rho(E_f) dE_f$$

The delta function enforces energy conservation $E_f = E_i + \hbar\omega$:

$$\boxed{\Gamma = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}|\langle f|\hat{V}|i\rangle|^2\rho(E_f)\bigg|_{E_f = E_i + \hbar\omega}}$$

**Conditions for validity:**
1. **Long time limit**: $t \gg \hbar/\Delta E$ where $\Delta E$ is the spacing between final states
2. **Weak coupling**: Perturbation theory is valid, $|V_{fi}| \ll E_f - E_i$
3. **Continuum**: Dense final states so the delta function approximation holds
4. **Constant density**: $\rho(E)$ varies slowly near the transition energy
5. **Irreversibility**: Transition to continuum is effectively irreversible (no recurrence)

**(b)** Physical meaning of phase shift:

Far from the scattering center where $V(r) \to 0$, the radial wavefunction approaches:
$$u_l(r) \sim A_l\sin(kr - l\pi/2 + \delta_l)$$

**For a free particle** ($V = 0$ everywhere), the solution is:
$$u_l^{\text{free}}(r) = A_l'\sin(kr - l\pi/2)$$

The **phase shift $\delta_l$** measures how much the potential shifts the phase of the wavefunction relative to the free particle case.

**Physical interpretation:**

1. **No scattering** ($V = 0$): $\delta_l = 0$
   - Wavefunction has free-particle phase

2. **Attractive potential**: $\delta_l > 0$
   - Phase is advanced (wavefunction pulled inward)
   - More time spent near origin
   - Positive phase shift

3. **Repulsive potential**: $\delta_l < 0$
   - Phase is retarded (wavefunction pushed outward)
   - Less time near origin
   - Negative phase shift

4. **Scattering strength**: 
   - $|\delta_l|$ measures interaction strength in $l$-channel
   - Partial cross section $\sigma_l \propto \sin^2\delta_l$
   - Maximum when $\delta_l = \pi/2$ (resonance)

5. **Low energy**: 
   - Only s-wave ($l=0$) contributes
   - Higher $l$ waves suppressed by centrifugal barrier

The phase shift encodes all scattering information for that partial wave and is the fundamental quantity in partial wave analysis.

**(c)** Partial wave amplitude and cross section:

The asymptotic form can be written using complex exponentials:
$$u_l(r) \sim A_l\sin(kr - l\pi/2 + \delta_l) = \frac{A_l}{2i}\left[e^{i(kr - l\pi/2 + \delta_l)} - e^{-i(kr - l\pi/2 + \delta_l)}\right]$$

$$= \frac{A_l e^{i\delta_l}}{2i}\left[e^{i(kr - l\pi/2)} - e^{-2i\delta_l}e^{-i(kr - l\pi/2)}\right]$$

$$= \frac{A_l}{2i}e^{-il\pi/2}\left[e^{2i\delta_l}e^{ikr} - e^{-ikr}\right]$$

$$\psi \sim e^{ikz} + f(\theta)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}$$

$$\sigma_l = \int |f_l P_l|^2 d\Omega = |f_l|^2 \cdot \frac{4\pi}{2l+1}$$

$$= \left|\frac{1}{k}e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l\right|^2 \cdot \frac{4\pi}{2l+1} = \frac{\sin^2\delta_l}{k^2} \cdot \frac{4\pi}{2l+1}$$

$$\boxed{\sigma_l = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l}$$

This shows:
- Maximum at $\delta_l = \pi/2$: $\sigma_l^{\text{max}} = \frac{4\pi(2l+1)}{k^2}$ (unitarity limit)

**(a)** Deriving Klein-Gordon from Dirac equation:

The Dirac equation is:
$$(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - m)\psi = 0$$

To derive the Klein-Gordon equation, act on the left with $(i\gamma^\nu\partial_\nu + m)$:

$$(i\gamma^\nu\partial_\nu + m)(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - m)\psi = 0$$

Expanding:
$$-\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu\partial_\nu\partial_\mu\psi + im\gamma^\nu\partial_\nu\psi - im\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi - m^2\psi = 0$$

The second and third terms cancel (both equal $im\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi$):
$$-\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu\partial_\nu\partial_\mu\psi - m^2\psi = 0$$

Now use the anticommutation relation $\{\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu\} = 2\eta^{\mu\nu}$:
$$\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu = \frac{1}{2}\{\gamma^\nu, \gamma^\mu\} + \frac{1}{2}[\gamma^\nu, \gamma^\mu] = \eta^{\nu\mu} + \frac{1}{2}[\gamma^\nu, \gamma^\mu]$$

When summed with $\partial_\nu\partial_\mu$ (which is symmetric), the commutator term vanishes:
$$\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu\partial_\nu\partial_\mu = \eta^{\nu\mu}\partial_\nu\partial_\mu = \partial^\mu\partial_\mu = \Box$$

Therefore:
$$-\Box\psi - m^2\psi = 0$$

$$\boxed{(\Box + m^2)\psi = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad (\partial_\mu\partial^\mu + m^2)\psi = 0}$$

This is the **Klein-Gordon equation**, showing that any solution of the Dirac equation automatically satisfies Klein-Gordon. However, the converse is not true—Klein-Gordon has more solutions than Dirac (includes both spin-0 and components of spin-1/2).

For $n=2$, possible states are:
- $2s_{1/2}$: $l=0$, $j=1/2$
- $2p_{1/2}$: $l=1$, $j=1/2$  
- $2p_{3/2}$: $l=1$, $j=3/2$

**Energy of $2s_{1/2}$:**
$$E_{2,1/2} = -\frac{13.6}{4}\left[1 + \frac{\alpha^2}{4}\left(\frac{2}{1/2 + 1/2} - \frac{3}{4}\right)\right]$$
$$= -3.4\left[1 + \frac{\alpha^2}{4}\left(2 - 0.75\right)\right] = -3.4\left[1 + \frac{1.25\alpha^2}{4}\right]$$

**Energy of $2p_{1/2}$:**
$$E_{2,1/2} = -\frac{13.6}{4}\left[1 + \frac{\alpha^2}{4}\left(\frac{2}{1/2 + 1/2} - \frac{3}{4}\right)\right]$$

**Same formula!** Both have $j = 1/2$, so:
$$\boxed{\Delta E_{2s_{1/2} - 2p_{1/2}} = 0}$$

**No splitting** according to Dirac equation—they are **degenerate**.

**Why no splitting?**

The Dirac fine structure formula depends only on $n$ and $j$, not on $l$ separately. This is because:
1. Both relativistic kinetic energy and spin-orbit coupling contribute
2. They combine in a way that depends only on total angular momentum $j$
3. This gives "accidental" degeneracy for states with same $n,j$ but different $l$

**What's missing? — The Lamb Shift:**

Experimentally, $2s_{1/2}$ and $2p_{1/2}$ are **not** degenerate:
$$\Delta E_{Lamb} \approx 1057 \text{ MHz} \approx 4.4 \times 10^{-6} \text{ eV}$$

This splitting is the **Lamb shift**, discovered by Lamb and Retherford (1947). It requires **quantum electrodynamics (QED)**:

1. **Vacuum polarization**: Virtual $e^+e^-$ pairs screen nuclear charge differently for $s$ vs $p$ states
2. **Self-energy**: Electron emits and reabsorbs virtual photons, shifting energy levels
3. **Wavefunction at origin**: $s$-states have $\psi(0) \neq 0$, so they're more affected

The Lamb shift was crucial evidence for QED and showed that the vacuum is not empty but filled with quantum fluctuations.

**Scoring:** 3 points
- 1 point: Calculation showing both states have same $j=1/2$, hence same energy
- 1 point: Concluding no splitting in Dirac theory
- 1 point: Explanation that Lamb shift (QED) is needed for observed splitting

**(e)** Feynman propagator and causality:

The Feynman propagator in momentum space is:
$$\tilde{S}_F(p) = \frac{i}{{\not}{p} - m + i\epsilon}$$

**Role of $i\epsilon$ term:**

1. **Pole prescription**: The propagator has poles at $p^0 = \pm E_{\mathbf{p}}$ where $E_{\mathbf{p}} = \sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2 + m^2}$. The $i\epsilon$ term ($\epsilon \to 0^+$) shifts the poles slightly:
   - Positive energy pole: $p^0 = +E_{\mathbf{p}} - i\epsilon$ (below real axis)
   - Negative energy pole: $p^0 = -E_{\mathbf{p}} + i\epsilon$ (above real axis)

2. **Causality**: The $i\epsilon$ prescription ensures the correct causal structure when Fourier transforming to position space:
$$S_F(x-y) = \langle 0|T\psi(x)\bar{\psi}(y)|0\rangle$$

where $T$ is time-ordering:
- For $x^0 > y^0$: particle propagates forward in time
- For $x^0 < y^0$: antiparticle propagates forward in time (= particle backward)

3. **Contour integration**: The $i\epsilon$ determines which contour to use when integrating over $p^0$, ensuring that:
   - Positive energies propagate forward in time
   - Negative energies (antiparticles) propagate backward in time
   - No propagation outside light cone (causality preserved)

**Difference from scalar (Klein-Gordon) propagator:**

For a scalar field:
$$\tilde{D}_F(p) = \frac{i}{p^2 - m^2 + i\epsilon} = \frac{i}{p_\mu p^\mu - m^2 + i\epsilon}$$

Key differences:

1. **Spin structure**: 
   - Dirac: $\tilde{S}_F = \frac{i({\not}{p} + m)}{p^2 - m^2 + i\epsilon}$ (when expanded)
   - Klein-Gordon: $\tilde{D}_F = \frac{i}{p^2 - m^2 + i\epsilon}$ (scalar)

The Dirac propagator contains the spin projector $({\not}{p} + m)$ which projects onto positive energy spinors, carrying spin-1/2 quantum numbers.

2. **Matrix vs scalar**:
   - Dirac: $4 \times 4$ matrix in spinor space
   - Klein-Gordon: scalar (just a number)

3. **Statistics**:
   - Fermion (Dirac): Anticommuting fields, Fermi-Dirac statistics
   - Boson (Klein-Gordon): Commuting fields, Bose-Einstein statistics

Both have the same pole structure and $i\epsilon$ prescription, ensuring causality.

**Scoring:** 3 points
- 1 point: Explanation of $i\epsilon$ pole prescription
- 1 point: Connection to causality and time-ordering
- 1 point: Difference from scalar propagator (spin structure)
