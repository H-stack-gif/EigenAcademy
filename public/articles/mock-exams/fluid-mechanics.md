# FLUID MECHANICS ANSWER KEY

## Multiple Choice Solutions

**1. A** - Hydrostatic equilibrium describes the balance between pressure forces and gravitational forces in a static fluid. The condition $\nabla P = \rho\mathbf{g}$ represents zero net force on fluid elements.

**2. C** - By continuity for incompressible flow: $A_1v_1 = A_2v_2$. Since $A \propto D^2$:
$$v_2 = v_1\frac{A_1}{A_2} = v_1\frac{D_1^2}{D_2^2} = 2\frac{(10)^2}{(5)^2} = 2 \times 4 = 8 \text{ m/s}$$

**3. A** - Vorticity is defined as the curl of the velocity field: $\boldsymbol{\omega} = \nabla \times \mathbf{u}$. It represents twice the local angular velocity of fluid elements.

**4. B** - For incompressible flow ($\rho = \text{constant following fluid particles}$), the continuity equation $\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho\mathbf{u}) = 0$ reduces to $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$ (divergence-free velocity field).

**5. C** - The hydrostatic force per unit width on a vertical dam is:
$$F = \frac{1}{2}\rho g H^2 = \frac{1}{2}(1000)(9.81)(20)^2 = \frac{1}{2}(1000)(9.81)(400) = 1.962 \times 10^6 \approx 2.0 \times 10^6 \text{ N/m}$$

**6. A** - Reynolds number $\text{Re} = \frac{\rho UL}{\mu} = \frac{UL}{\nu}$ represents the ratio of inertial forces ($\rho U^2$) to viscous forces ($\mu U/L$). It determines flow regime (laminar vs. turbulent).

**7. B** - In Stokes flow (Re ≪ 1), inertial terms are negligible and drag is dominated by viscosity. Stokes' law gives $F_D = 6\pi\mu aU$, which is linear in velocity: $F_D \propto U$.

**8. A** - The Navier-Stokes equation for incompressible Newtonian fluid:
$$\rho\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} = -\nabla P + \mu\nabla^2\mathbf{u} + \rho\mathbf{g}$$
balances acceleration with pressure gradient, viscous diffusion, and gravity.

**9. B** - Hagen-Poiseuille flow in circular pipes has parabolic velocity profile:
$$u(r) = U_{max}\left(1 - \frac{r^2}{R^2}\right)$$
This results from solving $\frac{d^2u}{dr^2} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{du}{dr} = \frac{1}{\mu}\frac{dP}{dx}$ with no-slip boundary conditions.

**10. B** - Boundary layer thickness on flat plate: $\delta \sim \sqrt{\frac{\nu x}{U}} \propto \sqrt{x}$ or $\delta \sim \frac{x}{\sqrt{\text{Re}_x}} \propto \sqrt{x}$. This comes from momentum diffusion over distance $x$.

**11. B** - The drag crisis occurs around Re ≈ 3×10⁵ when the boundary layer transitions from laminar to turbulent. Turbulent boundary layer has more momentum, delays separation, reduces wake size, and decreases pressure drag significantly ($C_D$ drops from ~0.5 to ~0.1).

**12. C** - For plane Poiseuille flow (parabolic profile between parallel plates): $u(y) = U_{max}(1 - y^2/h^2)$. Integrating:
$$U_{avg} = \frac{1}{2h}\int_{-h}^{h} U_{max}\left(1 - \frac{y^2}{h^2}\right)dy = \frac{2}{3}U_{max}$$

**13. C** - Reynolds stresses $\tau_{ij}^R = -\rho\overline{u_i'u_j'}$ arise from correlations of turbulent velocity fluctuations. They represent momentum transport by turbulent eddies and appear when time-averaging the nonlinear convective term in Navier-Stokes.

**14. B** - Kolmogorov scale $\eta = (\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}$ represents the smallest eddies in turbulent flow where viscous dissipation dominates. Below this scale, flow is effectively laminar.

**15. B** - The $E(k) \propto k^{-5/3}$ (Kolmogorov) law applies in the inertial subrange where energy cascades from large to small scales without input or dissipation—only inertial transfer occurs.

**16. B** - The logarithmic law of the wall: $u^+ = \frac{1}{\kappa}\ln(y^+) + B$ where $\kappa \approx 0.41$ (von Kármán constant) and $B \approx 5.0$ (smooth wall). Valid for $30 < y^+ < 300$.

**17. A** - Speed of sound: $c = \sqrt{(\partial P/\partial\rho)_s}$. For ideal gas with isentropic relation: $c = \sqrt{\gamma P/\rho} = \sqrt{\gamma RT}$.

**18. C** - From area-velocity relation: $\frac{dA}{A} = (\text{Ma}^2 - 1)\frac{du}{u}$. For Ma > 1 and $du > 0$ (acceleration), need $dA > 0$ (diverging). Counterintuitive result specific to supersonic flow!

**19. D** - Across normal shock: pressure increases dramatically ($P_2/P_1 = 1 + \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}(\text{Ma}_1^2-1)$), entropy increases (irreversible), Ma decreases from >1 to <1, $T_0$ constant (adiabatic), density increases.

**20. B** - Prandtl-Meyer expansion is continuous (expansion fan of Mach waves) and isentropic ($\Delta s = 0$). Unlike compression (shock), expansion can occur reversibly.

**21. B** - Ackeret formula for linearized supersonic thin airfoil: $C_L = \frac{4\alpha}{\sqrt{\text{Ma}^2-1}}$ where $\alpha$ is angle of attack in radians. Valid for small $\alpha$ and moderate supersonic Ma.

**22. D** - Fanno flow (friction, adiabatic) always drives flow toward Ma = 1: subsonic accelerates, supersonic decelerates. Entropy always increases (friction dissipative). $T_0 = \text{const}$ (adiabatic).

**23. C** - Laplace equation $\nabla^2\phi = 0$ for velocity potential represents irrotational ($\nabla \times \mathbf{u} = 0$), incompressible ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$) flow. Classic potential flow theory.

**24. D** - Circulation: $\Gamma = \oint_C \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$ (line integral definition) $= \int_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{u}) \cdot d\mathbf{A} = \int_S \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot d\mathbf{A}$ (by Stokes' theorem, surface integral of vorticity).

**25. B** - Kutta-Joukowski theorem: $L' = \rho_\infty U_\infty \Gamma$ (lift per unit span). Relates lift directly to circulation around airfoil—fundamental result in 2D aerodynamics.

## Free Response Solutions

### FRQ 1 Solutions

**(a)** **Given:** $H = 10$ m, $d = 5$ cm = 0.05 m

**Assumptions:**
- Large tank (velocity at top surface ≈ 0)
- Inviscid flow (can use Bernoulli)
- Steady discharge initially
- Exit pressure = atmospheric pressure

**Apply Bernoulli between surface and exit:**
$$P_{atm} + \rho g H + \frac{1}{2}\rho(0)^2 = P_{atm} + \rho g(0) + \frac{1}{2}\rho v^2$$

$$\rho g H = \frac{1}{2}\rho v^2$$

$$v = \sqrt{2gH} = \sqrt{2(9.81)(10)} = \sqrt{196.2} = 14.0 \text{ m/s}$$

**(b)** **Volume flow rate:**
$$Q = Av = \frac{\pi d^2}{4}v = \frac{\pi(0.05)^2}{4}(14.0)$$

$$Q = \frac{\pi(0.0025)}{4}(14.0) = (1.963 \times 10^{-3})(14.0) = 0.0275 \text{ m}^3/\text{s}$$

**Convert to liters per second:**
$$Q = 0.0275 \times 1000 = 27.5 \text{ L/s}$$

**(c)** **When water level at $h = 4$ m:**
$$v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2(9.81)(4)} = \sqrt{78.48} = 8.86 \text{ m/s}$$

**Comparison:**
$$\frac{v_{new}}{v_0} = \frac{8.86}{14.0} = 0.633 \text{ or } 63.3\%$$

**Change:**
$$\Delta v = 8.86 - 14.0 = -5.14 \text{ m/s}$$

The velocity has decreased by 5.14 m/s (or by 36.7%).

**Physical explanation:**
As the water level drops, the hydrostatic pressure at the hole decreases. By Bernoulli's equation, the exit velocity is proportional to $\sqrt{h}$. With less pressure head, there is less potential energy to convert to kinetic energy, resulting in lower exit velocity. The relationship $v = \sqrt{2gh}$ shows that velocity scales with the square root of height—cutting the height by more than half doesn't reduce velocity proportionally but by factor $\sqrt{h_{new}/h_0} = \sqrt{4/10} \approx 0.63$.

### FRQ 2 Solutions

**(a)** **Reynolds number:**
$$\text{Re} = \frac{VD}{\nu} = \frac{\rho VD}{\mu} = \frac{(1000)(0.5)(0.1)}{10^{-3}}$$

$$\text{Re} = \frac{50}{10^{-3}} = 50,000$$

**Verification:**
For pipe flow, transition occurs at Re ≈ 2300. Since Re = 50,000 > 2300, the flow is actually **turbulent**, not laminar!

**Note**: The problem assumes laminar conditions for the purpose of using Hagen-Poiseuille formula. In reality, this flow would be turbulent. We proceed with the given formulas understanding this discrepancy.

**(b)** **Volume flow rate:**
$$Q = V \times A = 0.5 \times \frac{\pi(0.1)^2}{4} = 0.5 \times 7.854 \times 10^{-3} = 3.927 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}^3/\text{s}$$

**Hagen-Poiseuille equation:**
$$\Delta P = \frac{128\mu LQ}{\pi D^4} = \frac{128(10^{-3})(50)(3.927 \times 10^{-3})}{\pi(0.1)^4}$$

$$\Delta P = \frac{128(10^{-3})(50)(3.927 \times 10^{-3})}{\pi(10^{-4})} = \frac{0.0251}{3.142 \times 10^{-4}} = 79.9 \text{ Pa} \approx 80 \text{ Pa}$$

**(c)** **For Poiseuille flow:**
$$U_{max} = 2V_{avg} = 2(0.5) = 1.0 \text{ m/s}$$

This relationship comes from integrating the parabolic velocity profile.

**(d)** **Wall shear stress using pressure gradient:**
$$\tau_w = \frac{D}{4}\frac{\Delta P}{L} = \frac{0.1}{4}\frac{80}{50} = (0.025)(1.6) = 0.04 \text{ Pa}$$

**Verification using velocity gradient:**
For Poiseuille flow: $\tau_w = \mu\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}\bigg|_{r=R}$

The velocity profile is: $u(r) = U_{max}\left(1 - \frac{r^2}{R^2}\right)$

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial r} = -\frac{2U_{max}r}{R^2}$$

At wall ($r = R$):
$$\tau_w = \mu\frac{2U_{max}}{R} = (10^{-3})\frac{2(1.0)}{0.05} = (10^{-3})(40) = 0.04 \text{ Pa}$$ ✓

**Physical relationship**: The relationship $\tau_w = \frac{D}{4}\frac{\Delta P}{L}$ comes from force balance on a cylindrical fluid element: wall shear force equals pressure force. This is a fundamental result in pipe flow.

### FRQ 3 Solutions

**(a)** At the throat of a choked nozzle, the Mach number is **Ma = 1** (sonic conditions).

**Justification:**
For flow through a converging-diverging nozzle to reach supersonic speeds in the diverging section, the flow must first pass through sonic conditions (Ma = 1) at the minimum area (throat). This is the choking condition. The area-velocity relation $\frac{dA}{A} = (\text{Ma}^2-1)\frac{du}{u}$ shows that Ma = 1 can only occur at $dA = 0$ (throat).

**(b)** **Mass flow rate calculation:**
$$\dot{m} = \frac{A^*P_0}{\sqrt{T_0}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{R}}\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{(\gamma+1)/(2(\gamma-1))}$$

**Calculate the coefficient:**
$$\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{R}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.4}{287}} = \sqrt{4.878 \times 10^{-3}} = 0.0698$$

$$\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{(\gamma+1)/(2(\gamma-1))} = \left(\frac{2}{2.4}\right)^{2.4/(2 \times 0.4)} = (0.833)^{3} = 0.578$$

**Coefficient:**
$$0.0698 \times 0.578 = 0.0404$$

**Mass flow rate:**
$$\dot{m} = \frac{(0.01)(500,000)}{\sqrt{600}} \times 0.0404 = \frac{5000}{24.49} \times 0.0404 = 204.2 \times 0.0404 = 8.25 \text{ kg/s}$$

**(c)** **Given:** $A_e/A^* = 0.025/0.01 = 2.5$

**Area-Mach relation:**
$$\frac{A_e}{A^*} = \frac{1}{\text{Ma}_e}\left[\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}\text{Ma}_e^2\right)\right]^{(\gamma+1)/(2(\gamma-1))}$$

$$2.5 = \frac{1}{\text{Ma}_e}\left[0.833\left(1 + 0.2\text{Ma}_e^2\right)\right]^{3}$$

**This equation has two solutions:**
1. Subsonic solution: Ma < 1
2. Supersonic solution: Ma > 1

**Physical determination:**
Since we have a de Laval nozzle with converging-diverging geometry and sonic conditions at the throat, and the problem asks about properly expanded supersonic flow, we seek the **supersonic solution**.

**Using the given information** that for $A/A^* = 2.5$, $\text{Ma}_e \approx 2.44$:

**Verification:**
$$\frac{A}{A^*} = \frac{1}{2.44}\left[0.833(1 + 0.2 \times 5.95)\right]^3 = \frac{1}{2.44}\left[0.833(2.19)\right]^3$$

$$= \frac{1}{2.44}(1.824)^3 = \frac{1}{2.44}(6.07) = 2.49 \approx 2.5$$ ✓

**Conclusion:**
The flow at the exit is **supersonic** with $\text{Ma}_e = 2.44$. This represents the design condition for a de Laval nozzle where the flow expands isentropically from subsonic at the inlet, through sonic at the throat, to supersonic at the exit.
