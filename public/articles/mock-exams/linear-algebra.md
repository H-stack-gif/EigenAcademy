## Multiple Choice Solutions

**1. A** - $2\mathbf{u} - 3\mathbf{v} = 2\langle 2, -1, 3 \rangle - 3\langle 1, 4, -2 \rangle = \langle 4, -2, 6 \rangle - \langle 3, 12, -6 \rangle = \langle 1, -14, 12 \rangle$

**2. B** - $\langle 3, -2, 1 \rangle \cdot \langle 2, 1, -3 \rangle = 3(2) + (-2)(1) + 1(-3) = 6 - 2 - 3 = 1$

**3. C** - $\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|} = \frac{1}{1 \cdot \sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$, so $\theta = 45°$

**4. B** - Using right-hand rule: $\mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{j} = \mathbf{k} = \langle 0, 0, 1 \rangle$

**5. C** - For $AB$ to be defined, columns of $A$ must equal rows of $B$. $A_{2×3} \cdot B_{3×4}$ works (result is $2×4$)

**6. A** - $\det(A) = 2(4) - 1(3) = 8 - 3 = 5$

**7. C** - $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{-2}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 3/2 & -1/2 \end{bmatrix}$

**8. B** - System has no solution when lines are parallel but not identical. This occurs when $\frac{1}{2} = \frac{2}{k}$ but $\frac{3}{5} \neq \frac{1}{2}$. So $k = 4$.

**9. C** - Row 2 is 2 times row 1, but row 3 is independent. After row reduction, 2 nonzero rows remain. Rank = 2.

**10. A** - Upper triangular matrix has eigenvalues on diagonal: $\lambda = 3, 2$

**11. C** - $A\mathbf{v} = 5\mathbf{v}$, so $A^2\mathbf{v} = A(5\mathbf{v}) = 5A\mathbf{v} = 5(5\mathbf{v}) = 25\mathbf{v}$

**12. B** - Rank-Nullity Theorem: $\text{rank}(A) + \text{nullity}(A) = n$, so nullity $= n - r$

**13. D** - Upper triangular matrices form a vector space (closed under addition and scalar multiplication, contains zero matrix). The others fail: (A) not closed under addition, (B) not closed under multiplication, (C) doesn't contain zero polynomial, (E) doesn't contain zero vector.

**14. B** - $\mathbf{v}_2 = 2\mathbf{v}_1$, so vectors are linearly dependent

**15. C** - All bases for $\mathbb{R}^3$ have exactly 3 vectors (dimension of space)

**16. A** - $\text{proj}_\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v} = \frac{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u}}{|\mathbf{u}|^2}\mathbf{u} = \frac{3}{1}\langle 1, 0 \rangle = \langle 3, 0 \rangle$

**17. C** - A matrix is diagonalizable iff it has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors. (Note: symmetric matrices are always diagonalizable, and distinct eigenvalues guarantee linear independence, but C is the fundamental criterion)

**18. D** - Both are correct: $\det(A - \lambda I) = \det\begin{bmatrix} 2-\lambda & 0 \\ 0 & 3-\lambda \end{bmatrix} = (2-\lambda)(3-\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 6$

**19. D** - $\det(cA) = c^n\det(A)$ for $n \times n$ matrix. $\det(2A) = 2^3 \cdot 2 = 16$

**20. B** - The null space is always a subspace (contains zero vector, closed under addition and scalar multiplication)

**21. A** - Orthogonal matrices satisfy $Q^TQ = I$ (columns are orthonormal)

**22. B** - Trace = sum of diagonal entries = $1 + 5 + 9 = 15$

**23. C** - Orthogonal vectors are linearly independent. Don't span $\mathbb{R}^3$ (need 3 vectors), but are independent.

**24. B** - Gram-Schmidt produces an orthogonal (or orthonormal) basis from a linearly independent set

**25. B** - Similar matrices have same determinant, trace, characteristic polynomial, and eigenvalues

## Free Response Solutions

### FRQ 1 Solutions

**(a)** Characteristic polynomial:
$$\det(A - \lambda I) = \det\begin{bmatrix} 4-\lambda & 2 \\ 1 & 3-\lambda \end{bmatrix} = (4-\lambda)(3-\lambda) - 2 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10$$

**(b)** Eigenvalues from $\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = 0$:
$$(\lambda - 5)(\lambda - 2) = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = 5, \lambda_2 = 2$$

**(c)** For $\lambda = 5$:
$$(A - 5I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \implies \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{0}$$
From $-x + 2y = 0$: $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

For $\lambda = 2$:
$$(A - 2I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \implies \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{0}$$
From $x + y = 0$: $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$

**(d)** Yes, $A$ is diagonalizable (2 distinct eigenvalues).
$$P = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad D = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Verify: $P^{-1} = \frac{1}{-3}\begin{bmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 & 1/3 \\ 1/3 & -2/3 \end{bmatrix}$

**(e)** Using $A = PDP^{-1}$:
$$A^{10} = PD^{10}P^{-1}$$
$$D^{10} = \begin{bmatrix} 5^{10} & 0 \\ 0 & 2^{10} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 9765625 & 0 \\ 0 & 1024 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$A^{10}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = PD^{10}P^{-1}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = PD^{10}\begin{bmatrix} 1/3 \\ 1/3 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$= P\begin{bmatrix} 3255208.33 \\ 341.33 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6510758 \\ 3254867 \end{bmatrix}$$

### FRQ 2 Solutions

**(a)** Form augmented matrix and row reduce:
$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Row operations show $\mathbf{v}_3 = \mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_2$, so set is **linearly dependent**.

**(b)** Basis for $V$: $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\}$ (removing $\mathbf{v}_3$)
$$\dim(V) = 2$$

**(c)** Gram-Schmidt:
$$\mathbf{u}_1 = \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\mathbf{u}_2 = \mathbf{v}_2 - \frac{\mathbf{v}_2 \cdot \mathbf{u}_1}{|\mathbf{u}_1|^2}\mathbf{u}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 \\ -1/2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Orthogonal basis: $\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 \\ -1/2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$

**(d)** Projection onto $V$:
$$\text{proj}_V(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{u}_1}{|\mathbf{u}_1|^2}\mathbf{u}_1 + \frac{\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{u}_2}{|\mathbf{u}_2|^2}\mathbf{u}_2$$

$$\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{u}_1 = 3, \quad |\mathbf{u}_1|^2 = 2$$
$$\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{u}_2 = 5/2, \quad |\mathbf{u}_2|^2 = 3/2$$

$$\text{proj}_V(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{3}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \frac{5/2}{3/2}\begin{bmatrix} 1/2 \\ -1/2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7/3 \\ 2/3 \\ 5/3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

### FRQ 3 Solutions

**(a)** Matrix representation:
$$[T] = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & -1 \\ 3 & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

**(b)** Kernel: Solve $Af(x) = f(0)}$:
$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & -1 \\ 3 & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = f(0)}$$

Row reduce:
$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -7 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$$

From second row: $-7y + 5z = 0 \implies y = \frac{5z}{7}$
From first row: $x = -2y + z = -\frac{10z}{7} + z = -\frac{3z}{7}$

Basis for $\ker(T)$: $\left\{\begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 5 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$, $\dim(\ker(T)) = 1$

**(c)** Image: spanned by columns of $A$
$$\text{Col}(A) = \text{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$$

These are linearly independent (not scalar multiples), so:
Basis for $\text{Im}(T)$: $\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$, $\dim(\text{Im}(T)) = 2$

**(d)** Rank-Nullity Theorem: $\text{rank}(T) + \text{nullity}(T) = \dim(\text{domain})$
$$2 + 1 = 3$$ ✓

**(e)** 
- **One-to-one?** No, because $\ker(T) \neq \{\mathbf{0}\}$ (nullity = 1)
- **Onto?** Yes, because $\text{Im}(T) = \mathbb{R}^2$ (rank = 2 = dimension of codomain)
