## Multiple Choice Solutions

**1. A** - For a reversible adiabatic process, $PV^\gamma = \text{constant}$. Combined with ideal gas law $PV = nRT$, this gives $TV^{\gamma-1} = \text{constant}$.

**2. C** - The entropy of mixing for ideal gases is $\Delta S_{\text{mix}} = -k_B[N_1\ln(N_1/(N_1+N_2)) + N_2\ln(N_2/(N_1+N_2))]$, which is always positive.

**3. A** - From $dF = -SdT - PdV$, we have $S = -(\partial F/\partial T)_V$ and $P = -(\partial F/\partial V)_T$. The Maxwell relation is $(\partial S/\partial V)_T = (\partial P/\partial T)_V$.

**4. A** - The Clausius-Clapeyron equation is $\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{L}{T\Delta V}$ where $L$ is latent heat and $\Delta V$ is the volume change.

**5. C** - For an ideal gas, enthalpy depends only on temperature, so $(\partial T/\partial P)_H = 0$. The Joule-Thomson coefficient is zero.

**6. B** - The canonical partition function is $Z = \sum_i e^{-\beta E_i}$ where $\beta = 1/(k_B T)$.

**7. A** - The Fermi-Dirac distribution is $f(E) = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(E-\mu)} + 1}$ where $\mu$ is the chemical potential.

**8. D** - From kinetic theory, $\lambda = \frac{k_B T}{\sqrt{2}\pi d^2 P}$, so $\lambda \propto 1/P$.

**9. B** - The most probable speed is $v_p = \sqrt{2k_B T/m}$, which is where the Maxwell-Boltzmann speed distribution peaks.

**10. B** - For a monatomic ideal gas with 3 translational degrees of freedom, $C_V = \frac{3}{2}Nk_B$.

**11. B** - Bose-Einstein condensation occurs when the chemical potential approaches the ground state energy (typically taken as zero), causing macroscopic occupation of the ground state.

**12. B** - Planck's distribution is $u(\omega, T) = \frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2 c^3}\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/(k_B T)} - 1}$.

**13. C** - Remarkably, kinetic theory predicts that gas viscosity is independent of pressure because density and mean free path effects cancel.

**14. D** - In a first-order phase transition, the entropy (and volume) are discontinuous, producing latent heat $L = T\Delta S$.

**15. C** - Mean-field theory predicts $\psi \sim (T_c - T)^{1/2}$, corresponding to critical exponent $\beta = 1/2$.

**16. C** - The grand canonical partition function sums over both energy states and particle numbers: $\mathcal{Z} = \sum_{N=0}^\infty \sum_i e^{\beta(\mu N - E_i)}$.

**17. D** - The Stefan-Boltzmann law states $u = \sigma T^4$ where $\sigma$ is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant.

**18. C** - The Debye model predicts $C_V \propto T^3$ at low temperatures (Debye $T^3$ law).

**19. C** - Carnot efficiency is $\eta = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H}$, the maximum possible efficiency for any heat engine.

**20. B** - Fick's first law is $J = -D\frac{\partial n}{\partial x}$ where the negative sign indicates diffusion down the concentration gradient.

**21. A** - The Ising model Hamiltonian with ferromagnetic coupling ($J > 0$) is $H = -J\sum_{\langle i,j \rangle} s_i s_j$ where the sum is over nearest neighbors.

**22. C** - The Van der Waals equation is $\left(P + \frac{an^2}{V^2}\right)(V - nb) = nRT$ where $a$ accounts for attractive forces and $b$ for molecular volume.

**23. C** - For 3D free particles, $g(E) = \frac{V}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt{E}$, so $g(E) \propto E^{1/2}$.

**24. B** - The Gibbs paradox (non-extensive entropy) is resolved by dividing the partition function by $N!$ to account for indistinguishability.

**25. C** - Mean-field theory for the Ising model gives $k_B T_c = Jz$ where $z$ is the coordination number (number of nearest neighbors).

## Free Response Solutions

### FRQ 1 Solutions

**(a)** For the isochoric process 3→1, $V_3 = V_1 = 1.0$ L.

The problem states the cycle is:
- 1→2: Isothermal expansion at $T_H$
- 2→3: Adiabatic expansion
- 3→1: Isochoric cooling

Adiabatic expansion to volume $V_3$" and then "isochoric cooling back to initial state" with $V_3 = V_1$:

This suggests:
- State 1: $(P_1, V_1, T_H)$
- State 2: $(P_2, V_2 = 3V_1, T_H)$ after isothermal expansion
- State 3: $(P_3, V_1, T_C)$ after adiabatic process back to $V_1$

For an adiabatic process from state 2 to state 3, if we go from larger volume to smaller volume, it's adiabatic **compression**, and temperature rises. But the problem says "cooling" in step 3→1, which suggests $T_C > T_H$ at state 3.

Solve assuming adiabatic compression 2→3:

For adiabatic process: $TV^{\gamma-1} = \text{constant}$
$$T_H V_2^{\gamma-1} = T_C V_1^{\gamma-1}$$
$$T_C = T_H \left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right)^{\gamma-1} = 600(3)^{2/3}$$
$$T_C = 600 \times 2.08 = 1248 \text{ K}$$

So $T_C = 1248$ K and $V_3 = V_1 = 1.0$ L.

**(b)** 

**Heat absorbed** (1→2, isothermal):
$$Q_H = nRT_H\ln(V_2/V_1) = (1)(8.314)(600)\ln 3 = 5482 \text{ J}$$

**Heat rejected** (2→3, isochoric):
$$Q_C = nC_V(T_C - T_H) = (1)(\frac{3}{2})(8.314)(289 - 600) = -3879 \text{ J}$$
So $|Q_C| = 3879$ J

**Efficiency**:
$$\eta = 1 - \frac{|Q_C|}{Q_H} = 1 - \frac{3879}{5482} = 0.292 = 29.2\%$$

**Carnot efficiency**:
$$\eta_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H} = 1 - \frac{289}{600} = 0.518 = 51.8\%$$

**Physical explanation**: This cycle is less efficient than Carnot because:
1. The isochoric cooling process rejects heat at varying temperatures from $T_H$ down to $T_C$, whereas Carnot rejects heat isothermally at $T_C$ only
2. Irreversible heat transfer occurs during the constant-volume cooling
3. Not all heat absorption occurs at the highest temperature or rejection at the lowest temperature

### FRQ 2 Solutions

**(a)** Single-particle partition function:
$$Z_1 = e^{-\beta E_0} + e^{-\beta E_1} + e^{-\beta E_2} = 1 + e^{-\beta\epsilon} + e^{-2\beta\epsilon}$$

where $\beta = 1/(k_B T)$.

Average energy:
$$\langle E \rangle = -\frac{1}{Z_1}\frac{\partial Z_1}{\partial \beta} = \frac{\epsilon e^{-\beta\epsilon} + 2\epsilon e^{-2\beta\epsilon}}{1 + e^{-\beta\epsilon} + e^{-2\beta\epsilon}}$$

$$\langle E \rangle = \frac{\epsilon(e^{-\beta\epsilon} + 2e^{-2\beta\epsilon})}{1 + e^{-\beta\epsilon} + e^{-2\beta\epsilon}}$$

**Limiting behavior**:

As $T \to 0$ ($\beta \to \infty$): $e^{-\beta\epsilon} \to 0$
$$\langle E \rangle \to 0$$ 
(All particles in ground state)

As $T \to \infty$ ($\beta \to 0$): $e^{-\beta\epsilon} \to 1$
$$\langle E \rangle \to \frac{\epsilon(1 + 2)}{1 + 1 + 1} = \frac{3\epsilon}{3} = \epsilon$$
(Equal population of all states; average of $0, \epsilon, 2\epsilon$ is $\epsilon$)

**(b)** For $N$ distinguishable particles:
$$Z_N = (Z_1)^N = \left(1 + e^{-\beta\epsilon} + e^{-2\beta\epsilon}\right)^N$$

Total average energy:
$$U = N\langle E \rangle = \frac{N\epsilon(e^{-\beta\epsilon} + 2e^{-2\beta\epsilon})}{1 + e^{-\beta\epsilon} + e^{-2\beta\epsilon}}$$

Heat capacity:
$$C_V = \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} = -k_B\beta^2\frac{\partial U}{\partial \beta}$$

Let $x = \beta\epsilon = \epsilon/(k_B T)$. Then:
$$U = \frac{N\epsilon(e^{-x} + 2e^{-2x})}{1 + e^{-x} + e^{-2x}}$$

Taking the derivative with respect to $T$:
$$C_V = k_B\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\left[\frac{N\epsilon(e^{-x} + 2e^{-2x})}{1 + e^{-x} + e^{-2x}}\right]$$

Using $\frac{\partial x}{\partial T} = -\frac{\epsilon}{k_BT^2} = -\frac{x}{T}$:

After differentiation (applying quotient rule):
$$C_V = Nk_Bx^2 \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{e^{-x} + 2e^{-2x}}{1 + e^{-x} + e^{-2x}}\right]$$

The numerator after differentiation becomes a positive function that peaks at intermediate $x$, showing that $C_V$ has a maximum at some $T_{\max}$ (the Schottky anomaly).

**(c)** High temperature limit ($k_B T \gg \epsilon$, so $x \ll 1$):

Expand: $e^{-x} \approx 1 - x + x^2/2$, $e^{-2x} \approx 1 - 2x + 2x^2$

$$Z_1 \approx 1 + (1-x+x^2/2) + (1-2x+2x^2) = 3 - 3x + \frac{5x^2}{2}$$

$$U \approx N\epsilon \frac{(1-x) + 2(1-2x)}{3-3x} = N\epsilon\frac{3-5x}{3(1-x)} \approx N\epsilon\left(1 - \frac{5x}{3} + \frac{5x}{3}\right) = N\epsilon$$

For heat capacity, keeping next order terms:
$$C_V \approx Nk_B \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\right) \left[N\epsilon - \text{corrections}\right]$$

The corrections vanish as $x \to 0$, so $C_V \to 0$ as $T \to \infty$.

**Physical explanation**:
- **Low $T$**: All particles frozen in ground state; adding energy barely populates excited states → $C_V \to 0$
- **High $T$**: All states equally populated; energy distribution can't change further with temperature → $C_V \to 0$
- **Intermediate $T$**: When $k_BT \sim \epsilon$, thermal energy can significantly redistribute populations → $C_V$ maximum

This is the **Schottky anomaly**: a peak in heat capacity due to thermal population of discrete energy levels.

### FRQ 3 Solutions

**(a)** Mean free path:
$$\lambda = \frac{k_B T}{\sqrt{2}\pi d^2 P} = \frac{(1.381 \times 10^{-23})(300)}{\sqrt{2}\pi(3.7 \times 10^{-10})^2(1.0 \times 10^5)}$$

$$= \frac{4.14 \times 10^{-21}}{(1.414)(3.14)(1.37 \times 10^{-19})(10^5)}$$

$$= \frac{4.14 \times 10^{-21}}{6.09 \times 10^{-14}} = 6.8 \times 10^{-8} \text{ m} = 68 \text{ nm}$$

Mean speed:
$$\langle v \rangle = \sqrt{\frac{8k_B T}{\pi m}} = \sqrt{\frac{8(1.381 \times 10^{-23})(300)}{\pi(28)(1.66 \times 10^{-27})}}$$

$$= \sqrt{\frac{3.31 \times 10^{-20}}{1.46 \times 10^{-25}}} = \sqrt{2.27 \times 10^5} = 476 \text{ m/s}$$

Collision frequency:
$$\nu = \frac{\langle v \rangle}{\lambda} = \frac{476}{6.8 \times 10^{-8}} = 7.0 \times 10^9 \text{ s}^{-1}$$

**Answer**: $\lambda = 68$ nm, $\nu = 7.0 \times 10^9$ collisions/second

**(b)** Diffusion coefficient:
$$D = \frac{1}{3}\lambda\langle v \rangle = \frac{1}{3}(6.8 \times 10^{-8})(476)$$

$$= \frac{1}{3}(3.24 \times 10^{-5}) = 1.08 \times 10^{-5} \text{ m}^2\text{/s}$$

Time to diffuse $L = 1.0$ cm $= 0.01$ m:

From $\langle x^2 \rangle = 2Dt$:
$$t = \frac{L^2}{2D} = \frac{(0.01)^2}{2(1.08 \times 10^{-5})} = \frac{10^{-4}}{2.16 \times 10^{-5}} = 4.6 \text{ s}$$

**Answer**: $D = 1.1 \times 10^{-5}$ m²/s, $t = 4.6$ s

**(c)** Number density:
$$n = \frac{P}{k_B T} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^5}{(1.381 \times 10^{-23})(300)} = 2.41 \times 10^{25} \text{ m}^{-3}$$

Thermal conductivity:
$$\kappa = \frac{1}{3}nC_V\lambda\langle v \rangle$$

where $C_V = \frac{5}{2}k_B = \frac{5}{2}(1.381 \times 10^{-23}) = 3.45 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K per molecule.

$$\kappa = \frac{1}{3}(2.41 \times 10^{25})(3.45 \times 10^{-23})(6.8 \times 10^{-8})(476)$$

$$= \frac{1}{3}(2.41)(3.45)(6.8)(476) \times 10^{-6}$$

$$= \frac{1}{3}(2.71 \times 10^4) \times 10^{-6} = 9.0 \times 10^{-3} \text{ W/(m·K)}$$

So $\kappa_{\text{theory}} \approx 0.009$ W/(m·K), while $\kappa_{\text{exp}} \approx 0.026$ W/(m·K).

**Comparison**: Theory gives about 1/3 of experimental value.

**Discussion**: The discrepancy arises because:
1. **Hard-sphere approximation**: Real molecules have complex interaction potentials, not hard spheres
2. **Internal degrees of freedom**: Rotational modes contribute to energy transport beyond simple translational motion
3. **Numerical factors**: The factor 1/3 is approximate; rigorous kinetic theory gives slightly different coefficients
4. **Temperature dependence**: Real gases have temperature-dependent collision cross sections
5. **Quantum effects**: At room temperature, some quantum effects (rotational quantization) begin to matter

Despite these limitations, kinetic theory correctly predicts the order of magnitude and qualitative behavior, which is remarkable for such a simple model!
