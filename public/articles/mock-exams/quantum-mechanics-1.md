# QUANTUM MECHANICS 1 ANSWER KEY

## Multiple Choice Solutions

**1. C** - The norm $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle$ represents the total probability of finding the particle anywhere in space. For a properly normalized quantum state, this must equal 1, expressing the certainty that the particle exists somewhere. The wavefunction $\psi(x) = \langle x|\psi\rangle$ gives the probability amplitude, and $|\psi(x)|^2$ is the probability density, but the norm is the integral of the probability density over all space.

**2. B** - The canonical commutation relation is $[\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = \hat{x}\hat{p} - \hat{p}\hat{x} = i\hbar$. This is one of the fundamental postulates of quantum mechanics and leads directly to the Heisenberg uncertainty principle. In position representation, $\hat{p} = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}$, and one can verify $[\hat{x}, -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}]f(x) = i\hbar f(x)$ for any function $f(x)$.

**3. B** - Two observables can be simultaneously measured with certainty (i.e., the system can be in a simultaneous eigenstate of both) if and only if the operators commute: $[\hat{A}, \hat{B}] = 0$. Commuting Hermitian operators share a complete set of simultaneous eigenstates. While both must be Hermitian to be observables (option A), this alone doesn't guarantee simultaneous measurability—they must also commute.

**4. B** - The Heisenberg uncertainty principle $\Delta x \Delta p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}$ represents a fundamental limit on the precision with which complementary variables can be simultaneously known. This is not due to measurement imperfections (option D) but is intrinsic to the quantum mechanical description of nature. It reflects the wave-particle duality and the non-commuting nature of position and momentum operators.

**5. B** - For a stationary state (energy eigenstate), the time evolution is $|\psi_n(t)\rangle = e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|\psi_n\rangle = e^{-i\omega_n t}|\psi_n\rangle$ where $\omega_n = E_n/\hbar$. The negative sign in the exponent comes from the time-dependent Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi\rangle = \hat{H}|\psi\rangle$. This phase factor doesn't affect probabilities since $|\langle\phi|\psi_n(t)\rangle|^2 = |\langle\phi|\psi_n\rangle|^2$.

**6. B** - In the Heisenberg picture, operators evolve according to $\hat{A}_H(t) = e^{i\hat{H}t/\hbar}\hat{A}_Se^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}$ while states remain constant: $|\psi_H\rangle = |\psi_S(0)\rangle$. This is in contrast to the Schrödinger picture where states evolve and operators are time-independent. The two pictures are equivalent and give the same expectation values: $\langle\psi_S(t)|\hat{A}_S|\psi_S(t)\rangle = \langle\psi_H|\hat{A}_H(t)|\psi_H\rangle$.

**7. B** - For a particle in an infinite square well of width $L$, the boundary conditions $\psi(0) = \psi(L) = 0$ lead to wavefunctions $\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin(\frac{n\pi x}{L})$ and energies $E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$. The quadratic scaling $E_n \propto n^2$ means the energy spacing increases linearly with $n$: $E_{n+1} - E_n \propto 2n + 1$.

**8. B** - The ground state ($n=1$) energy is $E_1 = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$. This is the zero-point energy—the minimum possible energy due to confinement. It can never be zero because the uncertainty principle requires $\Delta x \Delta p \geq \hbar/2$, and confining a particle (reducing $\Delta x$) necessitates a minimum kinetic energy. This zero-point energy is a purely quantum mechanical effect.

**9. C** - The equally spaced energy levels $E_n = \hbar\omega(n + \frac{1}{2})$ give a constant spacing $\Delta E = E_{n+1} - E_n = \hbar\omega$ independent of $n$. This is a unique property of the harmonic oscillator potential and makes it exactly solvable. This equal spacing allows transitions between adjacent levels to all have the same frequency $\omega$, which is important in quantum optics and vibrational spectroscopy.

**10. C** - The creation operator raises the quantum number: $\hat{a}_+|n\rangle = \sqrt{n+1}|n+1\rangle$. The factor $\sqrt{n+1}$ comes from the normalization of states. Similarly, the annihilation operator lowers it: $\hat{a}_-|n\rangle = \sqrt{n}|n-1\rangle$. These operators are called ladder operators because they "climb" up or down the energy ladder in discrete steps.

**11. C** - A free particle has Hamiltonian $\hat{H} = \frac{\hat{p}^2}{2m}$ with no potential. The eigenvalues are $E = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} = \frac{p^2}{2m}$ for any $k \in \mathbb{R}$, giving a continuous spectrum bounded below by $E = 0$. The eigenstates are plane waves $\psi_k(x) = Ae^{ikx}$ which are not normalizable in the usual sense, requiring delta function normalization.

**12. B** - Quantum tunneling is the phenomenon where particles have non-zero probability amplitude (and thus finite probability density) in regions where $E < V(x)$, which are classically forbidden. The wavefunction exponentially decays in the barrier: $\psi \sim e^{-\kappa x}$ where $\kappa = \sqrt{2m(V-E)}/\hbar$. This enables phenomena like alpha decay, STM imaging, and tunnel diodes.

**13. A** - For a rectangular barrier with $E < V_0$, the transmission coefficient is approximately $T \approx e^{-2\kappa a}$ where $\kappa = \sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar$ is the inverse penetration depth. The factor of 2 comes from traversing the barrier twice (in and out). Wider and higher barriers (larger $\kappa a$) result in exponentially suppressed transmission. This formula is exact in the WKB approximation for thick barriers.

**14. C** - For a central potential $V(r)$ (depending only on $r$, not $\theta$ or $\phi$), the angular part of the wavefunction is given by spherical harmonics $Y_l^m(\theta,\phi)$, which are eigenfunctions of $\hat{L}^2$ and $\hat{L}_z$. Hermite polynomials appear in the harmonic oscillator, Laguerre in the radial hydrogen wavefunction, and Bessel functions in cylindrical coordinates. Spherical harmonics are built from associated Legendre polynomials.

**15. A** - The orbital angular momentum quantum number $l$ takes non-negative integer values: $l = 0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ (corresponding to $s, p, d, f, \ldots$ orbitals). Half-integer values (option D) apply to spin angular momentum for fermions like electrons ($s = 1/2$), but orbital angular momentum is always integral. This follows from the requirement that wavefunctions be single-valued under $2\pi$ rotations.

**16. C** - The eigenvalue equation is $\hat{L}^2|l,m\rangle = \hbar^2 l(l+1)|l,m\rangle$. The magnitude of angular momentum is $L = \hbar\sqrt{l(l+1)}$, which is always larger than $\hbar l$. This "extra" $\hbar$ comes from the fact that only one component (conventionally $L_z = \hbar m$) can be specified, while the other components contribute to the total magnitude via uncertainty.

**17. C** - For a given orbital quantum number $l$, the magnetic quantum number $m$ takes $(2l+1)$ values: $m = -l, -l+1, \ldots, 0, \ldots, l-1, l$ in integer steps. These correspond to the different orientations of the angular momentum vector, with $m$ giving the $z$-component: $L_z = \hbar m$. The $(2l+1)$-fold degeneracy reflects rotational symmetry.

**18. D** - The hydrogen ground state ($n=1$) has energy $E_1 = -\frac{me^4}{32\pi^2\epsilon_0^2\hbar^2} = -13.6$ eV (the Rydberg energy). The negative sign indicates a bound state—energy must be added to ionize the atom. This energy arises from the balance between kinetic energy ($\sim +13.6$ eV) and potential energy ($\sim -27.2$ eV) with the virial theorem giving $\langle T \rangle = -\langle V \rangle/2$.

**19. C** - The hydrogen energy levels exhibit accidental degeneracy: $E_n = -\frac{13.6 \text{ eV}}{n^2}$ depends only on the principal quantum number $n$, not on $l$ or $m$. This is special to the Coulomb ($1/r$) potential and is related to a hidden SO(4) symmetry. Each level has degeneracy $\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(2l+1) = n^2$ (ignoring spin). When relativistic corrections are included (fine structure), the $l$-degeneracy is partially lifted.

**20. B** - The Pauli matrices satisfy the anticommutation relation $\{\sigma_i, \sigma_j\} = \sigma_i\sigma_j + \sigma_j\sigma_i = 2\delta_{ij}$. For $i = j$, this gives $\sigma_i^2 = I$ (each Pauli matrix squares to the identity). For $i \neq j$, this gives $\sigma_i\sigma_j = -\sigma_j\sigma_i$ (anticommutativity). They also satisfy commutation relations: $[\sigma_i, \sigma_j] = 2i\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k$, which defines the Lie algebra of SU(2).

**21. C** - The probability is $P(+\hbar/2) = |\langle +|\psi\rangle|^2 = |\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|^2 = \frac{1}{2}$. Since the state is an equal superposition of $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$, there's a 50% chance of measuring either outcome. If measured along a different axis (say $x$), the probabilities would differ. This state is actually an eigenstate of $\hat{S}_x$ with eigenvalue $+\hbar/2$.

**22. C** - Combining two spin-1/2 particles: $s_1 = 1/2 \otimes s_2 = 1/2$ gives total spin $s = 0$ (singlet) and $s = 1$ (triplet). The triplet has three states ($m_s = -1, 0, +1$) while the singlet has one ($m_s = 0$). This follows from the Clebsch-Gordan decomposition: $|1/2 - 1/2| \leq s \leq 1/2 + 1/2$ with $s$ taking integer values since the sum of half-integers is an integer. The dimension check: $(2 \cdot 1/2 + 1)^2 = 4 = 3 + 1$.

**23. B** - The Pauli exclusion principle states that no two identical fermions can occupy the same quantum state. This follows from the antisymmetry requirement for fermionic wavefunctions: $\psi(1,2) = -\psi(2,1)$. If both particles were in the same state, $\psi = -\psi \Rightarrow \psi = 0$. This principle is responsible for the structure of the periodic table, the stability of matter, and degeneracy pressure in white dwarfs.

**24. A** - In first-order non-degenerate perturbation theory, the energy correction is $E_n^{(1)} = \langle\psi_n^{(0)}|\hat{V}|\psi_n^{(0)}\rangle$—the expectation value of the perturbation in the unperturbed state. Option B gives the second-order correction $E_n^{(2)}$. The formula shows that to first order, the energy shift is just the average perturbation energy, without mixing with other states.

**25. B** - The fine structure constant $\alpha = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0\hbar c} \approx \frac{1}{137}$ is the dimensionless coupling constant for electromagnetic interactions. It characterizes the strength of the electron-photon interaction and appears in fine structure corrections to hydrogen ($\sim \alpha^2 \times 13.6$ eV $\sim 10^{-5}$ eV), the anomalous magnetic moment of the electron, and QED calculations. Its smallness makes perturbative QED extremely accurate.

### FRQ 1 Solutions

**(a)** Time-independent Schrödinger equation and solution:

Inside the well ($0 < x < L$), $V(x) = 0$, so the TISE is:
$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = E\psi$$

Rearranging:
$$\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = -k^2\psi \quad \text{where} \quad k = \sqrt{\frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}}$$

General solution:
$$\psi(x) = A\sin(kx) + B\cos(kx)$$

**Boundary conditions:**
- At $x = 0$: $\psi(0) = 0$ (infinite potential wall)
- At $x = L$: $\psi(L) = 0$ (infinite potential wall)

Applying $\psi(0) = 0$: $B = 0$

Applying $\psi(L) = 0$: $A\sin(kL) = 0$

For non-trivial solution ($A \neq 0$): $\sin(kL) = 0 \Rightarrow kL = n\pi$ where $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$

Therefore: $k_n = \frac{n\pi}{L}$

**Energy eigenvalues:**
$$E_n = \frac{\hbar^2k_n^2}{2m} = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$$

**Normalized eigenfunctions:**
$$\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$$

(Normalization: $\int_0^L |\psi_n|^2 dx = 1$)

**Scoring:** 6 points
- 1 point: Correct TISE inside well
- 1 point: Both boundary conditions stated
- 2 points: Correct derivation leading to quantization condition
- 1 point: Correct energy formula $E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$
- 1 point: Normalized wavefunctions with correct form

---

**(b)** Finding normalization constant:

The state at $t=0$ is:
$$\psi(x,0) = A[\psi_1(x) + 2\psi_2(x)]$$

Normalization requires:
$$\int_0^L |\psi(x,0)|^2 dx = 1$$

$$\int_0^L |A|^2|\psi_1 + 2\psi_2|^2 dx = 1$$

Expanding:
$$|A|^2\int_0^L [\psi_1^2 + 4\psi_1\psi_2 + 4\psi_2^2] dx = 1$$

Using orthonormality $\int_0^L \psi_n\psi_m dx = \delta_{nm}$:
$$|A|^2[1 + 0 + 4] = 1$$

$$|A|^2 = \frac{1}{5}$$

Therefore: $A = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}$ (taking the positive real root)

**Scoring:** 2 points
- 1 point: Setting up normalization integral correctly
- 1 point: Correct use of orthonormality and final answer $A = 1/\sqrt{5}$

**(c)** Time-dependent wavefunction:

Each energy eigenstate evolves with phase factor $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$:

$$\psi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\left[\psi_1(x)e^{-iE_1t/\hbar} + 2\psi_2(x)e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}\right]$$

With $E_1 = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$ and $E_2 = \frac{4\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$:

$$\psi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\left[\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right)e^{-i\pi^2\hbar t/(2mL^2)} + 2\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi x}{L}\right)e^{-i4\pi^2\hbar t/(2mL^2)}\right]$$

Or more compactly:
$$\psi(x,t) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{5L}}\left[\sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right)e^{-i\omega_1 t} + 2\sin\left(\frac{2\pi x}{L}\right)e^{-i\omega_2 t}\right]$$

where $\omega_n = E_n/\hbar = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar}{2mL^2}$

**Scoring:** 2 points
- 1 point: Correct time evolution factors $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$ for each state
- 1 point: Complete expression with both spatial and temporal parts

**(d)** Expectation value of energy:

The expectation value is:
$$\langle\hat{H}\rangle = \langle\psi(t)|\hat{H}|\psi(t)\rangle$$

Since $\hat{H}\psi_n = E_n\psi_n$ and the states are orthonormal:

$$\langle\hat{H}\rangle = \frac{1}{5}\left[|1|^2 E_1 + |2|^2 E_2\right] = \frac{1}{5}[E_1 + 4E_2]$$

**(a)** Deriving the uncertainty relation:

Given: $[\hat{A}, \hat{B}] = i\hat{C}$ where $\hat{C}$ is Hermitian.

The general uncertainty relation states:
$$\Delta A \Delta B \geq \frac{1}{2}|\langle[\hat{A}, \hat{B}]\rangle|$$

Substituting the commutator:
$$\Delta A \Delta B \geq \frac{1}{2}|\langle i\hat{C}\rangle|$$

$$= \frac{1}{2}|i||\langle\hat{C}\rangle|$$

$$= \frac{1}{2}|\langle\hat{C}\rangle|$$

Therefore: $$\boxed{\Delta A \Delta B \geq \frac{1}{2}|\langle\hat{C}\rangle|}$$

**(b)** Verifying the canonical commutation relation:

Given:
$$\hat{x} = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}(\hat{a}_+ + \hat{a}_-), \quad \hat{p} = i\sqrt{\frac{m\hbar\omega}{2}}(\hat{a}_+ - \hat{a}_-)$$

with $[\hat{a}_-, \hat{a}_+] = 1$.

Computing the commutator:
$$[\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = \left[\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}(\hat{a}_+ + \hat{a}_-), \, i\sqrt{\frac{m\hbar\omega}{2}}(\hat{a}_+ - \hat{a}_-)\right]$$

$$= i\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{m\hbar\omega}{2}} \, [(\hat{a}_+ + \hat{a}_-), (\hat{a}_+ - \hat{a}_-)]$$

$$= i\frac{\hbar}{2}[(\hat{a}_+ + \hat{a}_-), (\hat{a}_+ - \hat{a}_-)]$$

Expanding the commutator:
$$[(\hat{a}_+ + \hat{a}_-), (\hat{a}_+ - \hat{a}_-)] = [\hat{a}_+, \hat{a}_+] - [\hat{a}_+, \hat{a}_-] + [\hat{a}_-, \hat{a}_+] - [\hat{a}_-, \hat{a}_-]$$

Using $[\hat{a}_+, \hat{a}_+] = [\hat{a}_-, \hat{a}_-] = 0$ and $[\hat{a}_+, \hat{a}_-] = -[\hat{a}_-, \hat{a}_+] = -1$:

$$= 0 - (-1) + 1 - 0 = 2$$

Therefore:
$$[\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = i\frac{\hbar}{2} \cdot 2 = i\hbar$$ ✓

**Scoring:** 3 points
- 1 point: Setting up the commutator calculation correctly
- 1 point: Correctly expanding and using $[\hat{a}_-, \hat{a}_+] = 1$
- 1 point: Final answer $[\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = i\hbar$

**(c)** Calculate $\langle\hat{x}\rangle$ for state $|n\rangle$:

$$\langle\hat{x}\rangle = \langle n|\hat{x}|n\rangle = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\langle n|(\hat{a}_+ + \hat{a}_-)|n\rangle$$

$$= \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}[\langle n|\hat{a}_+|n\rangle + \langle n|\hat{a}_-|n\rangle]$$

Using:
- $\hat{a}_+|n\rangle = \sqrt{n+1}|n+1\rangle$
- $\hat{a}_-|n\rangle = \sqrt{n}|n-1\rangle$

We get:
$$= \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}[\sqrt{n+1}\langle n|n+1\rangle + \sqrt{n}\langle n|n-1\rangle]$$

By orthogonality of energy eigenstates: $\langle n|n+1\rangle = \langle n|n-1\rangle = 0$

Therefore: $$\boxed{\langle\hat{x}\rangle = 0}$$

**Physical interpretation:** The oscillator's mean position is at the equilibrium point (origin) for all energy eigenstates.

**(d)** Calculate $\langle\hat{x}^2\rangle$ for state $|n\rangle$:

$$\hat{x}^2 = \frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}(\hat{a}_+ + \hat{a}_-)^2$$

$$= \frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}(\hat{a}_+^2 + \hat{a}_+\hat{a}_- + \hat{a}_-\hat{a}_+ + \hat{a}_-^2)$$

Therefore:
$$\langle\hat{x}^2\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}\langle n|(\hat{a}_+^2 + \hat{a}_+\hat{a}_- + \hat{a}_-\hat{a}_+ + \hat{a}_-^2)|n\rangle$$

Consider each term:

1) $\langle n|\hat{a}_+^2|n\rangle = \sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)}\langle n|n+2\rangle = 0$ (orthogonality)

2) $\langle n|\hat{a}_+\hat{a}_-|n\rangle = \langle n|\hat{a}_+(\sqrt{n}|n-1\rangle) = \sqrt{n}\langle n|\hat{a}_+|n-1\rangle$
   $= \sqrt{n} \cdot \sqrt{n}\langle n|n\rangle = n$

3) $\langle n|\hat{a}_-\hat{a}_+|n\rangle = \langle n|\hat{a}_-(\sqrt{n+1}|n+1\rangle) = \sqrt{n+1}\langle n|\hat{a}_-|n+1\rangle$
   $= \sqrt{n+1} \cdot \sqrt{n+1}\langle n|n\rangle = n+1$

4) $\langle n|\hat{a}_-^2|n\rangle = \sqrt{n(n-1)}\langle n|n-2\rangle = 0$ (orthogonality)

Therefore:
$$\langle\hat{x}^2\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}[0 + n + (n+1) + 0] = \frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}(2n + 1)$$

$$\boxed{\langle\hat{x}^2\rangle = \frac{\hbar(2n+1)}{2m\omega}}$$

**(e)** Uncertainty product verification:

From part (c): $\langle\hat{x}\rangle = 0$

From part (d): $\langle\hat{x}^2\rangle = \frac{\hbar(2n+1)}{2m\omega}$

Therefore:
$$(\Delta x)^2 = \langle\hat{x}^2\rangle - \langle\hat{x}\rangle^2 = \frac{\hbar(2n+1)}{2m\omega}$$

$$\Delta x = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar(2n+1)}{2m\omega}}$$

Given: $\langle\hat{p}^2\rangle = m\hbar\omega(n + \frac{1}{2})$ and by symmetry $\langle\hat{p}\rangle = 0$:

$$(\Delta p)^2 = \langle\hat{p}^2\rangle = m\hbar\omega(n + \frac{1}{2}) = \frac{m\hbar\omega(2n+1)}{2}$$

**Verification:**

$$\Delta x \Delta p = \frac{\hbar(2n+1)}{2} \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}$$ ✓

The uncertainty principle is satisfied! For the ground state ($n=0$), we have $\Delta x \Delta p = \hbar/2$, which is the minimum uncertainty (equality inthe Heisenberg relation). For excited states ($n > 0$), the uncertainty product is larger.

### FRQ 3 Solutions

**(a)** Time-independent Schrödinger equation and separation:

The TISE for hydrogen in spherical coordinates is:
$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi - \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}\psi = E\psi$$

where the Laplacian in spherical coordinates is:
$$\nabla^2 = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^2\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}$$

**Separation of variables:** Assume $\psi(r,\theta,\phi) = R(r)Y(\theta,\phi)$

Substituting into the TISE and dividing by $RY$:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left[\frac{1}{R}\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2Y}\left(\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial Y}{\partial\theta}\right) + \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial\phi^2}\right)\right] - \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r} = E$$

Multiply by $\frac{2mr^2}{\hbar^2}$:

$$-\frac{1}{R}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) - \frac{2mr^2}{\hbar^2}\left[\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r} + E\right] = \frac{1}{Y}\left[\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial Y}{\partial\theta}\right) + \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial\phi^2}\right]$$

The left side depends only on $r$, the right side only on $(\theta,\phi)$. Both must equal a constant, which we write as $l(l+1)$:

**Angular equation:** 
$$\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial Y}{\partial\theta}\right) + \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial\phi^2} + l(l+1)Y = 0$$

(Solution: spherical harmonics $Y_l^m(\theta,\phi)$)

**Radial equation:**
$$-\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) + \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\left[-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r} + \frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2mr^2}\right]R = \frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}R$$

Rearranging:
$$\boxed{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) + \left[-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r} + \frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2mr^2}\right]R = ER}$$

- 1 point: Final radial equation in correct form

**(b)** Physical origin and effect of centrifugal barrier:

The effective potential is:
$$V_{\text{eff}}(r) = -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r} + \frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2mr^2}$$

**Physical origin of centrifugal term:**

The second term $\frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2mr^2}$ is the **centrifugal barrier**, which arises from the rotational kinetic energy associated with angular momentum. Classically, a particle with angular momentum $L$ has rotational kinetic energy:
$$T_{\text{rot}} = \frac{L^2}{2mr^2}$$

In quantum mechanics, $L^2 \to \hbar^2l(l+1)$, giving the centrifugal term. This creates an effective repulsive potential that prevents the particle from reaching $r = 0$ when $l > 0$.

**Effect near $r = 0$:**

For **$l = 0$ (s-states):**
- No centrifugal barrier: $V_{\text{eff}} \approx -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}$ (pure Coulomb)
- Near $r = 0$: The radial wavefunction $R(r) \propto r^l = r^0 = \text{constant}$
- $R(0)$ is **finite** (non-zero)
- The electron has non-zero probability density at the nucleus: $|\psi(0)|^2 \neq 0$
- This is important for hyperfine structure and isotope shifts

For **$l > 0$ (p, d, f, ... states):**
- Centrifugal barrier dominates as $r \to 0$: $V_{\text{eff}} \sim +\frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2mr^2} \to +\infty$
- Near $r = 0$: $R(r) \propto r^l$ (vanishes as $r \to 0$)
- $R(0) = 0$ (wavefunction must vanish)
- Higher $l$ → stronger barrier → faster vanishing
- The electron is **excluded** from the nucleus
- This affects the wavefunction penetration and reduces contact interactions

**Summary:** The centrifugal barrier is a repulsive quantum mechanical effect from angular momentum that prevents particles from approaching the center. Only s-states ($l=0$) can penetrate to the nucleus.

**Scoring:** 4 points
- 1 point: Correct identification as rotational/centrifugal energy
- 1 point: Classical analogy $L^2/(2mr^2)$ and quantum replacement
- 1 point: Correct behavior for $l=0$ (finite at origin)
- 1 point: Correct behavior for $l>0$ (vanishes as $r^l$)

**(c)** Allowed quantum numbers and degeneracy:

**Allowed values of $l$:**
$$l = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, n-1$$

This gives $n$ possible values of $l$ for each $n$.

**For each $l$, allowed values of $m$:**
$$m = -l, -l+1, \ldots, 0, \ldots, l-1, l$$

This gives $(2l+1)$ possible values of $m$ for each $l$.

**Total degeneracy (ignoring spin):**
$$g_n = \sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(2l+1)$$

This is the sum of the first $n$ odd numbers:
$$= 1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots + (2n-1)$$

Using the formula $\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(2l+1) = n^2$:

$$\boxed{g_n = n^2}$$

**Examples:**
- $n=1$: $l=0$, $m=0$ → degeneracy = 1
- $n=2$: $(l,m) = (0,0), (1,-1), (1,0), (1,1)$ → degeneracy = 4
- $n=3$: $(l,m)$ has 9 combinations → degeneracy = 9

This $n^2$ degeneracy is **accidental** (special to the Coulomb potential) and arises from hidden SO(4) symmetry. Including spin doubles this to $2n^2$.

**(e)** Weak-field Zeeman effect using perturbation theory:

**Initial state:** $n=2$, $l=1$ (a 2p state)

For $l=1$, the allowed values are $m = -1, 0, +1$ (three degenerate states before perturbation)

**Perturbation:**
$$\hat{V} = \frac{eB_0}{2m_e}\hat{L}_z$$

**First-order energy correction:**

For each $m$ value:
$$E_m^{(1)} = \langle n,l,m|\hat{V}|n,l,m\rangle = \frac{eB_0}{2m_e}\langle n,l,m|\hat{L}_z|n,l,m\rangle$$

Since $\hat{L}_z|l,m\rangle = \hbar m|l,m\rangle$:
$$E_m^{(1)} = \frac{eB_0}{2m_e} \cdot \hbar m = \frac{e\hbar B_0}{2m_e} m = \mu_B B_0 m$$

where $\mu_B = \frac{e\hbar}{2m_e} = 9.274 \times 10^{-24}$ J/T is the **Bohr magneton**.

**Energy shifts:**

$$\boxed{\Delta E_m = \mu_B B_0 m}$$

For the three states:
- $m = -1$: $\Delta E_{-1} = -\mu_B B_0$ (shifts down)
- $m = 0$: $\Delta E_0 = 0$ (no shift)
- $m = +1$: $\Delta E_{+1} = +\mu_B B_0$ (shifts up)

The originally degenerate $n=2$, $l=1$ level **splits into three levels** separated by $\Delta E = \mu_B B_0$.

**Physical interpretation:** The magnetic field breaks the rotational symmetry, lifting the degeneracy. The energy shift is proportional to the $z$-component of orbital angular momentum, with states aligned with the field having lower energy (for electron's negative charge, but we use the magnitude here for the orbital contribution).
