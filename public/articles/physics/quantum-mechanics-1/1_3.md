# Quantum Mechanics Topic 1.3: Time Evolution and the Schrödinger Equation

Classical mechanics is deterministic: Newton's laws $F = ma$ evolve position and momentum forward in time uniquely. Quantum mechanics is equally deterministic—for the state vector. The **time-dependent Schrödinger equation** $i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi(t)\rangle = \hat{H}|\psi(t)\rangle$ governs how quantum states evolve. Give me $|\psi(0)\rangle$ and the Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$, and I can predict $|\psi(t)\rangle$ for all future time. The probabilistic aspect enters only at measurement, not in the evolution itself.

The equation's structure reveals deep physics. The Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$ (total energy operator) generates time evolution. States with definite energy—**stationary states** satisfying $\hat{H}|\psi\rangle = E|\psi\rangle$—evolve by a simple phase: $|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-iEt/\hbar}|\psi\rangle$. General states decompose into energy eigenstates: $|\psi(t)\rangle = \sum c_n e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|\psi_n\rangle$. The time evolution operator $\hat{U}(t) = e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}$ is unitary, preserving probability—particles don't spontaneously appear or disappear.

This article develops time evolution from first principles. We'll solve the time-dependent Schrödinger equation via separation of variables, understand stationary states and their physical meaning, derive the continuity equation expressing probability conservation, prove Ehrenfest's theorem connecting quantum and classical mechanics, and explore how conserved quantities arise from symmetries. You'll see that quantum evolution, though fundamentally different from classical, preserves probability and obeys classical-like equations for expectation values.

## The Time-Dependent Schrödinger Equation

**Postulate (Time Evolution)**: 

$$i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi(t)\rangle = \hat{H}|\psi(t)\rangle$$

In position representation:

$$i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} + V(x)\psi$$

This is first-order in time, second-order in space—requires initial condition $\psi(x, 0)$ and boundary conditions in space.

### Example 1: Separation of Variables

For time-independent Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$ (potential $V$ doesn't depend on time), solve by assuming $\psi(x, t) = \phi(x)T(t)$.

**Solution:**

Substitute into Schrödinger equation:

$$i\hbar\phi(x)\frac{dT}{dt} = T(t)\hat{H}\phi(x)$$

Divide by $\phi T$:

$$\frac{i\hbar}{T}\frac{dT}{dt} = \frac{1}{\phi}\hat{H}\phi = E$$ (constant, since LHS depends only on $t$, RHS only on $x$)

Two equations:

**Time part**: $i\hbar\frac{dT}{dt} = ET \Rightarrow T(t) = e^{-iEt/\hbar}$

**Space part**: $\hat{H}\phi = E\phi$ (time-independent Schrödinger equation, eigenvalue problem)

**Answer:** $\psi(x, t) = \phi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ where $\phi$ is energy eigenstate

### Example 2: Stationary States

Why are solutions $\psi_n(x, t) = \phi_n(x)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$ called "stationary"?

**Solution:**

Probability density:

$$|\psi_n(x, t)|^2 = |\phi_n(x)|^2|e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|^2 = |\phi_n(x)|^2$$

Phase $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$ has $|e^{i\theta}| = 1$, so cancels in probability.

**Answer:** Probability distribution time-independent (though state vector rotates in phase)

### Example 3: General Solution

For Hamiltonian with energy eigenstates $\{\phi_n\}$ and eigenvalues $\{E_n\}$, write general solution to time-dependent equation.

**Solution:**

Since eigenstates form complete basis:

$$\psi(x, 0) = \sum_n c_n\phi_n(x)$$

Each eigenstate evolves: $\phi_n(x) \to \phi_n(x)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$

By linearity (superposition):

$$\psi(x, t) = \sum_n c_n\phi_n(x)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$$

where $c_n = \int \phi_n^*(x)\psi(x, 0) dx$ (from initial condition)

**Answer:** Superposition of stationary states with time-dependent phases

## Time Evolution Operator

Define **time evolution operator**:

$$\hat{U}(t) = e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}$$

Then: $|\psi(t)\rangle = \hat{U}(t)|\psi(0)\rangle$

### Example 4: Verifying Time Evolution Operator

Show that $|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}|\psi(0)\rangle$ satisfies the Schrödinger equation.

**Solution:**

Take time derivative:

$$i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi(t)\rangle = i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}|\psi(0)\rangle\right]$$

Using $\frac{d}{dt}e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar} = -\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}$:

$$= i\hbar \cdot \left(-\frac{i}{\hbar}\right)\hat{H}e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}|\psi(0)\rangle = \hat{H}e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}|\psi(0)\rangle = \hat{H}|\psi(t)\rangle$$ ✓

**Answer:** Satisfies $i\hbar\partial_t|\psi\rangle = \hat{H}|\psi\rangle$

### Example 5: Unitarity

Show that $\hat{U}(t)$ is unitary: $\hat{U}^\dagger\hat{U} = \mathbb{1}$.

**Solution:**

$$\hat{U}^\dagger = \left(e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}\right)^\dagger = e^{-i\hat{H}^\dagger t/\hbar}$$

Since $\hat{H}$ is Hermitian: $\hat{H}^\dagger = \hat{H}$

$$\hat{U}^\dagger = e^{i\hat{H}t/\hbar}$$

$$\hat{U}^\dagger\hat{U} = e^{i\hat{H}t/\hbar}e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar} = e^{0} = \mathbb{1}$$ ✓

**Physical meaning**: Probability conserved:

$$\langle\psi(t)|\psi(t)\rangle = \langle\psi(0)|\hat{U}^\dagger\hat{U}|\psi(0)\rangle = \langle\psi(0)|\psi(0)\rangle$$

**Answer:** Unitary evolution preserves normalization (total probability = 1)

### Example 6: Free Particle Evolution

For free particle ($V = 0$), energy eigenstates are $\phi_k(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{ikx}$ with $E_k = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}$.

If $\psi(x, 0) = e^{-x^2/2a^2}$ (Gaussian), how does it evolve?

**Solution:**

Expand in momentum eigenstates:

$$\psi(x, 0) = \int \tilde{\psi}(k)\phi_k(x) dk$$

where $\tilde{\psi}(k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-ikx}e^{-x^2/2a^2} dx$ (Fourier transform)

Each component evolves: $\phi_k(x)e^{-iE_kt/\hbar} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{ikx}e^{-i\hbar k^2t/2m}$

$$\psi(x, t) = \int \tilde{\psi}(k)e^{ikx}e^{-i\hbar k^2t/2m} dk$$

Result (Gaussian integrals): Width spreads $a(t) = a\sqrt{1 + (\hbar t/ma^2)^2}$

**Answer:** Gaussian wave packet spreads in time (dispersion)

## Probability Conservation

From Schrödinger equation, derive **continuity equation** expressing probability conservation.

### Example 7: Probability Density Evolution

Define probability density $\rho = |\psi|^2$. Compute $\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}$.

**Solution:**

$$\rho = \psi^*\psi$$

$$\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial\psi^*}{\partial t}\psi + \psi^*\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}$$

From Schrödinger: $i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} + V\psi$

Complex conjugate: $-i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi^*}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\psi^*}{\partial x^2} + V\psi^*$

$$\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\left[\psi\left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi^*_{xx} + V\psi^*\right) - \psi^*\left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi_{xx} + V\psi\right)\right]$$

$V$ terms cancel:

$$= \frac{\hbar}{2mi}\left(\psi\psi^*_{xx} - \psi^*\psi_{xx}\right)$$

**Answer:** Can write as $\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial j}{\partial x}$ (continuity)

### Example 8: Probability Current

Define probability current:

$$j = \frac{\hbar}{2mi}(\psi^*\psi_x - \psi\psi_x^*) = \frac{\hbar}{m}\text{Im}(\psi^*\psi_x)$$

Verify continuity equation: $\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial j}{\partial x} = 0$

**Solution:**

From Example 7, we had:

$$\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = \frac{\hbar}{2mi}\left(\psi\psi^*_{xx} - \psi^*\psi_{xx}\right)$$

Compute $\frac{\partial j}{\partial x}$:

$$\frac{\partial j}{\partial x} = \frac{\hbar}{2mi}\left(\psi^*_x\psi_x + \psi^*\psi_{xx} - \psi_x\psi^*_x - \psi\psi^*_{xx}\right)$$

$$= \frac{\hbar}{2mi}\left(\psi^*\psi_{xx} - \psi\psi^*_{xx}\right) = -\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}$$ ✓

**Answer:** $\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial j}{\partial x} = 0$ (probability conserved locally)

### Example 9: Integrated Conservation

Show total probability conserved: $\frac{d}{dt}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi|^2 dx = 0$

**Solution:**

$$\frac{d}{dt}\int\rho dx = \int\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} dx = -\int\frac{\partial j}{\partial x} dx$$

$$= -[j(x)]_{-\infty}^{\infty}$$

If $\psi \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$: $j(\pm\infty) = 0$

$$= 0$$ ✓

**Answer:** Total probability constant (normalization preserved)

## Ehrenfest's Theorem

**Ehrenfest's theorem**: Quantum expectation values obey classical-like equations.

$$\frac{d\langle\hat{x}\rangle}{dt} = \frac{\langle\hat{p}\rangle}{m}$$

$$\frac{d\langle\hat{p}\rangle}{dt} = -\left\langle\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\right\rangle$$

### Example 10: Deriving First Ehrenfest Equation

Prove $\frac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt} = \frac{\langle p\rangle}{m}$.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\int\psi^* x\psi dx = \int\left(\frac{\partial\psi^*}{\partial t}x\psi + \psi^* x\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}\right) dx$$

From Schrödinger equation (and conjugate):

$$= \int\left[\frac{1}{i\hbar}\left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi^*_{xx} + V\psi^*\right)x\psi + \psi^* x\frac{1}{i\hbar}\left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi_{xx} + V\psi\right)\right] dx$$

$V$ terms cancel (Hermitian). Integration by parts on $\psi^*_{xx}$:

$$= \frac{\hbar}{2mi}\int(\psi^*_{xx}x\psi - \psi^* x\psi_{xx}) dx$$

$$= \frac{\hbar}{mi}\int\psi^*\psi_x dx = \frac{1}{m}\int\psi^*\left(-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)\psi dx$$

$$= \frac{\langle\hat{p}\rangle}{m}$$ ✓

**Answer:** Expectation of position evolves like classical $v = p/m$

### Example 11: Second Ehrenfest Equation

Prove $\frac{d\langle p\rangle}{dt} = -\langle V'(x)\rangle$ (quantum analog of $F = ma$).

**Solution:**

$$\frac{d\langle p\rangle}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\int\psi^*\left(-i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\right) dx$$

Using Schrödinger equation (tedious but straightforward):

$$= \int\psi^*\left(-\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\right)\psi dx = -\left\langle\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\right\rangle$$

**Answer:** Force equals mass times acceleration (for expectation values)

### Example 12: Classical Limit

For macroscopic system where $\Delta x \ll \langle x \rangle$, what happens?

**Solution:**

Ehrenfest: $m\frac{d^2\langle x\rangle}{dt^2} = -\langle V'(x)\rangle$

If wavepacket narrow: $V'(x) \approx V'(\langle x\rangle)$ over support of $|\psi|^2$

$$\langle V'(x)\rangle \approx V'(\langle x\rangle)$$

$$m\frac{d^2\langle x\rangle}{dt^2} \approx -V'(\langle x\rangle)$$

This is Newton's second law for position $\langle x\rangle$!

**Answer:** Classical mechanics emerges as limit (narrow wavepackets, $\hbar \to 0$)

## Conservation Laws and Symmetries

**Theorem**: If $[\hat{H}, \hat{A}] = 0$, then $\langle\hat{A}\rangle$ is constant in time.

### Example 13: Conserved Quantities

For free particle ($V = 0$), show momentum is conserved.

**Solution:**

$$[\hat{H}, \hat{p}] = \left[\frac{\hat{p}^2}{2m}, \hat{p}\right]$$

Since $\hat{p}$ commutes with itself: $[\hat{p}^2, \hat{p}] = 0$

$$\Rightarrow [\hat{H}, \hat{p}] = 0$$

Therefore: $\frac{d\langle\hat{p}\rangle}{dt} = 0$

**Answer:** Momentum conserved (no external force, translation symmetry)

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Stationary state: $\psi_n(x,t) = \phi_n(x)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$
$|\psi_n|^2 = |\phi_n|^2$ (time-independent)
Phase rotates: $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$ but $|e^{i\theta}| = 1$
Energy eigenstates are stationary
Superpositions are not stationary

**Problem 2: C**
$\hat{U}^\dagger\hat{U} = e^{i\hat{H}t/\hbar}e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar} = \mathbb{1}$
Preserves inner products: $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle$ constant
Reversible: $\hat{U}^{-1} = \hat{U}^\dagger$
Not Hermitian: $\hat{U}^\dagger = e^{i\hat{H}t/\hbar} \neq \hat{U}$
Generates rotations in Hilbert space

**Problem 3: B**
$\frac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt} = \langle p\rangle/m$ (velocity)
$\frac{d\langle p\rangle}{dt} = -\langle V'(x)\rangle$ (force)
Classical limit: narrow wavepackets
$\hbar \to 0$ recovers Newton's laws
Correspondence principle

**Problem 4: C**
$\rho = |\psi|^2$ (probability density)
$j$ (probability current/flux)
$\int\rho dx = 1$ conserved
Local conservation (not just global)
Analogy: charge conservation in EM

**Problem 5: B**
$[\hat{H}, \hat{A}] = 0 \Rightarrow \frac{d\langle A\rangle}{dt} = 0$
Conserved quantities from symmetries
Example: free particle, $[\hat{H}, \hat{p}] = 0$
Noether's theorem (quantum version)
Energy always conserved ($[\hat{H}, \hat{H}] = 0$)
