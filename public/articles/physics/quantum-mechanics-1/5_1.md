# Quantum Mechanics Topic 5.1: Non-degenerate Perturbation Theory

Most quantum systems lack exact analytical solutions. The hydrogen atom, harmonic oscillator, and infinite square well are exceptional—most realistic problems involve potentials too complicated to solve exactly. When a system is "close" to a solvable one, **perturbation theory** provides systematic approximations. If we know the eigenstates and energies of $\hat{H}_0$ exactly, and our actual Hamiltonian differs only slightly—$\hat{H} = \hat{H}_0 + \lambda\hat{V}$ where $\lambda$ is small—we can expand the unknown solutions in powers of $\lambda$.

Non-degenerate perturbation theory addresses systems where each unperturbed energy level is unique. The method treats the perturbation $\hat{V}$ as a small correction, expanding both energies and eigenstates in power series. First-order corrections give the expectation value of $\hat{V}$ in the unperturbed state—a surprisingly simple result. Second-order corrections account for "virtual transitions" to other states, revealing how nearby energy levels influence each other. The theory's power lies in its systematic nature: higher orders provide increasingly accurate results, and the formulae work for any perturbation, from anharmonic oscillators to fine structure corrections.

This article develops non-degenerate perturbation theory from first principles. We'll derive the fundamental formulas for energy and state corrections, explore when the approximation is valid, and apply the method to physical systems. You'll learn to identify appropriate unperturbed Hamiltonians, compute corrections to arbitrary order, and recognize the theory's limitations. Perturbation theory is essential for atomic physics, molecular spectroscopy, and solid-state physics—anywhere quantum systems deviate slightly from exactly solvable models.

## The Perturbation Expansion

Consider a Hamiltonian that can be written as a solvable part plus a small correction:

$$\hat{H} = \hat{H}_0 + \lambda\hat{V}$$

where $\lambda$ is a small dimensionless parameter (set to 1 at the end), and we know the complete solution to the unperturbed problem:

$$\hat{H}_0|\psi_n^{(0)}\rangle = E_n^{(0)}|\psi_n^{(0)}\rangle$$

We seek the perturbed eigenvalues $E_n$ and eigenstates $|\psi_n\rangle$ as power series:

$$E_n = E_n^{(0)} + \lambda E_n^{(1)} + \lambda^2 E_n^{(2)} + \cdots$$

$$|\psi_n\rangle = |\psi_n^{(0)}\rangle + \lambda|\psi_n^{(1)}\rangle + \lambda^2|\psi_n^{(2)}\rangle + \cdots$$

The superscript $(k)$ denotes the $k$-th order correction.

### Example 1: Setting Up Perturbation Theory

An electron in a one-dimensional infinite well of width $L$ experiences a small electric field producing potential $V(x) = -eEx$. Identify $\hat{H}_0$ and $\hat{V}$.

**Solution:**

The unperturbed Hamiltonian is the infinite square well:
$$\hat{H}_0: \psi_n^{(0)}(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right), \quad E_n^{(0)} = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$$

The perturbation is:
$$\hat{V} = -eEx$$

(assuming $x$ measured from left edge)

**Answer:** $\hat{H}_0$ is infinite well, $\hat{V} = -eEx$ is perturbation

### Example 2: First-Order Energy Correction

The first-order energy correction is given by:

$$E_n^{(1)} = \langle\psi_n^{(0)}|\hat{V}|\psi_n^{(0)}\rangle$$

This is simply the **expectation value** of the perturbation in the unperturbed state.

For the electric field perturbation in Example 1, compute $E_1^{(1)}$.

**Solution:**

$$E_1^{(1)} = \langle\psi_1^{(0)}|\hat{V}|\psi_1^{(0)}\rangle = -eE\int_0^L x\left|\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right)\right|^2 dx$$

$$= -eE \cdot \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L x\sin^2\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right)dx$$

Using $\int_0^L x\sin^2(n\pi x/L)dx = L^2/4$:

$$E_1^{(1)} = -eE \cdot \frac{2}{L} \cdot \frac{L^2}{4} = -\frac{eEL}{2}$$

**Physical interpretation:** The electric field shifts the electron toward lower potential, lowering the energy by the field times the average position.

**Answer:** $E_1^{(1)} = -eEL/2$ (energy lowered by field)

### Example 3: First-Order State Correction

The first-order correction to the state is:

$$|\psi_n^{(1)}\rangle = \sum_{m \neq n}\frac{\langle\psi_m^{(0)}|\hat{V}|\psi_n^{(0)}\rangle}{E_n^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)}}|\psi_m^{(0)}\rangle$$

Notice the sum excludes $m = n$, and denominators are **energy differences**.

For an anharmonic oscillator $\hat{H} = \hat{H}_0 + \lambda x^4$ where $\hat{H}_0$ is the standard harmonic oscillator, which states contribute to $|\psi_0^{(1)}\rangle$?

**Solution:**

The perturbation is $\hat{V} = x^4$. Using ladder operators: $\hat{x} = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}(\hat{a}_+ + \hat{a}_-)$

$$x^4 = \left(\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}\right)^2(\hat{a}_+ + \hat{a}_-)^4$$

Acting on $|0\rangle$, this creates terms with $|4\rangle, |2\rangle$, and $|0\rangle$.

For $|\psi_0^{(1)}\rangle$, we need $\langle m|x^4|0\rangle$ with $m \neq 0$.

Only $m = 2$ and $m = 4$ give non-zero matrix elements (even selection rule).

**Answer:** States $|2\rangle$ and $|4\rangle$ contribute to first-order correction

## Second-Order Corrections

### Example 4: Second-Order Energy Formula

The second-order energy correction is:

$$E_n^{(2)} = \sum_{m \neq n}\frac{|\langle\psi_m^{(0)}|\hat{V}|\psi_n^{(0)}\rangle|^2}{E_n^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)}}$$

This involves **all other states** weighted by matrix elements squared and inverse energy gaps.

For the anharmonic oscillator ground state, why is $E_0^{(2)}$ always negative?

**Solution:**

From Example 3, only states with $m > 0$ contribute (higher energy).

Each term has:
- Numerator: $|\langle m|x^4|0\rangle|^2 > 0$ (positive)
- Denominator: $E_0^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)} < 0$ (ground state has lowest energy)

Therefore each term is **negative**, making $E_0^{(2)} < 0$.

**Physical interpretation:** The perturbation allows "virtual transitions" to higher states, lowering the energy through quantum fluctuations.

**Answer:** $E_0^{(2)} < 0$ because all energy denominators are negative

### Example 5: Computing Second-Order Correction

For a particle in an infinite well with perturbation $\hat{V} = V_0\sin^2(\pi x/L)$, compute $E_1^{(2)}$.

**Solution:**

Need $\langle\psi_m^{(0)}|\hat{V}|\psi_1^{(0)}\rangle$ for all $m \neq 1$.

Using $\sin^2(\pi x/L) = \frac{1}{2}[1 - \cos(2\pi x/L)]$:

$$\hat{V} = \frac{V_0}{2} - \frac{V_0}{2}\cos(2\pi x/L)$$

The constant term contributes only to first order (already computed).

For the cosine term:
$$\langle m|\cos(2\pi x/L)|1\rangle = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L \sin\frac{m\pi x}{L}\cos\frac{2\pi x}{L}\sin\frac{\pi x}{L}dx$$

Using product formulas, only $m = 1$ and $m = 3$ give non-zero contributions.

For $m = 3$: $\langle 3|\hat{V}|1\rangle = -\frac{V_0}{2} \cdot \frac{8}{3\pi}$

$$E_1^{(2)} = \frac{\left(\frac{V_0}{2} \cdot \frac{8}{3\pi}\right)^2}{E_1^{(0)} - E_3^{(0)}} = \frac{64V_0^2}{9\pi^2} \cdot \frac{1}{\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}(1 - 9)}$$

$$= -\frac{64V_0^2}{9\pi^2} \cdot \frac{mL^2}{4\pi^2\hbar^2}$$

**Answer:** $E_1^{(2)} = -\frac{16V_0^2mL^2}{9\pi^4\hbar^2}$ (negative, as expected)

## Validity and Convergence

### Example 6: When Perturbation Theory Fails

Perturbation theory requires $|E_n^{(1)}| \ll |E_n^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)}|$ for nearby states. When does this break down?

**Solution:**

Consider the infinite well with a **delta function barrier** at center: $\hat{V} = \alpha\delta(x - L/2)$.

For symmetric states ($n$ odd): $\psi_n(L/2) = \sqrt{2/L}\sin(n\pi/2) = \pm\sqrt{2/L}$

First-order correction: $E_n^{(1)} = \alpha|\psi_n(L/2)|^2 = 2\alpha/L$

For large $\alpha$, this becomes comparable to or larger than level spacing $\Delta E \sim E_{n+2}^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)} \sim n\pi^2\hbar^2/(mL^2)$.

When $\alpha > \pi^2\hbar^2/(2m)$, perturbation theory breaks down—the barrier fundamentally alters the spectrum.

**Answer:** Fails when perturbation becomes comparable to level spacing

### Example 7: Level Repulsion

When two unperturbed levels have nearby energies, second-order perturbation shows they "repel" each other. Why?

**Solution:**

Consider two levels $n$ and $m$ with $E_n^{(0)} \approx E_m^{(0)}$. 

If $\langle m|\hat{V}|n\rangle \neq 0$:

$$E_n^{(2)} \text{ includes } \frac{|\langle m|\hat{V}|n\rangle|^2}{E_n^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)}}$$

If $E_n^{(0)} < E_m^{(0)}$ (small positive denominator): $E_n^{(2)} > 0$ (level pushed up)

If $E_m^{(0)} < E_n^{(0)}$ (small negative denominator): $E_m^{(2)} < 0$ (level pushed down)

The levels **repel** and never cross under perturbation (avoided crossing).

**Answer:** Levels repel via second-order mixing; no crossing if coupled

### Example 8: Harmonic Oscillator with Small Anharmonicity

For $\hat{H} = \frac{\hat{p}^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2\hat{x}^2 + \lambda\hat{x}^4$, compute $E_0^{(1)}$ and estimate $E_0^{(2)}$.

**Solution:**

First order:
$$E_0^{(1)} = \langle 0|\lambda x^4|0\rangle$$

Using $\langle x^2\rangle_0 = \hbar/(2m\omega)$ and $\langle x^4\rangle_0 = 3(\hbar/(2m\omega))^2$:

$$E_0^{(1)} = 3\lambda\left(\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}\right)^2$$

Second order (approximate, using only $|2\rangle$):
$$E_0^{(2)} \approx \frac{|\langle 2|x^4|0\rangle|^2}{E_0^{(0)} - E_2^{(0)}} = \frac{|\langle 2|x^4|0\rangle|^2}{-2\hbar\omega}$$

Computing $\langle 2|x^4|0\rangle$ using ladder operators gives result proportional to $\lambda^2$.

**Answer:** $E_0^{(1)} = 3\lambda\hbar^2/(4m^2\omega^2)$; $E_0^{(2)} < 0$

## Physical Applications

### Example 9: Van der Waals Interaction

Two neutral atoms at large separation $R$ interact via instantaneous dipole-dipole interaction: $\hat{V} \propto 1/R^6$. This is a perturbation to two isolated atoms. Why does it lower energy?

**Solution:**

Unperturbed: Two atoms in ground states $|0\rangle_A|0\rangle_B$ with energy $E_0^{(0)} = E_{A,0} + E_{B,0}$.

Perturbation couples to excited states $|n\rangle_A|m\rangle_B$ where atoms have instantaneous dipole moments.

First order: $E^{(1)} = 0$ (ground states have no permanent dipole)

Second order: Virtual transitions to excited states:
$$E^{(2)} = \sum_{n,m \neq 0,0}\frac{|\langle nm|\hat{V}|00\rangle|^2}{E_0^{(0)} - E_{nm}^{(0)}} < 0$$

All denominators are negative (excited states higher), giving attractive $-C_6/R^6$ interaction.

**Answer:** Second-order perturbation gives attractive $-C_6/R^6$ (van der Waals)

### Example 10: Zeeman Effect (Weak Field Preview)

An electron in hydrogen ground state experiences weak magnetic field $B$ along $z$. The perturbation is $\hat{V} = \frac{eB}{2m}(\hat{L}_z + 2\hat{S}_z)$. What is $E_{1s}^{(1)}$?

**Solution:**

Ground state: $|n,l,m_l,m_s\rangle = |1,0,0,m_s\rangle$

Since $l = 0$: $\hat{L}_z|1,0,0,m_s\rangle = 0$

$$E_{1s}^{(1)} = \langle 1,0,0,m_s|\frac{eB}{2m} \cdot 2\hat{S}_z|1,0,0,m_s\rangle$$

$$= \frac{eB}{m} \cdot \frac{\hbar}{2}m_s = \frac{eB\hbar}{2m}m_s$$

where $m_s = \pm 1/2$.

Energy shift: $\Delta E = \pm\mu_B B$ where $\mu_B = e\hbar/(2m)$ is the Bohr magneton.

**Answer:** $E^{(1)} = \pm\mu_B B$ (Zeeman splitting into two levels)

### Example 11: Selection of Unperturbed Hamiltonian

For the hydrogen atom with relativistic corrections, why do we choose the non-relativistic Coulomb problem as $\hat{H}_0$ rather than free particle?

**Solution:**

Perturbation theory works when perturbed and unperturbed spectra are "close."

**Choice 1:** $\hat{H}_0$ = free particle
- Continuous spectrum
- No natural states to expand around
- Relativistic correction $\sim mc^2(\alpha)^2 \approx 13.6$ eV comparable to kinetic energy
- Perturbation theory fails

**Choice 2:** $\hat{H}_0$ = Coulomb problem
- Discrete bound states with known wavefunctions
- Relativistic correction $\sim \alpha^2 \times$ binding energy $\approx 10^{-5}$ eV
- Small compared to level spacing $\sim$ eV
- Perturbation theory valid

**Answer:** Choose $\hat{H}_0$ with spectrum similar to full problem; Coulomb appropriate

### Example 12: Higher-Order Corrections

Why are second-order corrections often sufficient in practice?

**Solution:**

Energy corrections scale as:
$$E_n = E_n^{(0)} + \lambda E_n^{(1)} + \lambda^2 E_n^{(2)} + \lambda^3 E_n^{(3)} + \cdots$$

If perturbation is truly small ($\lambda \ll 1$), successive terms decrease rapidly:
$$\frac{E_n^{(2)}}{E_n^{(1)}} \sim \lambda, \quad \frac{E_n^{(3)}}{E_n^{(2)}} \sim \lambda$$

Example: Hydrogen fine structure with $\alpha \approx 1/137$:
- First order: $\sim 10^{-5}$ eV
- Second order: $\sim 10^{-7}$ eV (barely measurable)
- Third order: $\sim 10^{-9}$ eV (negligible)

**Answer:** Geometric series in $\lambda$; higher orders rapidly negligible for small $\lambda$

### Example 13: Non-perturbative Regimes

Some systems require **non-perturbative** methods even for "small" perturbations. Example: tunneling in double well potential.

**Solution:**

Consider symmetric double well separated by barrier height $V_0$.

Perturbation from single well: $\hat{V} =$ second well

Even for large separation, ground state splits into symmetric/antisymmetric combination:
$$\Delta E \sim e^{-const./\hbar}$$

This **exponentially small** splitting cannot be captured by any finite order perturbation theory (all orders give zero for large separation).

Requires instanton methods or WKB (non-perturbative).

**Answer:** Tunneling effects are non-perturbative; invisible in power series

## Practice Problems

**Problem 1:** The first-order energy correction $E_n^{(1)}$ is:

A) $\sum_m \langle\psi_m^{(0)}|\hat{V}|\psi_n^{(0)}\rangle$
B) $\langle\psi_n^{(0)}|\hat{V}|\psi_n^{(0)}\rangle$
C) $\langle\psi_n^{(0)}|\hat{H}_0|\psi_n^{(0)}\rangle$
D) $\sum_{m \neq n}\frac{|\langle\psi_m^{(0)}|\hat{V}|\psi_n^{(0)}\rangle|^2}{E_n^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)}}$
E) $\langle\psi_n^{(0)}|\hat{V}^2|\psi_n^{(0)}\rangle$

**Problem 2:** Second-order energy corrections are always:

A) Positive
B) Negative for ground state
C) Zero
D) Equal to first-order
E) Independent of other states

**Problem 3:** Perturbation theory breaks down when:

A) Perturbation is very small
B) All energy levels are degenerate
C) $|\langle\psi_m|\hat{V}|\psi_n\rangle| \ll |E_m - E_n|$
D) Perturbation is comparable to level spacing
E) System is one-dimensional

**Problem 4:** In first-order perturbation, $|\psi_n^{(1)}\rangle$ is a superposition of:

A) Only ground state
B) Only excited states
C) All states except $|\psi_n^{(0)}\rangle$
D) Only $|\psi_n^{(0)}\rangle$
E) Random states

**Problem 5:** Level repulsion occurs because:

A) Electrons repel electrostatically
B) Perturbation mixes nearby states
C) Energy is conserved
D) Wavefunctions must be orthogonal
E) Quantum numbers must be integers

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
First-order energy correction is expectation value of perturbation in unperturbed state:
$$E_n^{(1)} = \langle\psi_n^{(0)}|\hat{V}|\psi_n^{(0)}\rangle$$
This is the simplest result in perturbation theory—just compute $\langle\hat{V}\rangle$ in the state you're correcting. No sum over states, no energy denominators. The diagonal matrix element of $\hat{V}$ gives the first-order shift.

**Problem 2: B**
For ground state, all other states have higher energy: $E_m^{(0)} > E_0^{(0)}$
Second-order formula: $E_0^{(2)} = \sum_{m \neq 0}\frac{|\langle\psi_m^{(0)}|\hat{V}|\psi_0^{(0)}\rangle|^2}{E_0^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)}}$
Every denominator is negative, numerators are positive, so $E_0^{(2)} < 0$. Physical interpretation: virtual transitions to higher states lower ground state energy through quantum fluctuations. For excited states, can be positive or negative depending on which nearby states dominate.

**Problem 3: D**
Perturbation theory assumes corrections are small: $|E_n^{(1)}| \ll |E_n^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)}|$ for nearby states. When perturbation becomes comparable to level spacing, this fails. Example: strong external fields comparable to internal atomic fields. The expansion parameter must be small for convergence. Choice D correctly identifies breakdown condition.

**Problem 4: C**
First-order state correction: $|\psi_n^{(1)}\rangle = \sum_{m \neq n}\frac{\langle\psi_m^{(0)}|\hat{V}|\psi_n^{(0)}\rangle}{E_n^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)}}|\psi_m^{(0)}\rangle$
The sum explicitly excludes $m = n$. The perturbation "mixes in" all other unperturbed states, weighted by matrix elements and inverse energy gaps. States with larger $\langle m|\hat{V}|n\rangle$ or smaller $|E_m - E_n|$ contribute more.

**Problem 5: B**
Level repulsion is quantum mechanical effect from perturbation mixing. When two levels have nearby energies and non-zero coupling $\langle m|\hat{V}|n\rangle \neq 0$, second-order perturbation pushes them apart: lower level goes down, upper level goes up. Levels cannot cross if they have same symmetry and couple under perturbation—this is **avoided crossing**. Fundamental in molecular and atomic physics.
