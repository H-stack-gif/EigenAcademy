# Quantum Mechanics Topic 4.3: Identical Particles and Exchange Symmetry

Two electrons look identical. You cannot tag one with red paint and the other with blue—they're fundamentally **indistinguishable**. This isn't experimental limitation but nature's law. Quantum mechanics forbids labeling identical particles. The profound consequence: wavefunctions must have definite **exchange symmetry**. Swap particles 1 and 2, and the wavefunction either stays the same (**symmetric**, for bosons) or flips sign (**antisymmetric**, for fermions). This simple requirement—$\psi(1,2) = \pm\psi(2,1)$—explains the periodic table, white dwarf stability, superfluidity, and why matter has structure.

The **spin-statistics theorem** (proven in quantum field theory) connects spin to exchange symmetry: particles with integer spin (0, 1, 2, ...) are bosons (symmetric); particles with half-integer spin (1/2, 3/2, ...) are fermions (antisymmetric). Electrons, protons, neutrons—all spin-1/2, all fermions. The **Pauli exclusion principle** follows immediately: two fermions cannot occupy the same quantum state. Put $N$ fermions in a box, and you must stack them in energy levels. This "Pauli pressure" supports white dwarf stars against gravitational collapse.

This article develops identical particle physics rigorously. We'll prove wavefunctions must be symmetric or antisymmetric, construct proper two-particle states, derive the Pauli exclusion principle, introduce Slater determinants for many fermions, explore exchange interaction as effective force, and solve the helium atom including exchange effects. You'll see that particle statistics—not forces—determines much of nature's structure.

## Indistinguishability and Exchange

**Classical**: Particles have identity. Track particle 1's trajectory separately from particle 2.

**Quantum**: **Identical particles are indistinguishable**. Cannot assign labels.

### Example 1: Exchange Operator

Define permutation operator $\hat{P}_{12}$ that swaps particles:

$$\hat{P}_{12}\psi(1, 2) = \psi(2, 1)$$

**Solution:**

**Properties**:
- Hermitian: $\hat{P}_{12}^\dagger = \hat{P}_{12}$
- Idempotent: $\hat{P}_{12}^2 = \mathbb{1}$ (swap twice returns original)

From $\hat{P}_{12}^2 = \mathbb{1}$: eigenvalues satisfy $\lambda^2 = 1$, so $\lambda = \pm 1$.

**Eigenvalue $+1$**: Symmetric
**Eigenvalue $-1$**: Antisymmetric

**Physical requirement**: Observables cannot distinguish particles.

$$|\langle\psi|\hat{A}|\psi\rangle|^2 = |\langle\hat{P}_{12}\psi|\hat{A}|\hat{P}_{12}\psi\rangle|^2$$

This requires $|\hat{P}_{12}\psi\rangle = e^{i\phi}|\psi\rangle$ (phase factor).

For multiparticle systems: $e^{i\phi} = \pm 1$ (proven in QFT).

**Answer:** Wavefunction must be eigenstate of exchange with eigenvalue $\pm 1$

### Example 2: Two-Particle States

Construct symmetric and antisymmetric combinations.

**Solution:**

Given single-particle states $\phi_a$ and $\phi_b$:

**Symmetric** (bosons):

$$|\psi_+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\phi_a\rangle_1|\phi_b\rangle_2 + |\phi_b\rangle_1|\phi_a\rangle_2)$$

**Antisymmetric** (fermions):

$$|\psi_-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\phi_a\rangle_1|\phi_b\rangle_2 - |\phi_b\rangle_1|\phi_a\rangle_2)$$

**Special case**: If $\phi_a = \phi_b$:

$$|\psi_-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\phi_a\rangle_1|\phi_a\rangle_2 - |\phi_a\rangle_1|\phi_a\rangle_2) = 0$$

**Pauli exclusion**: Two fermions cannot occupy same state!

**Answer:** Symmetrize for bosons, antisymmetrize for fermions

## Spin-Statistics Connection

### Example 3: Spin-Statistics Theorem

**Statement**: Particles with integer spin are bosons; half-integer spin are fermions.

**Solution:**

**Proof**: Requires relativistic quantum field theory (beyond scope).

**Key ingredients**:
- Lorentz invariance
- Causality (spacelike-separated operators commute)
- Positive energy

**Result**: Connection between intrinsic spin and exchange statistics.

**Examples**:
- Photon (spin-1): boson
- Electron (spin-1/2): fermion
- Helium-4 nucleus (spin-0): boson
- Helium-3 nucleus (spin-1/2): fermion

**Answer:** Fundamental theorem linking spin to statistics (not proven from first principles in non-relativistic QM)

### Example 4: Total Wavefunction Symmetry

For fermions, **total** wavefunction must be antisymmetric.

**Solution:**

Total wavefunction: $\Psi = \psi_{\text{space}}(1,2) \cdot \chi_{\text{spin}}(1,2)$

**Requirement**: $\Psi$ antisymmetric under exchange.

**Options**:
1. Spatial symmetric × Spin antisymmetric (singlet)
2. Spatial antisymmetric × Spin symmetric (triplet)

**Example** (helium ground state):

Both electrons in $1s$ orbital: Spatial symmetric.

Must have spin antisymmetric (singlet): $\chi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle - |\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)$

Total spin $S = 0$ (para-helium).

**Answer:** Spatial and spin symmetries compensate to give overall antisymmetry

### Example 5: Exchange Interaction

Even without spin-dependent forces, exchange symmetry creates effective interaction.

**Solution:**

For two electrons in states $\phi_a, \phi_b$:

**Energy** (antisymmetric spatial):

$$E = 2\epsilon_0 + J - K$$

**Energy** (symmetric spatial):

$$E = 2\epsilon_0 + J + K$$

where:
- $J = \int\int|\phi_a(1)|^2 V(r_{12})|\phi_b(2)|^2 d^3r_1 d^3r_2$ (direct/Coulomb)
- $K = \int\int\phi_a^*(1)\phi_b^*(2)V(r_{12})\phi_b(1)\phi_a(2) d^3r_1 d^3r_2$ (exchange)

**Exchange integral** $K > 0$ (for repulsive $V$).

**Triplet lower**: Spatial antisymmetric (spin symmetric) has lower energy.

**Hund's rules**: Maximize spin to minimize energy (exchange-driven).

**Answer:** Exchange symmetry creates effective force (not from potential!)

## Pauli Exclusion Principle

### Example 6: Pauli Exclusion Statement

**Principle**: No two fermions can occupy the same quantum state.

**Solution:**

For identical fermions in same state $|\phi\rangle$:

$$|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\phi\rangle_1|\phi\rangle_2 - |\phi\rangle_1|\phi\rangle_2) = 0$$

Antisymmetric combination vanishes!

**Consequence**: Must fill different states → energy level structure.

**Periodic table**: Electrons fill shells ($1s^2, 2s^2, 2p^6, \ldots$).

Superscript = number of electrons in subshell (max $2(2l+1)$).

**Answer:** Follows directly from antisymmetrization requirement

### Example 7: Slater Determinant

For $N$ fermions in states $\phi_1, \ldots, \phi_N$:

**Solution:**

$$\Psi(1, 2, \ldots, N) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\begin{vmatrix} \phi_1(1) & \phi_2(1) & \cdots & \phi_N(1) \\ \phi_1(2) & \phi_2(2) & \cdots & \phi_N(2) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \phi_1(N) & \phi_2(N) & \cdots & \phi_N(N) \end{vmatrix}$$

**Properties**:
- Determinant changes sign under row exchange (antisymmetric) ✓
- Vanishes if two columns identical (Pauli exclusion) ✓
- Automatically normalized (if $\phi_i$ orthonormal)

**Example** ($N = 2$):

$$\Psi(1,2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\phi_1(1)\phi_2(2) - \phi_1(2)\phi_2(1)]$$ ✓

**Answer:** Compact notation for antisymmetrized $N$-particle state

### Example 8: Helium Ground State

Two electrons in $1s$ orbital of helium ($Z = 2$).

**Solution:**

**Spatial**: Both in $\phi_{100}(r) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi a_0^3}}e^{-2r/a_0}$ (symmetric)

**Spin**: Must be antisymmetric (singlet)

$$\chi_{\text{singlet}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle - |\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)$$

**Total**:

$$\Psi(1,2) = \phi_{100}(r_1)\phi_{100}(r_2) \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle - |\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)$$

**Configuration**: $1s^2$ (closed shell, $S = 0$)

**Answer:** Ground state is para-helium (total spin zero)

### Example 9: Helium Excited States

One electron in $1s$, other in $2s$. What are possible states?

**Solution:**

Spatial: $\phi_{100}(1)\phi_{200}(2)$ can be symmetric or antisymmetric.

**Ortho-helium** (triplet, $S = 1$): Spatial antisymmetric

$$\psi_{\text{space}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\phi_{100}(1)\phi_{200}(2) - \phi_{100}(2)\phi_{200}(1)]$$

Spin symmetric: $|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle$, $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle + |\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)$, $|\downarrow\downarrow\rangle$

**Para-helium** (singlet, $S = 0$): Spatial symmetric

$$\psi_{\text{space}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\phi_{100}(1)\phi_{200}(2) + \phi_{100}(2)\phi_{200}(1)]$$

Spin antisymmetric: $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle - |\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)$

**Energy**: Ortho (triplet) lies **lower** due to exchange integral $K > 0$.

**Answer:** Ortho (triplet) and para (singlet) with different energies

## Bosons vs. Fermions

### Example 10: Bose-Einstein Statistics

For bosons, symmetric wavefunction allows:

**Solution:**

**Multiple occupation**: Any number of bosons in same state.

**Example**: Photons in laser (coherent state)

$$|n_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle = \frac{(\hat{a}^\dagger_{\mathbf{k}})^n}{\sqrt{n!}}|0\rangle$$

No limit on $n$ (occupation number).

**Bose-Einstein condensation**: Macroscopic number in ground state below critical temperature.

**Answer:** Bosons "like" to bunch together (enhances occupation)

### Example 11: Fermi-Dirac Statistics

For fermions, antisymmetric wavefunction requires:

**Solution:**

**Occupation number**: $n = 0$ or $1$ only (Pauli exclusion).

**Ground state**: Fill lowest $N$ states (Fermi sea).

**Fermi energy** $E_F$: Highest occupied level at $T = 0$.

**Degeneracy pressure**: Fermions resist compression (uncertainty principle + exclusion).

**Applications**: White dwarfs, neutron stars (supported by electron/neutron degeneracy pressure).

**Answer:** Fermions fill states one-by-one (creates energy level structure)

### Example 12: Identical Bosons in Harmonic Trap

Three identical bosons in 1D harmonic oscillator. Find ground state.

**Solution:**

**Ground state**: All three in $n = 0$ (allowed for bosons!).

$$\Psi_{\text{ground}} = \phi_0(x_1)\phi_0(x_2)\phi_0(x_3)$$

(Symmetric under all permutations)

**Energy**: $E = 3 \times \frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega = \frac{3}{2}\hbar\omega$

**Answer:** All bosons in lowest state (condensation)

### Example 13: Identical Fermions in Harmonic Trap

Three identical fermions (spin-1/2) in 1D harmonic oscillator. Find ground state.

**Solution:**

Must fill states respecting Pauli exclusion.

**Spin**: Each orbital can hold 2 fermions (spin up and down).

**Configuration**: $n = 0$ (2 fermions), $n = 1$ (1 fermion)

$$\Psi_{\text{ground}} = \text{Slater determinant of } \phi_0\uparrow, \phi_0\downarrow, \phi_1\uparrow$$

**Energy**: $E = 2 \times \frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega + 1 \times \frac{3}{2}\hbar\omega = \frac{5}{2}\hbar\omega$

Higher than bosons! (Pauli pressure)

**Answer:** Fill states one-by-one with two spin states per orbital

**Problem 1: B**
$\hat{P}_{12}^2 = \mathbb{1}$ (swap twice = identity)
Eigenvalues: $\lambda^2 = 1 \Rightarrow \lambda = \pm 1$
$+1$: symmetric (bosons)
$-1$: antisymmetric (fermions)
No other values possible

**Problem 2: C**
Fermions only (half-integer spin)
Antisymmetric wavefunction → no two in same state
Bosons: can multiple-occupy
Foundation of periodic table
Explains atomic structure, chemistry

**Problem 3: A**
Ground state: $1s^2$ configuration
Spatial symmetric (both in same orbital)
Spin must be antisymmetric (singlet)
$S = 0$ (para-helium)

$K = \int\int\phi_a^*(1)\phi_b^*(2)V(r_{12})\phi_b(1)\phi_a(2)$
For repulsive $V(r_{12}) > 0$: $K > 0$
Purely quantum (no classical analog)
Lowers triplet energy relative to singlet
Hund's rules: maximize spin
