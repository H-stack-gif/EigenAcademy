# Quantum Mechanics Topic 5.3: Zeeman and Stark Effects

When atoms are placed in external fields, their energy levels shift and split—a powerful probe of atomic structure. The **Zeeman effect** describes atoms in magnetic fields: electron magnetic moments couple to $\mathbf{B}$, splitting degenerate levels. The **Stark effect** describes atoms in electric fields: the field polarizes the atom, shifting energies. Both effects reveal internal atomic structure and test quantum mechanical predictions. They're also practically crucial: Zeeman splitting enables magnetic resonance imaging and nuclear magnetic resonance, while Stark shifts control atoms in optical lattices and quantum computing.

The Zeeman effect comes in two regimes. **Weak field** (Zeeman splitting $\ll$ fine structure): total angular momentum $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{L} + \mathbf{S}$ is good quantum number, levels split proportional to $m_j$ with an effective **Landé g-factor** that depends on $l, s, j$. **Strong field** (Paschen-Back regime): field decouples $\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{S}$, both precess independently, and splitting is proportional to $m_l + 2m_s$. The transition between regimes illustrates competing interactions—internal spin-orbit coupling versus external field.

This article explores both Zeeman and Stark effects systematically. We'll derive the Landé formula, understand weak versus strong field regimes, see why hydrogen ground state has no linear Stark effect (requiring second-order), and compute the dramatic linear Stark splitting for $n = 2$ from degeneracy. These effects demonstrate degenerate perturbation theory in action and connect quantum mechanics to experimental spectroscopy.

## The Zeeman Effect: Atoms in Magnetic Fields

An electron has both orbital and spin magnetic moments:

$$\boldsymbol{\mu}_L = -\frac{e}{2m}\mathbf{L}, \quad \boldsymbol{\mu}_S = -g_s\frac{e}{2m}\mathbf{S}$$

where $g_s \approx 2$ is the spin g-factor. In magnetic field $\mathbf{B} = B\hat{z}$:

$$\hat{V} = -(\boldsymbol{\mu}_L + \boldsymbol{\mu}_S) \cdot \mathbf{B} = \frac{eB}{2m}(\hat{L}_z + 2\hat{S}_z) = \mu_B B(\hat{L}_z + 2\hat{S}_z)$$

where $\mu_B = e\hbar/(2m) = 5.79 \times 10^{-5}$ eV/T is the **Bohr magneton**.

### Example 1: Weak Field Zeeman (LS Coupling)

When $\mu_B B \ll$ fine structure splitting, $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{L} + \mathbf{S}$ remains good quantum number. Express $\hat{L}_z + 2\hat{S}_z$ in terms of $\hat{J}_z$.

**Solution:**

Need $\hat{S}_z$ in terms of $\mathbf{J}$. Project $\mathbf{S}$ onto $\mathbf{J}$:

$$\mathbf{S} = (\mathbf{S} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{J}})\hat{\mathbf{J}} + \mathbf{S}_\perp$$

The perpendicular component $\mathbf{S}_\perp$ precesses rapidly around $\mathbf{J}$ and averages to zero.

$$\mathbf{S} \cdot \mathbf{J} = \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{J}^2 + \mathbf{S}^2 - \mathbf{L}^2)$$

$$\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \frac{\mathbf{J}^2 + \mathbf{S}^2 - \mathbf{L}^2}{2\mathbf{J}^2}\mathbf{J}$$

Similarly: $\langle\mathbf{L}\rangle = \frac{\mathbf{J}^2 - \mathbf{S}^2 + \mathbf{L}^2}{2\mathbf{J}^2}\mathbf{J}$

$$\langle\hat{L}_z + 2\hat{S}_z\rangle = \left(\frac{J^2 - S^2 + L^2}{2J^2} + 2\frac{J^2 + S^2 - L^2}{2J^2}\right)J_z$$

$$= \frac{3J^2 + S^2 - L^2}{2J^2}J_z = g_J J_z$$

where **Landé g-factor**:
$$g_J = 1 + \frac{j(j+1) + s(s+1) - l(l+1)}{2j(j+1)}$$

**Answer:** $\langle\hat{V}\rangle = \mu_B B g_J m_j$ with Landé g-factor

### Example 2: Computing Landé g-Factor

For $^2P_{3/2}$ state (meaning $l=1, s=1/2, j=3/2$), compute $g_J$.

**Solution:**

$$g_J = 1 + \frac{j(j+1) + s(s+1) - l(l+1)}{2j(j+1)}$$

$$= 1 + \frac{(3/2)(5/2) + (1/2)(3/2) - (1)(2)}{2(3/2)(5/2)}$$

$$= 1 + \frac{15/4 + 3/4 - 8/4}{15/2} = 1 + \frac{10/4}{15/2} = 1 + \frac{10/4 \cdot 2/15} = 1 + \frac{20}{60} = 1 + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{4}{3}$$

**Answer:** $g_J = 4/3$ for $^2P_{3/2}$

### Example 3: Zeeman Splitting Pattern

The $^2P_{3/2}$ level has $m_j = -3/2, -1/2, +1/2, +3/2$ (four states). In weak magnetic field, into how many levels does it split?

**Solution:**

Energy shift: $\Delta E = \mu_B B g_J m_j = \frac{4}{3}\mu_B B m_j$

For four different $m_j$ values: **four distinct energy levels**

Splitting between adjacent levels: $\Delta E = \frac{4}{3}\mu_B B$

Pattern:
- $m_j = -3/2$: $E = -2\mu_B B$
- $m_j = -1/2$: $E = -\frac{2}{3}\mu_B B$
- $m_j = +1/2$: $E = +\frac{2}{3}\mu_B B$
- $m_j = +3/2$: $E = +2\mu_B B$

**Experimental signature:** Four equally spaced lines (in spectroscopy)

**Answer:** Splits into 4 levels, equally spaced by $\frac{4}{3}\mu_B B$

### Example 4: Strong Field (Paschen-Back Effect)

When $\mu_B B \gg$ fine structure, spin-orbit coupling is negligible compared to Zeeman. Now $m_l$ and $m_s$ are good quantum numbers.

For this regime, what is the energy shift?

**Solution:**

Perturbation: $\hat{V} = \mu_B B(\hat{L}_z + 2\hat{S}_z)$

Since $\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{S}$ precess independently around $\mathbf{B}$:

$$\Delta E = \mu_B B(m_l + 2m_s)$$

No g-factor! Simple sum.

For $l = 1, s = 1/2$:
- $m_l = -1, 0, +1$
- $m_s = -1/2, +1/2$

Six combinations give energy shifts:
$$\Delta E = \mu_B B(m_l + 2m_s)$$

Example: $(m_l = 1, m_s = 1/2)$: $\Delta E = \mu_B B(1 + 1) = 2\mu_B B$

**Answer:** Strong field: $\Delta E = \mu_B B(m_l + 2m_s)$ (no Landé factor)

### Example 5: Transition Between Regimes

Estimate the magnetic field strength at which hydrogen $n = 2$ transitions from weak to strong Zeeman regime.

**Solution:**

Fine structure splitting (from Topic 5.2): $\Delta E_{FS} \sim \alpha^2 |E_2| \sim 10^{-5}$ eV

Weak regime: $\mu_B B \ll \Delta E_{FS}$

Strong regime: $\mu_B B \gg \Delta E_{FS}$

Crossover: $\mu_B B \sim 10^{-5}$ eV

$$B \sim \frac{10^{-5} \text{ eV}}{\mu_B} = \frac{10^{-5}}{5.79 \times 10^{-5} \text{ eV/T}} \approx 0.17 \text{ T}$$

Laboratory fields: $\sim 1$ T easily achievable → can reach strong field regime.

**Answer:** Crossover at $\sim 0.1$ T; accessible experimentally

### Example 6: Hydrogen Ground State Zeeman

The hydrogen $1s$ state has $l = 0, s = 1/2, j = 1/2$. Compute the Zeeman splitting.

**Solution:**

Since $l = 0$: $\hat{L}_z = 0$, so $\hat{V} = \mu_B B \cdot 2\hat{S}_z = 2\mu_B B \hat{S}_z$

$$\Delta E = 2\mu_B B m_s = 2\mu_B B \cdot (\pm 1/2) = \pm \mu_B B$$

**Or using Landé formula:**

$$g_J = 1 + \frac{(1/2)(3/2) + (1/2)(3/2) - 0}{2(1/2)(3/2)} = 1 + \frac{3/2}{3/2} = 2$$

$$\Delta E = g_J \mu_B B m_j = 2\mu_B B (\pm 1/2) = \pm \mu_B B$$ ✓

**Physical meaning:** Pure spin splitting; two levels separated by $2\mu_B B$.

**Answer:** Splits into two levels: $E = \pm\mu_B B$

## The Stark Effect: Atoms in Electric Fields

Electric field $\mathcal{E}$ along $z$ gives perturbation:

$$\hat{V} = -e\mathcal{E}z = -e\mathcal{E}r\cos\theta$$

### Example 7: Why No Linear Stark for Ground State

The hydrogen ground state $|1,0,0\rangle$ has no linear Stark effect. Why?

**Solution:**

First order: $E^{(1)} = \langle 1,0,0|(-e\mathcal{E}z)|1,0,0\rangle$

The $1s$ wavefunction has **definite parity** (even): $\psi_{1s}(-\mathbf{r}) = \psi_{1s}(\mathbf{r})$

Operator $z$ is odd: $z(-\mathbf{r}) = -z(\mathbf{r})$

Integrand: $\psi_{1s}^* z \psi_{1s}$ is odd function

$$\int_{\text{all space}} (\text{odd function}) = 0$$

Therefore: $E^{(1)} = 0$

**Physical interpretation:** Spherically symmetric state has no preferred direction; cannot have linear shift.

**Answer:** Parity forbids linear Stark; must go to second order

### Example 8: Quadratic Stark (Polarizability)

For hydrogen ground state, second-order Stark gives:

$$E^{(2)} = -\frac{1}{2}\alpha \mathcal{E}^2$$

where $\alpha$ is the **polarizability**. This is always negative (field lowers energy by polarizing atom). Why?

**Solution:**

Second-order formula:
$$E_{1s}^{(2)} = \sum_{n \neq 1}\frac{|\langle n,l,m|(-e\mathcal{E}z)|1,0,0\rangle|^2}{E_1 - E_n}$$

All excited states: $E_n > E_1$, so all denominators negative.

Numerators: positive (squared matrix elements)

Therefore: $E^{(2)} < 0$

**Physical interpretation:** Electric field polarizes atom, inducing dipole moment proportional to $\mathcal{E}$. Interaction energy $-\mathbf{d} \cdot \mathbf{E} \propto -\mathcal{E}^2$ lowers energy.

**Answer:** All denominators negative → $E^{(2)} < 0$ (polarization energy)

### Example 9: Linear Stark for $n = 2$

The $n = 2$ level is degenerate. States with $m = 0$ can mix: $|2,0,0\rangle$ and $|2,1,0\rangle$ couple.

Construct the $2 \times 2$ perturbation matrix.

**Solution:**

$$W = \begin{pmatrix} \langle 2s|(-e\mathcal{E}z)|2s\rangle & \langle 2s|(-e\mathcal{E}z)|2p_0\rangle \\ \langle 2p_0|(-e\mathcal{E}z)|2s\rangle & \langle 2p_0|(-e\mathcal{E}z)|2p_0\rangle \end{pmatrix}$$

**Diagonal elements:** Zero (both states have definite parity)

**Off-diagonal:** $\langle 2s|z|2p_0\rangle = -3a_0$ (standard integral)

$$W = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 3e\mathcal{E}a_0 \\ 3e\mathcal{E}a_0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

Eigenvalues: $\lambda = \pm 3e\mathcal{E}a_0$

**Answer:** Linear Stark splitting: $E^{(1)} = \pm 3ea_0\mathcal{E}$ for $m=0$ states

### Example 10: Stark Eigenstates

Find the eigenstates corresponding to eigenvalues $\pm 3ea_0\mathcal{E}$.

**Solution:**

For $\lambda_+ = 3ea_0\mathcal{E}$: Eigenvector equation gives equal coefficients

$$|\psi_+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|2s\rangle + |2p_0\rangle)$$

For $\lambda_- = -3ea_0\mathcal{E}$: Opposite sign

$$|\psi_-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|2s\rangle - |2p_0\rangle)$$

**Physical interpretation:**
- $|\psi_+\rangle$: Electron density shifted toward $+z$ (along field)
- $|\psi_-\rangle$: Electron density shifted toward $-z$ (against field)

These have **permanent induced dipole moments**.

**Answer:** $|\psi_{\pm}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|2s\rangle \pm |2p_0\rangle)$ with $E^{(1)} = \pm 3ea_0\mathcal{E}$

### Example 11: Stark Effect for $m = \pm 1$ States

What happens to $|2,1,\pm 1\rangle$ states under Stark perturbation?

**Solution:**

Electric field along $z$ preserves $m$ (rotation symmetry around $z$).

States $|2,1,1\rangle$ and $|2,1,-1\rangle$ **cannot mix** with anything else in $n=2$ manifold:
- Can't mix with $|2,0,0\rangle$ (different $m$)
- Can't mix with $|2,1,0\rangle$ (different $m$)
- Can't mix with each other (selection rule: $\Delta m = 0$, but already same $m$)

They remain **unshifted** to first order:
$$E^{(1)} = \langle 2,1,\pm 1|(-e\mathcal{E}z)|2,1,\pm 1\rangle = 0$$ (parity)

Get only **quadratic Stark** (second order).

**Answer:** $m = \pm 1$ states unshifted in first order; only quadratic Stark

### Example 12: Stark Effect Spectroscopy

In Stark spectroscopy, we measure how spectral lines split in electric field. For $n=2$ to $n=1$ transition in hydrogen, how many lines appear?

**Solution:**

**Without field:** Single line $2p \to 1s$ (ignoring fine structure)

**With field:**
- $n=2, m=0$: Two states $|\psi_\pm\rangle$ with energies $E_2 \pm 3ea_0\mathcal{E}$
- $n=2, m=\pm 1$: Two states (unshifted to first order)
- $n=1$: Ground state (quadratic shift, negligible)

Selection rule: $\Delta m = 0, \pm 1$

Transitions:
1. $|\psi_+\rangle (m=0) \to |1s\rangle$: Energy $\hbar\omega_0 + 3ea_0\mathcal{E}$
2. $|\psi_-\rangle (m=0) \to |1s\rangle$: Energy $\hbar\omega_0 - 3ea_0\mathcal{E}$
3. $|2,1,0\rangle \to |1s\rangle$: Energy $\hbar\omega_0$ (unshifted)

**Three lines** (assuming $\pi$ polarization, $\Delta m = 0$)

**Answer:** Three lines in Stark spectrum for $n=2 \to n=1$

### Example 13: Stark vs. Zeeman Comparison

Compare selection rules and splitting patterns for Stark and Zeeman effects.

**Solution:**

| Feature | Zeeman | Stark |
|---------|--------|-------|
| Perturbation | $\mu_B B(L_z + 2S_z)$ | $-e\mathcal{E}z$ |
| Good QN | $m_j$ (weak) or $m_l, m_s$ (strong) | $m$ (always) |
| Parity | Even | Odd |
| Ground state | Linear (spin) | Quadratic (no dipole) |
| Degeneracy lift | Always | Only if opposite parity states degenerate |
| Typical size | $\sim \mu_B B$ | $\sim ea_0\mathcal{E}$ (if linear) |

**Key difference:** Zeeman always linear in $B$; Stark linear only for degenerate opposite-parity states.

**Answer:** Zeeman simpler (always linear); Stark depends on degeneracy

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
For $l=0$ (no orbital angular momentum): $g_J = 1 + \frac{j(j+1) + s(s+1) - l(l+1)}{2j(j+1)} = 1 + \frac{(1/2)(3/2) + (1/2)(3/2) - 0}{2(1/2)(3/2)} = 1 + 1 = 2$
Pure spin: $g_J = 2$ (from $g_s \approx 2$). Alternatively: $\hat{V} = \mu_B B(L_z + 2S_z) = 2\mu_B B S_z$ for $l=0$, giving $\Delta E = 2\mu_B B m_s$.

**Problem 2: B**
Linear Stark requires $\langle n|z|n\rangle \neq 0$, but this vanishes by parity for single state. Need **degenerate** states of **opposite parity** that can mix. Example: hydrogen $n=2$ has $|2s\rangle$ (even) and $|2p_0\rangle$ (odd) degenerate. Electric field couples them: $\langle 2s|z|2p_0\rangle = -3a_0 \neq 0$, giving linear splitting $\pm 3ea_0\mathcal{E}$. Ground state and non-degenerate excited states: only quadratic Stark.

**Problem 3: C**
Paschen-Back regime: $\mu_B B \gg$ fine structure. External magnetic field dominates over internal spin-orbit coupling. $\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{S}$ precess **independently** around $\mathbf{B}$ rather than coupling to form $\mathbf{J}$. Good quantum numbers: $(m_l, m_s)$ instead of $m_j$. Energy: $\Delta E = \mu_B B(m_l + 2m_s)$—simple addition, no Landé factor. Transition from weak (LS coupling) to strong field is continuous crossover.

**Problem 4: B**
Hydrogen $1s$: spherically symmetric, definite parity (even). First-order: $\langle 1s|z|1s\rangle = 0$ (odd operator, even state). **No linear Stark**. Second-order: virtual transitions to $np$ states (opposite parity). $E^{(2)} = -\frac{1}{2}\alpha\mathcal{E}^2$ where $\alpha \approx 9/2 \times (4\pi\epsilon_0)a_0^3$ is polarizability. **Quadratic Stark**: field polarizes atom, induced dipole lowers energy. All non-degenerate states have quadratic Stark.

**Problem 5: B**
Bohr magneton: $\mu_B = \frac{e\hbar}{2m_e} = 5.788 \times 10^{-5}$ eV/T. Natural unit of atomic magnetic moments. Orbital contribution: $\boldsymbol{\mu}_L = -\mu_B \mathbf{L}/\hbar$. Spin contribution: $\boldsymbol{\mu}_S = -g_s\mu_B\mathbf{S}/\hbar$ with $g_s \approx 2$. Sets scale for Zeeman splitting: $\Delta E \sim \mu_B B$. At $B = 1$ T: $\Delta E \sim 10^{-4}$ eV (measurable). Analogous to nuclear magneton $\mu_N = e\hbar/(2m_p)$ for nuclei.
