# Quantum Mechanics Topic 5.4: Hyperfine Structure

Beyond electronic structure lies a subtler level splitting: **hyperfine structure**, arising from the interaction between the electron's magnetic field and the nuclear magnetic moment. Whereas fine structure corrections are $\sim 10^{-5}$ eV, hyperfine splittings are typically $\sim 10^{-6}$ eV—smaller by roughly the electron-to-proton mass ratio. Yet this tiny effect has profound consequences: the 21-cm line from hydrogen's hyperfine transition maps our galaxy, atomic clocks based on hyperfine transitions define the second, and hyperfine structure provides detailed information about nuclear properties.

The physics is beautiful in its simplicity. The nucleus has spin $\mathbf{I}$ and an associated magnetic moment. The electron's orbital motion and spin create a magnetic field at the nucleus. The interaction energy is $\mathbf{B} \cdot \boldsymbol{\mu}_I \propto \mathbf{I} \cdot \mathbf{J}$, coupling nuclear and electronic angular momentum. Just as spin-orbit coupling led to total electronic angular momentum $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{L} + \mathbf{S}$, hyperfine coupling creates **total atomic angular momentum** $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{I} + \mathbf{J}$. The quantum number $F$ labels the energy levels, and transitions between hyperfine levels are extremely sharp—ideal for precision measurements.

This article develops the theory of hyperfine structure and explores its applications. We'll derive the hyperfine Hamiltonian, compute energy splittings using angular momentum coupling, understand the famous 21-cm line, and see why hyperfine transitions power atomic clocks. Hyperfine structure connects nuclear physics to atomic physics and demonstrates how even "small" quantum effects can have enormous practical importance.

## The Hyperfine Interaction

The hyperfine Hamiltonian for magnetic dipole interaction is:

$$\hat{H}_{hf} = A\mathbf{I} \cdot \mathbf{J}$$

where $A$ is the **hyperfine constant** (depends on nucleus and electronic state) and $\mathbf{I}$ is nuclear spin.

### Example 1: Origin of Hyperfine Coupling

The nucleus has magnetic moment $\boldsymbol{\mu}_I = g_I\mu_N\mathbf{I}$ where $\mu_N = e\hbar/(2m_p)$ is the nuclear magneton and $g_I$ is nuclear g-factor.

Electrons create magnetic field at nucleus. For $s$-electrons (which penetrate to $r=0$):

$$\mathbf{B}(0) = \frac{8\pi}{3}\mu_0 g_s\mu_B\langle\mathbf{S}\delta^3(\mathbf{r})\rangle$$

Interaction energy: $E = -\boldsymbol{\mu}_I \cdot \mathbf{B}$. For $l \neq 0$ (non-penetrating):

$$\mathbf{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\left[\frac{3(\boldsymbol{\mu}_e \cdot \hat{r})\hat{r} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_e}{r^3}\right]$$

Both give $\hat{H}_{hf} \propto \mathbf{I} \cdot \mathbf{J}$.

**Answer:** Nuclear moment interacts with electron's magnetic field

### Example 2: Total Angular Momentum

Hyperfine coupling means $\mathbf{I}$ and $\mathbf{J}$ couple to form:

$$\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{I} + \mathbf{J}$$

with quantum number $F$ ranging from $|I - J|$ to $I + J$ in integer steps.

For hydrogen ground state: $I = 1/2$ (proton), $J = 1/2$ (electron). What are possible $F$ values?

**Solution:**

$$F = |I - J|, |I - J| + 1, \ldots, I + J$$

$$F = |1/2 - 1/2|, \ldots, 1/2 + 1/2 = 0, 1$$

Two levels:
- $F = 0$: **Singlet** (antiparallel)
- $F = 1$: **Triplet** (parallel, with $m_F = -1, 0, +1$)

**Physical meaning:** Nuclear and electronic spins can be parallel or antiparallel.

**Answer:** $F = 0$ (singlet) and $F = 1$ (triplet)

### Example 3: Energy Formula

Using $\mathbf{F}^2 = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{J})^2 = \mathbf{I}^2 + \mathbf{J}^2 + 2\mathbf{I} \cdot \mathbf{J}$:

$$\mathbf{I} \cdot \mathbf{J} = \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{F}^2 - \mathbf{I}^2 - \mathbf{J}^2)$$

Eigenvalue:
$$\langle\mathbf{I} \cdot \mathbf{J}\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}{2}[F(F+1) - I(I+1) - J(J+1)]$$

Energy:
$$E_F = \frac{A}{2}[F(F+1) - I(I+1) - J(J+1)]$$

For hydrogen ground state ($I = J = 1/2$), compute energy difference between $F = 1$ and $F = 0$.

**Solution:**

$$E_{F=1} = \frac{A}{2}[1(2) - (1/2)(3/2) - (1/2)(3/2)] = \frac{A}{2}[2 - 3/2] = \frac{A}{4}$$

$$E_{F=0} = \frac{A}{2}[0 - 3/4 - 3/4] = -\frac{3A}{4}$$

**Splitting:**
$$\Delta E = E_{F=1} - E_{F=0} = \frac{A}{4} - (-\frac{3A}{4}) = A$$

**Physical interpretation:** Parallel configuration (triplet) higher energy than antiparallel (singlet) if $A > 0$.

**Answer:** $\Delta E = A$ between triplet and singlet

### Example 4: The Hyperfine Constant

For hydrogen ground state, the hyperfine constant is:

$$A = \frac{16}{3}\mu_0 g_I\mu_N g_s\mu_B |\psi_{1s}(0)|^2$$

where $|\psi_{1s}(0)|^2 = 1/(\pi a_0^3)$. This gives $A/(h\hbar) = 1420$ MHz. Why is $A$ proportional to $|\psi(0)|^2$?

**Solution:**

Hyperfine interaction strongest when electron is **at nucleus** ($r = 0$).

For $s$-states: wavefunction non-zero at origin, giving **Fermi contact** interaction.

Magnetic field from electron spin at nucleus:
$$\mathbf{B}(0) \propto \langle\mathbf{S}\delta^3(\mathbf{r})\rangle = |\psi(0)|^2\mathbf{S}$$

Larger $|\psi(0)|^2$ → stronger field → larger hyperfine splitting.

For $l \neq 0$: $\psi(0) = 0$ (angular momentum barrier), so Fermi contact absent. Different (weaker) dipole-dipole mechanism.

**Answer:** Fermi contact interaction $\propto |\psi(0)|^2$ for $s$-states

### Example 5: Hyperfine Splitting for Other States

For hydrogen $2s$ state ($n=2, l=0, j=1/2$), the hyperfine splitting is:

$$\Delta E_{2s} = \frac{A_{2s}}{4}$$

where $A_{2s} = A_{1s} \cdot |\psi_{2s}(0)|^2/|\psi_{1s}(0)|^2$.

Since $|\psi_{2s}(0)|^2 = 1/(8\pi a_0^3) = |\psi_{1s}(0)|^2/8$:

$$A_{2s} = A_{1s}/8$$

What is the frequency of the $2s$ hyperfine transition?

**Solution:**

$$\Delta E_{2s} = A_{1s}/8$$

$$\nu_{2s} = \frac{\Delta E_{2s}}{h} = \frac{A_{1s}}{8h} = \frac{1420 \text{ MHz}}{8} = 177.5 \text{ MHz}$$

**Physical meaning:** Higher $n$ → more extended wavefunction → smaller $|\psi(0)|^2$ → weaker hyperfine interaction.

**Answer:** $\nu_{2s} \approx 178$ MHz (weaker than ground state)

## The 21-cm Line

### Example 6: The 21-cm Transition

The hydrogen ground state hyperfine transition ($F = 1 \to F = 0$) has:

$$\Delta E = A = h \times 1420.405751 \text{ MHz}$$

This corresponds to wavelength:
$$\lambda = \frac{c}{\nu} = \frac{3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}}{1.42 \times 10^9 \text{ Hz}} = 21.1 \text{ cm}$$

Why is this transition so important in radio astronomy?

**Solution:**

**Ubiquity:** Hydrogen is most abundant element in universe

**Penetration:** Radio waves pass through dust (unlike optical)

**Narrow line:** Hyperfine transition extremely sharp ($\sim$ MHz natural width)

**Doppler mapping:** Velocity information from Doppler shift reveals galactic rotation, structure

**Applications:**
- Map spiral arms of Milky Way
- Detect neutral hydrogen in galaxies
- Measure galactic rotation curves
- Probe dark matter distribution

**Answer:** 21-cm line maps neutral hydrogen throughout universe

### Example 7: Selection Rules for 21-cm Transition

The transition is $F = 1 \to F = 0$ with $\Delta F = 1$. What are the selection rules?

**Solution:**

Magnetic dipole transition (not electric dipole—requires spin flip).

Selection rules:
- $\Delta F = 0, \pm 1$ (but $F = 0 \not\leftrightarrow F' = 0$)
- $\Delta m_F = 0, \pm 1$

For $F = 1 \to F = 0$:
- Initial: $m_F = -1, 0, +1$ (three substates)
- Final: $m_F = 0$ (one state)

Three transitions possible (different polarizations).

**Transition rate:** Extremely slow (forbidden by parity and spin selection).

Lifetime: $\tau \sim 10^7$ years! (metastable state)

Despite slow rate, observable in space due to vast amounts of hydrogen.

**Answer:** Magnetic dipole, $\Delta F = 1$; extremely slow (metastable)

### Example 8: Temperature of 21-cm Line

At thermal equilibrium, the population ratio is:

$$\frac{n_{F=1}}{n_{F=0}} = \frac{g_1}{g_0}e^{-\Delta E/(k_BT)}$$

where $g_1 = 3$ (triplet), $g_0 = 1$ (singlet).

At typical interstellar temperature $T = 100$ K, what is the population ratio?

**Solution:**

$$\Delta E = h \times 1420 \text{ MHz} = 5.9 \times 10^{-6} \text{ eV}$$

$$k_B T = 8.6 \times 10^{-5} \text{ eV/K} \times 100 \text{ K} = 8.6 \times 10^{-3} \text{ eV}$$

$$\frac{\Delta E}{k_BT} = \frac{5.9 \times 10^{-6}}{8.6 \times 10^{-3}} \approx 6.9 \times 10^{-4} \ll 1$$

$$\frac{n_1}{n_0} = 3e^{-0.00069} \approx 3(1 - 0.00069) \approx 3$$

**High temperature limit:** Levels essentially equally populated (slight excess in triplet due to degeneracy).

**Answer:** Nearly thermal equilibrium; ratio $\approx 3$ from statistical weight

## Atomic Clocks

### Example 9: Why Hyperfine Transitions for Clocks?

Atomic clocks use hyperfine transitions (e.g., Cs-133: $6^2S_{1/2}$, $F=4 \leftrightarrow F=3$). Why not use optical transitions?

**Solution:**

**Advantages of hyperfine:**

1. **Narrow linewidth:** Transition extremely sharp (natural width $\sim$ Hz), allowing precise frequency measurement
2. **Insensitive to external fields:** First-order Zeeman/Stark small for ground state
3. **Long-lived:** Metastable states reduce systematic errors
4. **Reproducible:** Frequency depends only on fundamental constants and nuclear structure

**Optical disadvantages:**
- Broader lines
- More sensitive to environmental perturbations
- Shorter lifetimes

**Answer:** Hyperfine transitions narrower, more stable, better for clocks

### Example 10: Cesium-133 Atomic Clock

The SI second is defined by Cs-133 hyperfine transition:

$$1 \text{ second} = 9,192,631,770 \text{ periods of Cs-133 transition}$$

This is $F = 4 \to F = 3$ transition of $6S_{1/2}$ ground state.

For Cs: $I = 7/2$ (nuclear spin), $J = 1/2$ (electronic). What are the $F$ values?

**Solution:**

$$F = |I - J| \text{ to } I + J$$

$$F = |7/2 - 1/2| \text{ to } 7/2 + 1/2 = 3 \text{ to } 4$$

Two levels: $F = 3$ and $F = 4$

Transition frequency: $\nu = 9.192631770$ GHz (microwave)

**Accuracy:** Modern atomic clocks: $\sim 10^{-16}$ fractional frequency stability

**Applications:** GPS, telecommunications, fundamental constants measurements

**Answer:** $F = 3$ and $F = 4$; transition defines the second

### Example 11: Magnetic Field Perturbation

Atomic clocks must shield from magnetic fields. A weak field $B$ causes Zeeman shift:

$$\Delta E = g_F\mu_B B m_F$$

For Cs $F = 4, m_F = 0$ state (used in clocks), what is $g_F$?

**Solution:**

Similar to electronic Landé factor, but for $\mathbf{F}$:

$$g_F = g_J\frac{F(F+1) + J(J+1) - I(I+1)}{2F(F+1)}$$

For $m_F = 0$ state: **First-order Zeeman shift is zero** (by design).

Clock uses $m_F = 0$ transition to minimize field sensitivity.

Second-order shift: $\propto B^2$, much smaller.

**Shielding:** Magnetic shields reduce $B$ to nT levels.

**Answer:** $m_F = 0$ state chosen to eliminate first-order field shift

### Example 12: Comparison of Hyperfine and Fine Structure

Compare magnitudes:

**Solution:**

**Fine structure:**
$$\Delta E_{FS} \sim \alpha^2 \times \text{(binding energy)} \sim 10^{-5} \text{ eV}$$

$$\nu_{FS} \sim 10^{10} \text{ Hz (10 GHz)}$$

**Hyperfine structure:**
$$\Delta E_{hf} \sim \frac{m_e}{m_p} \times \Delta E_{FS} \sim 10^{-6} \text{ eV}$$

$$\nu_{hf} \sim 10^9 \text{ Hz (1 GHz)}$$

**Ratio:**
$$\frac{\Delta E_{hf}}{\Delta E_{FS}} \sim \frac{m_e}{m_p} \approx \frac{1}{1836}$$

**Physical origin of ratio:** Nuclear magnetic moment $\sim \mu_N = \mu_B(m_e/m_p)$ is smaller than electronic $\mu_B$ by mass ratio.

**Answer:** Hyperfine $\sim 10^{-3} \times$ fine structure (mass ratio)

### Example 13: Hyperfine Constant and Nuclear Moments

The hyperfine constant $A$ is proportional to the nuclear magnetic moment:

$$A \propto g_I\mu_N|\psi(0)|^2$$

Different isotopes have different $g_I$. This allows **isotope identification** via hyperfine spectroscopy.

For hydrogen isotopes:
- $^1$H: $I = 1/2$, $g_I = 5.59$
- $^2$H (deuterium): $I = 1$, $g_I = 0.86$
- $^3$H (tritium): $I = 1/2$, $g_I = 5.96$

How do hyperfine splittings compare?

**Solution:**

For same electronic state, $A \propto g_I$.

Deuterium has $I = 1$ → different $F$ structure:
- $J = 1/2$, $I = 1$ → $F = 1/2, 3/2$

Splittings depend on both $g_I$ and $I$.

**Applications:** Isotope shifts in spectroscopy reveal nuclear structure.

**Answer:** Different isotopes have different hyperfine patterns (nuclear structure probe)

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Hyperfine structure: interaction between **nuclear magnetic moment** and **magnetic field from electrons**. Nuclear spin $\mathbf{I}$ couples to electronic angular momentum $\mathbf{J}$ via $\hat{H}_{hf} = A\mathbf{I} \cdot \mathbf{J}$. For $s$-electrons: **Fermi contact** interaction $\propto |\psi(0)|^2$. For $l \neq 0$: dipole-dipole interaction. Magnitude: $\Delta E \sim \mu_N/\mu_B \times$ fine structure $\sim (m_e/m_p) \times 10^{-5}$ eV $\sim 10^{-6}$ eV. Much smaller than fine structure.

**Problem 2: C**
21-cm line: hyperfine transition in hydrogen ground state, $F=1 \to F=0$. Frequency: $\nu = 1420.405751$ MHz. Wavelength: $\lambda = c/\nu = 21.1$ cm (radio). Nuclear and electronic spins flip from parallel (triplet) to antiparallel (singlet). Magnetic dipole transition, extremely slow (lifetime $\sim 10^7$ years). Observable in astronomy due to vast H abundance. Maps neutral hydrogen in galaxies, reveals galactic structure and rotation.

**Problem 3: C**
Hydrogen: proton $I = 1/2$, electron $J = 1/2$. Total: $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{I} + \mathbf{J}$ with $F = |I-J|$ to $I+J$ in integer steps. $F = |1/2 - 1/2|$ to $1/2 + 1/2$ → $F = 0$ (singlet) or $F = 1$ (triplet). Energy splitting: $\Delta E = A$ where $A$ is hyperfine constant. 21-cm line is $1 \to 0$ transition. For excited states: same $I$, but different $J$ gives different $F$ values.

**Problem 4: B**
Atomic clocks exploit **narrow, stable** hyperfine transitions. Cs-133 standard: $9,192,631,770$ Hz defines the second. Advantages: (1) Natural linewidth extremely small ($\sim$ Hz) → precise frequency, (2) First-order Zeeman shift eliminated by using $m_F = 0$ state, (3) Transition frequency reproducible (depends on fundamental constants), (4) Long coherence time. Modern clocks: fractional stability $\sim 10^{-16}$. Applications: GPS requires $\sim$ ns timing accuracy.

**Problem 5: C**
Hyperfine smaller than fine structure by mass ratio: $\Delta E_{hf}/\Delta E_{FS} \sim m_e/m_p \approx 1/1836$. **Physical reason:** Nuclear magnetic moment $\mu_N = e\hbar/(2m_p)$ smaller than Bohr magneton $\mu_B = e\hbar/(2m_e)$ by $m_e/m_p$. Typical values: Fine structure $\sim 10^{-5}$ eV ($\sim$ GHz), Hyperfine $\sim 10^{-6}$ eV ($\sim$ MHz to GHz). Hydrogen ground state: $\Delta E_{hf} = 5.9 \times 10^{-6}$ eV (1420 MHz).
