# Quantum Mechanics Topic 5.2: Degenerate Perturbation Theory and Fine Structure

When an unperturbed energy level has multiple linearly independent eigenstates—a **degeneracy**—non-degenerate perturbation theory catastrophically fails. The problem is mathematical: first-order formulas contain denominators $E_n^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)}$ which vanish when states share the same energy. But the breakdown is also physical: which linear combination of degenerate states should we perturb? The "correct" states to use aren't the original basis but rather the eigenstates of the perturbation within the degenerate subspace. Degenerate perturbation theory solves both problems by **diagonalizing the perturbation matrix** before applying corrections.

The resolution is elegant: within each degenerate subspace, treat $\hat{V}$ as a finite matrix and find its eigenstates. These "good" states are the proper zeroth-order wavefunctions to perturb. The eigenvalues give first-order energy corrections, lifting the degeneracy. Only after this preliminary diagonalization can we apply standard perturbation formulas. The method is crucial for atomic physics: hydrogen's $n = 2$ level is four-fold degenerate, and relativistic corrections split it into distinct energies depending on total angular momentum.

This article develops degenerate perturbation theory systematically and applies it to hydrogen fine structure—the relativistic corrections that give electrons their richly detailed spectra. We'll learn to identify "good quantum numbers" (those preserved by the perturbation), construct the perturbation matrix, and understand how spin-orbit coupling splits atomic levels. Fine structure corrections are small but measurable, providing crucial tests of quantum electrodynamics and revealing the deep connection between special relativity and quantum mechanics.

## The Degeneracy Problem

### Example 1: Why Non-Degenerate Theory Fails

Consider hydrogen's $n = 2$ level with four degenerate states: $|2,0,0\rangle, |2,1,1\rangle, |2,1,0\rangle, |2,1,-1\rangle$. Why can't we apply non-degenerate formulas directly?

**Solution:**

Non-degenerate first-order formula:
$$|\psi_n^{(1)}\rangle = \sum_{m \neq n}\frac{\langle\psi_m^{(0)}|\hat{V}|\psi_n^{(0)}\rangle}{E_n^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)}}|\psi_m^{(0)}\rangle$$

For states within $n = 2$ manifold: $E_{2,0,0}^{(0)} = E_{2,1,m}^{(0)} = -13.6\text{ eV}/4$

Denominators: $E_n^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)} = 0$ → **division by zero**!

Moreover, any linear combination $\alpha|2,0,0\rangle + \beta|2,1,0\rangle + \cdots$ is equally valid initially. Which one do we perturb?

**Answer:** Zero denominators make formula undefined; choice of basis ambiguous

### Example 2: The Degenerate Subspace

For a $d$-fold degenerate level with energy $E_n^{(0)}$, the degenerate subspace is spanned by $\{|\psi_{n\alpha}^{(0)}\rangle\}$ where $\alpha = 1, 2, \ldots, d$.

The perturbation matrix within this subspace is:
$$W_{\alpha\beta} = \langle\psi_{n\alpha}^{(0)}|\hat{V}|\psi_{n\beta}^{(0)}\rangle$$

Why must we diagonalize this matrix?

**Solution:**

After perturbation, degeneracy is typically lifted—different states get different energies.

The "good" states—those that remain eigenstates to first approximation—are eigenvectors of $W_{\alpha\beta}$:
$$\sum_\beta W_{\alpha\beta}c_\beta^{(k)} = E_n^{(1,k)}c_\alpha^{(k)}$$

The eigenvalues $E_n^{(1,k)}$ are the first-order energy corrections.

The eigenvectors $\{c_\alpha^{(k)}\}$ define the proper zeroth-order states:
$$|\psi_{nk}^{(0)}\rangle = \sum_\alpha c_\alpha^{(k)}|\psi_{n\alpha}^{(0)}\rangle$$

**Physical meaning:** Perturbation "prefers" certain combinations; these split in energy.

**Answer:** Diagonalization finds states that perturbation doesn't mix (to first order)

### Example 3: Hydrogen $n=2$ with Stark Effect (Linear)

The $n = 2$ level of hydrogen in electric field $\mathcal{E}$ (along $z$) experiences $\hat{V} = -e\mathcal{E}z$.

States: $|2,0,0\rangle$ and $|2,1,0\rangle$ have $m = 0$ (same $z$-component). Compute perturbation matrix in this 2D subspace.

**Solution:**

Only $m = 0$ states couple (electric field along $z$ preserves $m$).

$$W = \begin{pmatrix} \langle 2,0,0|(-e\mathcal{E}z)|2,0,0\rangle & \langle 2,0,0|(-e\mathcal{E}z)|2,1,0\rangle \\ \langle 2,1,0|(-e\mathcal{E}z)|2,0,0\rangle & \langle 2,1,0|(-e\mathcal{E}z)|2,1,0\rangle \end{pmatrix}$$

Diagonal elements vanish by parity: $z$ is odd, $|2,0,0\rangle$ and $|2,1,0\rangle$ have definite parity.

Off-diagonal: $\langle 2,0,0|z|2,1,0\rangle = -3a_0$ (standard calculation)

$$W = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -3e\mathcal{E}a_0 \\ -3e\mathcal{E}a_0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

Eigenvalues: $\lambda_{\pm} = \pm 3e\mathcal{E}a_0$

**Answer:** Linear Stark splitting: $E^{(1)} = \pm 3ea_0\mathcal{E}$ for $m = 0$ states

### Example 4: Good Quantum Numbers

A "good quantum number" is one whose operator commutes with $\hat{H}_0 + \hat{V}$. Why does this simplify the perturbation matrix?

**Solution:**

If $[\hat{A}, \hat{H}] = 0$, we can choose simultaneous eigenstates of $\hat{A}$ and $\hat{H}$.

Suppose $\hat{A}|\psi_{n\alpha}\rangle = a_\alpha|\psi_{n\alpha}\rangle$ and $\hat{A}|\psi_{n\beta}\rangle = a_\beta|\psi_{n\beta}\rangle$ with $a_\alpha \neq a_\beta$.

Then:
$$\langle\psi_{n\alpha}|\hat{V}|\psi_{n\beta}\rangle = \langle\psi_{n\alpha}|\hat{A}^\dagger\hat{V}|\psi_{n\beta}\rangle/a_\alpha$$
$$= \langle\psi_{n\alpha}|\hat{V}\hat{A}|\psi_{n\beta}\rangle/a_\alpha = (a_\beta/a_\alpha)\langle\psi_{n\alpha}|\hat{V}|\psi_{n\beta}\rangle$$

This implies: $(1 - a_\beta/a_\alpha)\langle\psi_{n\alpha}|\hat{V}|\psi_{n\beta}\rangle = 0$

Since $a_\alpha \neq a_\beta$: $\langle\psi_{n\alpha}|\hat{V}|\psi_{n\beta}\rangle = 0$

**Matrix blocks diagonalize** by quantum number.

**Answer:** Good quantum numbers → perturbation matrix block-diagonal

## Fine Structure in Hydrogen

The fine structure of hydrogen arises from two relativistic corrections:

1. **Relativistic kinetic energy:** $\hat{V}_{rel} = -\frac{p^4}{8m^3c^2}$
2. **Spin-orbit coupling:** $\hat{V}_{SO} = \frac{e^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0 m^2c^2 r^3}\mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{S}$

### Example 5: Relativistic Correction

The relativistic kinetic energy correction comes from expanding $E = \sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4} \approx mc^2 + \frac{p^2}{2m} - \frac{p^4}{8m^3c^2}$.

For hydrogen state $|n,l,m\rangle$, compute $\langle\hat{V}_{rel}\rangle$.

**Solution:**

$$\langle V_{rel}\rangle = -\frac{1}{8m^3c^2}\langle p^4\rangle = -\frac{1}{8m^3c^2}\langle(2m(\hat{H}_0 - V))^2\rangle$$

Using $\hat{H}_0 = \frac{p^2}{2m} + V$ where $V = -e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0 r)$:

$$\langle p^2\rangle = 2m(\langle E_n\rangle - \langle V\rangle) = 2m\left(-\frac{13.6\text{ eV}}{n^2} + \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\langle\frac{1}{r}\rangle\right)$$

For hydrogen: $\langle\frac{1}{r}\rangle_{nl} = \frac{1}{n^2a_0}$

After algebra (using virial theorem and specific matrix elements):

$$\langle V_{rel}\rangle_{nl} = -\frac{(E_n)^2}{2mc^2}\left(\frac{4n}{l + 1/2} - 3\right)$$

**Answer:** Relativistic correction depends on both $n$ and $l$

### Example 6: Spin-Orbit Coupling Origin

Spin-orbit coupling arises from the electron's motion in the proton's electric field. In the electron's rest frame, the proton appears to orbit, creating a magnetic field $\mathbf{B} \sim \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{E}/c^2$. The electron's magnetic moment $\boldsymbol{\mu}_s \propto \mathbf{S}$ interacts with this field.

Show that $\hat{V}_{SO} \propto \mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{S}$.

**Solution:**

Electric field from proton: $\mathbf{E} = -\frac{e}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}\mathbf{r}$

In electron frame: $\mathbf{B} = \frac{1}{c^2}\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{e}{4\pi\epsilon_0 mc^2r^3}\mathbf{p} \times \mathbf{r}$

But $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}$, so: $\mathbf{B} = \frac{e}{4\pi\epsilon_0 mc^2r^3}\mathbf{L}$

Interaction energy: $\hat{V} = -\boldsymbol{\mu}_s \cdot \mathbf{B}$ where $\boldsymbol{\mu}_s = -g_s\frac{e}{2m}\mathbf{S}$ with $g_s \approx 2$

$$\hat{V}_{SO} = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 m^2c^2 r^3}\mathbf{S} \cdot \mathbf{L}$$

(Thomas precession gives factor of 1/2, yielding correct formula)

**Answer:** Spin-orbit $\propto \mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{S}$ from magnetic interaction

### Example 7: Total Angular Momentum

Spin-orbit coupling mixes $\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{S}$, so neither is conserved individually. But **total angular momentum** $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{L} + \mathbf{S}$ is conserved.

Good quantum numbers: $(n, l, s, j, m_j)$ where $j = |l - s|, \ldots, l + s$.

For electron: $s = 1/2$, so $j = l \pm 1/2$.

Express $\mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{S}$ in terms of $j, l, s$.

**Solution:**

$$\mathbf{J}^2 = (\mathbf{L} + \mathbf{S})^2 = \mathbf{L}^2 + \mathbf{S}^2 + 2\mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{S}$$

$$\mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{S} = \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{J}^2 - \mathbf{L}^2 - \mathbf{S}^2)$$

Eigenvalues:
$$\langle\mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{S}\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}{2}[j(j+1) - l(l+1) - s(s+1)]$$

For $s = 1/2$:
- If $j = l + 1/2$: $\langle\mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{S}\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}{2}l$
- If $j = l - 1/2$: $\langle\mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{S}\rangle = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2}(l+1)$

**Answer:** $\langle\mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{S}\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}{2}[j(j+1) - l(l+1) - 3/4]$

### Example 8: Fine Structure Formula

Combining relativistic and spin-orbit corrections (plus Darwin term for $l=0$):

$$E_{nj} = E_n^{(0)}\left[1 + \frac{\alpha^2}{n^2}\left(\frac{n}{j + 1/2} - \frac{3}{4}\right)\right]$$

where $\alpha = e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0\hbar c) \approx 1/137$ is the **fine structure constant**.

For $n = 2$, what are the energy levels?

**Solution:**

$n = 2$: Possible $(l, j)$ values are $(0, 1/2)$, $(1, 1/2)$, $(1, 3/2)$.

States $|2,0,1/2\rangle$ and $|2,1,1/2\rangle$: Both have $j = 1/2$
$$E_{2,j=1/2} = E_2^{(0)}\left[1 + \frac{\alpha^2}{4}\left(\frac{2}{1} - \frac{3}{4}\right)\right] = E_2^{(0)}\left[1 + \frac{5\alpha^2}{16}\right]$$

State $|2,1,3/2\rangle$: Has $j = 3/2$
$$E_{2,j=3/2} = E_2^{(0)}\left[1 + \frac{\alpha^2}{4}\left(\frac{2}{2} - \frac{3}{4}\right)\right] = E_2^{(0)}\left[1 + \frac{\alpha^2}{16}\right]$$

Splitting: $\Delta E = E_2^{(0)} \cdot \frac{4\alpha^2}{16} = \frac{\alpha^2}{4}|E_2^{(0)}| \approx 4.5 \times 10^{-5}$ eV

**Answer:** Two levels: $j = 1/2$ (doubly degenerate) and $j = 3/2$ (four-fold from $m_j$)

### Example 9: Accidental Degeneracy

Notice that $2S_{1/2}$ and $2P_{1/2}$ have the same energy in the Dirac equation (and fine structure formula). Why?

**Solution:**

The fine structure formula depends on $n$ and $j$, but **not separately on $l$**.

For given $n$ and $j$, there can be two values of $l = j \pm 1/2$ (if both allowed).

$2S_{1/2}$: $(n,l,j) = (2,0,1/2)$
$2P_{1/2}$: $(n,l,j) = (2,1,1/2)$

Both have same $n = 2$ and $j = 1/2$ → same energy.

This is an **accidental degeneracy** from the Dirac equation's special structure.

**Broken by:** Lamb shift (QED corrections) lifts this degeneracy: $2S_{1/2}$ is ~1057 MHz higher than $2P_{1/2}$.

**Answer:** Accidental degeneracy in $n, j$; Lamb shift breaks it

## Degenerate Perturbation Technique

### Example 10: Constructing Perturbation Matrix

For hydrogen $n = 3$ with perturbation $\hat{V} = \beta L_z^2$, construct the perturbation matrix for $l = 1$ states.

**Solution:**

$l = 1$ has three states: $|3,1,1\rangle, |3,1,0\rangle, |3,1,-1\rangle$ with $m = 1, 0, -1$.

$$W_{m,m'} = \langle 3,1,m|\beta L_z^2|3,1,m'\rangle$$

$\hat{L}_z$ is diagonal: $\hat{L}_z|l,m\rangle = \hbar m|l,m\rangle$

$$W_{m,m'} = \beta\hbar^2 m^2 \delta_{m,m'}$$

$$W = \beta\hbar^2\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

Already diagonal! Eigenvalues: $\beta\hbar^2$ (twice) and $0$.

States $|m = \pm 1\rangle$ degenerate at $E^{(1)} = \beta\hbar^2$; $|m = 0\rangle$ at $E^{(1)} = 0$.

**Answer:** Perturbation matrix diagonal in $m$; splits into two levels

### Example 11: When to Use Degenerate Theory

If perturbation is very small and splits degenerate states by amount $\delta \ll \Delta E$ (gap to other levels), can we use non-degenerate theory?

**Solution:**

No! Even tiny splitting requires degenerate treatment within the subspace.

**Reason:** Non-degenerate formulas have divergent denominators between degenerate states.

**Procedure:**
1. First, diagonalize within degenerate manifold (lifting degeneracy)
2. Then, if split levels are well-separated from other states, can treat as non-degenerate for higher orders

**Example:** Hydrogen $n = 2$ with fine structure ($\sim 10^{-5}$ eV splitting) is still degenerate compared to $n = 1$ vs. $n = 2$ gap ($\sim 10$ eV).

**Answer:** Must use degenerate theory within manifold, regardless of splitting size

### Example 12: Complete Diagonalization Example

Perturbation matrix in 2D subspace: $W = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}$

Find eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

**Solution:**

Secular equation: $\det(W - \lambda I) = 0$

$$(a - \lambda)^2 - b^2 = 0$$

$$\lambda_{\pm} = a \pm b$$

Eigenvectors:

For $\lambda_+ = a + b$: $(a - a - b)v_1 + bv_2 = 0$ → $v_1 = v_2$ → $|\psi_+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\psi_1\rangle + |\psi_2\rangle)$

For $\lambda_- = a - b$: $(a - a + b)v_1 + bv_2 = 0$ → $v_1 = -v_2$ → $|\psi_-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\psi_1\rangle - |\psi_2\rangle)$

First-order energies: $E_{\pm}^{(1)} = a \pm b$

**Answer:** Symmetric/antisymmetric combinations with energies $a \pm b$

### Example 13: Selection Rules in Degenerate Theory

A perturbation $\hat{V}$ that is **parity-odd** (like $z$ or $\mathbf{L} \times \mathbf{S}$) has zero diagonal elements between states of definite parity. Why?

**Solution:**

Parity operator $\hat{\Pi}$: $\hat{\Pi}|\psi\rangle = \pm|\psi\rangle$ (even/odd)

If $\hat{V}$ is odd: $\hat{\Pi}\hat{V}\hat{\Pi} = -\hat{V}$

For state with definite parity $\hat{\Pi}|\psi\rangle = \pi_\psi|\psi\rangle$:

$$\langle\psi|\hat{V}|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\hat{\Pi}^\dagger\hat{V}\hat{\Pi}|\psi\rangle = \pi_\psi^2\langle\psi|\hat{V}|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|(-\hat{V})|\psi\rangle = -\langle\psi|\hat{V}|\psi\rangle$$

This implies: $2\langle\psi|\hat{V}|\psi\rangle = 0$ → $\langle\psi|\hat{V}|\psi\rangle = 0$

**Physical meaning:** Odd perturbations don't shift energy in first order, only mix states.

**Answer:** Parity conservation → odd operators have zero diagonal elements

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Core of degenerate perturbation theory: diagonalize $W_{\alpha\beta} = \langle\psi_{n\alpha}|\hat{V}|\psi_{n\beta}\rangle$ within degenerate subspace. Eigenvalues give first-order energy corrections; eigenvectors define proper zeroth-order states. Only after this diagonalization can we proceed with perturbation expansion. Non-degenerate formulas fail due to zero denominators. This preliminary diagonalization is essential step.

**Problem 2: B**
Fine structure constant $\alpha = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0\hbar c} \approx \frac{1}{137}$ is fundamental dimensionless constant characterizing electromagnetic interaction strength. Appears as expansion parameter in fine structure: corrections are $\mathcal{O}(\alpha^2) \approx 10^{-5}$. In QED, $\alpha$ is coupling constant. Small value makes perturbation theory valid for atomic physics. One of fundamental constants of nature.

**Problem 3: C**
Spin-orbit coupling: $\hat{V}_{SO} = \frac{e^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0 m^2c^2r^3}\mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{S}$
Arises from electron's magnetic moment in magnetic field from its motion through electric field. Couples orbital and spin angular momentum. Neither $\mathbf{L}$ nor $\mathbf{S}$ conserved individually, but total $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{L} + \mathbf{S}$ is conserved. Using $\mathbf{J}^2 = \mathbf{L}^2 + \mathbf{S}^2 + 2\mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{S}$, can evaluate expectation value.

**Problem 4: B**
Good quantum number: corresponds to operator commuting with total Hamiltonian $\hat{H} = \hat{H}_0 + \hat{V}$. These label states that don't mix under perturbation. Block-diagonalize perturbation matrix by good quantum numbers. In hydrogen with spin-orbit, good quantum numbers are $(n, l, s, j, m_j)$, not $(n, l, m_l, m_s)$ since $[\hat{L}_z, \hat{V}_{SO}] \neq 0$ and $[\hat{S}_z, \hat{V}_{SO}] \neq 0$, but $[\hat{J}_z, \hat{V}_{SO}] = 0$.

**Problem 5: C**
Dirac equation predicts fine structure depending only on $n$ and $j$, creating accidental degeneracy: states with same $j$ but different $l = j \pm 1/2$ have identical energy. For $n=2$: $2S_{1/2}$ and $2P_{1/2}$ both have $j=1/2$ → degenerate. QED corrections (Lamb shift) break this: virtual photon loops cause $2S_{1/2}$ to lie ~1057 MHz higher. Precision measurement (Lamb, Retherford 1947) provided crucial test of QED, leading to modern quantum field theory.
