# Quantum Mechanics Topic 4.4: Quantum Statistics (Fermi-Dirac and Bose-Einstein)

At absolute zero, classical particles settle into the ground state. But quantum particles—governed by exchange statistics—behave radically differently. **Fermions** (electrons, protons, neutrons) obey the Pauli exclusion principle: each state holds at most one particle. Stack them in a box, and they fill energy levels like water filling a bucket, with the **Fermi surface** marking the top occupied level. Even at $T = 0$, fermions have enormous kinetic energy—**Fermi pressure**—that supports white dwarf stars against gravity.

**Bosons** (photons, helium-4 atoms, pions) have no such restriction. Cool them below a critical temperature $T_c$, and they undergo **Bose-Einstein condensation**: a macroscopic fraction occupies the ground state. This quantum phase transition creates superfluids (helium-4 below 2.17 K) and Bose-Einstein condensates (ultracold atoms). The **Fermi-Dirac** and **Bose-Einstein** distribution functions $\langle n_i\rangle = \frac{1}{e^{(\epsilon_i - \mu)/(k_BT)} \pm 1}$ govern occupation numbers, differing only in sign but producing vastly different physics.

This article develops quantum statistical mechanics. We'll derive FD and BE distributions from grand canonical ensemble, understand the chemical potential $\mu$ and its role, explore Fermi energy and degenerate fermion gases, examine BEC and critical temperature, compute thermodynamic properties (pressure, energy, heat capacity), and see applications from metals to neutron stars to ultracold atoms. You'll discover that particle statistics—not interactions—determines bulk properties of matter.

## Occupation Numbers and Distributions

**Quantum statistics**: Many identical particles distributed among single-particle states.

**Occupation number** $n_i$: Number of particles in state $i$ with energy $\epsilon_i$.

### Example 1: Occupation Number Constraints

For fermions and bosons, what values can $n_i$ take?

**Solution:**

**Fermions**: Pauli exclusion

$$n_i = 0 \text{ or } 1$$

**Bosons**: No restriction

$$n_i = 0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots$$

**Total particles**: $N = \sum_i n_i$ (conserved)

**Total energy**: $E = \sum_i n_i\epsilon_i$

**Answer:** Fermions $\{0, 1\}$; bosons $\{0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$

### Example 2: Grand Canonical Ensemble

For system exchanging particles and energy with reservoir:

**Solution:**

**Probability**: $P(\{n_i\}) \propto e^{-\beta(E - \mu N)}$ where $\beta = 1/(k_BT)$, $\mu$ is chemical potential.

$$P(\{n_i\}) \propto \exp\left[-\beta\sum_i(\epsilon_i - \mu)n_i\right]$$

**Average occupation**:

$$\langle n_i\rangle = \frac{\sum_{\{n_i\}} n_i e^{-\beta\sum_j(\epsilon_j - \mu)n_j}}{\sum_{\{n_i\}} e^{-\beta\sum_j(\epsilon_j - \mu)n_j}}$$

**Answer:** Occupation determined by temperature $T$ and chemical potential $\mu$

### Example 3: Fermi-Dirac Distribution

Derive $\langle n_i\rangle$ for fermions.

**Solution:**

For fermions: $n_i \in \{0, 1\}$

$$\langle n_i\rangle = \frac{0 \cdot e^{-\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu) \cdot 0} + 1 \cdot e^{-\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu) \cdot 1}}{e^{-\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu) \cdot 0} + e^{-\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu) \cdot 1}}$$

$$= \frac{e^{-\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)}}{1 + e^{-\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)}} = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)} + 1}$$

**Fermi-Dirac distribution**:

$$f_{FD}(\epsilon) = \frac{1}{e^{(\epsilon - \mu)/(k_BT)} + 1}$$

**Answer:** $f_{FD} = \frac{1}{e^{(\epsilon - \mu)/k_BT} + 1}$ (plus sign in denominator)

### Example 4: Bose-Einstein Distribution

Derive $\langle n_i\rangle$ for bosons.

**Solution:**

For bosons: $n_i \in \{0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$

$$\langle n_i\rangle = \frac{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} n e^{-\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)n}}{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} e^{-\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)n}}$$

Geometric series: $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} x^n = \frac{1}{1-x}$ for $|x| < 1$

With $x = e^{-\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)}$:

$$\langle n_i\rangle = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)} - 1}$$

**Bose-Einstein distribution**:

$$f_{BE}(\epsilon) = \frac{1}{e^{(\epsilon - \mu)/(k_BT)} - 1}$$

**Constraint**: $\mu < \epsilon_{\min}$ (chemical potential below lowest energy)

**Answer:** $f_{BE} = \frac{1}{e^{(\epsilon - \mu)/k_BT} - 1}$ (minus sign in denominator)

### Example 5: High-Temperature Limit

Show both FD and BE reduce to Maxwell-Boltzmann at high $T$.

**Solution:**

When $e^{(\epsilon - \mu)/k_BT} \gg 1$:

$$f_{FD} \approx \frac{1}{e^{(\epsilon - \mu)/k_BT}} = e^{-(\epsilon - \mu)/k_BT}$$

$$f_{BE} \approx \frac{1}{e^{(\epsilon - \mu)/k_BT}} = e^{-(\epsilon - \mu)/k_BT}$$

Both approach **Maxwell-Boltzmann**:

$$f_{MB}(\epsilon) = e^{-(\epsilon - \mu)/k_BT}$$

**Classical limit**: Quantum statistics don't matter when occupation $\ll 1$ (dilute, high-temperature).

**Answer:** FD and BE both → MB when $k_BT \gg \epsilon - \mu$

## Fermi-Dirac Systems

### Example 6: Fermi Energy

At $T = 0$, fermions fill states up to **Fermi energy** $E_F$.

**Solution:**

$$f_{FD}(\epsilon, T=0) = \begin{cases} 1 & \epsilon < \mu(T=0) \equiv E_F \\ 0 & \epsilon > E_F \end{cases}$$

(Sharp step function)

For 3D free electron gas with density $n$:

$$E_F = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(3\pi^2 n)^{2/3}$$

**Typical values**: Metals have $E_F \sim 1$-10 eV ≫ room temperature $k_BT \sim 0.025$ eV.

**Answer:** Fermi energy = chemical potential at $T = 0$

### Example 7: Fermi Temperature

Define $T_F = E_F/k_B$.

**Solution:**

For copper: $E_F \approx 7$ eV

$$T_F = \frac{7 \text{ eV}}{8.617 \times 10^{-5} \text{ eV/K}} \approx 81,000 \text{ K}$$

**Physical meaning**: Temperature where thermal energy comparable to Fermi energy.

Most metals: $T_{\text{room}} \ll T_F$ (degenerate regime).

**Answer:** Fermi temperature enormous compared to room temperature

### Example 8: Degeneracy Pressure

Fermions at $T = 0$ have kinetic energy from Pauli exclusion.

**Solution:**

**Energy density**:

$$u = \frac{3}{5}nE_F \propto n^{5/3}$$

**Pressure** (from thermodynamics):

$$P = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\bigg|_N = \frac{2}{5}nE_F \propto n^{5/3}$$

**Application**: White dwarf stars

Electron degeneracy pressure balances gravity:

$$P_{\text{deg}} \sim \frac{\hbar^2}{m_e}\left(\frac{M}{R^3}\right)^{5/3}$$

Chandrasekhar limit: $M_{\max} \approx 1.4 M_{\odot}$ (above this, collapse to neutron star).

**Answer:** Degeneracy pressure from Pauli exclusion (purely quantum)

### Example 9: Heat Capacity of Electron Gas

At low $T \ll T_F$, only electrons near Fermi surface excited.

**Solution:**

Fraction excited: $\sim k_BT/E_F$

**Heat capacity**:

$$C_V \approx N k_B \frac{T}{T_F} \propto T$$

(Linear in $T$, not classical $3Nk_B/2$)

**Experimental**: Metals show $C_V = \gamma T + \beta T^3$ (linear from electrons + cubic from phonons).

**Answer:** Electronic heat capacity $\propto T$ (characteristic of degenerate Fermi gas)

## Bose-Einstein Systems

### Example 10: Bose-Einstein Condensation

Below critical temperature $T_c$, macroscopic occupation of ground state.

**Solution:**

**Critical temperature** (3D):

$$k_BT_c = \frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{m}\left(\frac{n}{\zeta(3/2)}\right)^{2/3}$$

where $\zeta(3/2) \approx 2.612$ (Riemann zeta function).

For $T < T_c$:

$$N_0 = N\left(1 - \left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^{3/2}\right)$$

(Fraction in ground state)

**Examples**:
- $^4$He: $T_c = 2.17$ K (superfluid transition)
- Rb-87 atoms: $T_c \sim 100$ nK (first atomic BEC, 1995)

**Answer:** Macroscopic ground state occupation for $T < T_c$

### Example 11: Photon Statistics

Photons are bosons with $\mu = 0$ (not conserved).

**Solution:**

**Planck distribution**:

$$f(\epsilon) = \frac{1}{e^{\epsilon/k_BT} - 1}$$

**Energy density**:

$$u(\nu) = \frac{8\pi h\nu^3}{c^3}\frac{1}{e^{h\nu/k_BT} - 1}$$

Reproduces **blackbody radiation** (Planck 1900).

**Stefan-Boltzmann law**: $u \propto T^4$

**Answer:** Photons follow BE with $\mu = 0$ (Planck's law)

### Example 12: Superfluidity

$^4$He becomes superfluid below $T_\lambda = 2.17$ K.

**Solution:**

Liquid helium-4: bosons (spin-0 nucleus)

Below $T_\lambda$: Macroscopic wavefunction (BEC in interacting system)

**Properties**:
- Zero viscosity (flows without resistance)
- Fountain effect
- Quantized vortices

**Two-fluid model**: Normal component + superfluid component.

Fraction superfluid: $\rho_s/\rho \to 1$ as $T \to 0$.

**Answer:** BEC in interacting liquid → superfluidity

### Example 13: Superconductivity

Electron pairs (Cooper pairs) form composite bosons.

**Solution:**

Electrons (fermions) pair via phonon-mediated attraction.

**Cooper pairs**: Spin-0 bosons (two spin-1/2 with opposite spins)

Below $T_c$: Pairs undergo BEC → superconducting state

**Properties**:
- Zero electrical resistance
- Meissner effect (expels magnetic field)
- Energy gap $\Delta \sim k_BT_c$

**BCS theory** (Bardeen-Cooper-Schrieffer, 1957): Microscopic theory.

**Answer:** Fermions pair to form bosons → BEC → superconductivity

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
$f_{FD} = \frac{1}{e^{(\epsilon - \mu)/k_BT} + 1}$ (plus sign)
Contrast BE: minus sign
Occupation $\leq 1$ (Pauli exclusion)
Step function at $T = 0$
Smooth at finite temperature

**Problem 2: B**
At $T=0$: $\mu = E_F$ (Fermi energy)
All states $\epsilon < E_F$ occupied ($f=1$)
All states $\epsilon > E_F$ empty ($f=0$)
Sharp Fermi surface (zero-temperature limit)
Typical $E_F \sim$ few eV in metals

**Problem 3: C**
BEC at $T < T_c$ (critical temperature)
Macroscopic ground state occupation
$N_0/N = 1 - (T/T_c)^{3/2}$ for $T < T_c$
Quantum phase transition
Examples: $^4$He, ultracold atoms

**Problem 4: C**
Photons: $\mu = 0$ (not conserved)
Created/destroyed freely
Planck distribution: $f = 1/(e^{\epsilon/k_BT} - 1)$
No constraint on photon number
Blackbody radiation

**Problem 5: B**
Electron degeneracy pressure (Pauli exclusion)
$P \propto n^{5/3}$ (purely quantum)
Balances gravitational collapse
Chandrasekhar limit: $M_{\max} \approx 1.4 M_{\odot}$
Relativistic degeneracy in neutron stars
