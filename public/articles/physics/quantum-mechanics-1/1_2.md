# Quantum Mechanics Topic 1.2: Measurement, Eigenvalues, and Uncertainty

In classical physics, measurement reveals pre-existing values. Measure a ball's position, you discover where it *was*. Quantum mechanics shatters this picture. Before measurement, a particle doesn't *have* a definite position—it exists in a **superposition** of all possibilities. Measurement doesn't reveal a hidden value; it *creates* a definite outcome by collapsing the wavefunction to an eigenstate. The result you get is fundamentally random, governed only by probability amplitudes.

This isn't measurement error or ignorance—it's nature's bedrock. The **eigenvalue equation** $\hat{A}|\psi_n\rangle = a_n|\psi_n\rangle$ determines possible measurement outcomes $a_n$. A system in eigenstate $|\psi_n\rangle$ always yields $a_n$ with certainty. But a general state $|\psi\rangle = \sum c_n|\psi_n\rangle$ yields $a_n$ with probability $|c_n|^2$, collapsing to $|\psi_n\rangle$ after measurement. The **uncertainty principle** $\Delta x\Delta p \geq \hbar/2$ emerges mathematically from the commutator $[\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = i\hbar$—it's not about measurement precision but fundamental incompatibility of observables.

This article develops the measurement postulates rigorously. We'll solve eigenvalue problems for position and momentum, compute expectation values and uncertainties, derive the generalized uncertainty relation from commutators, and understand why some observables cannot be simultaneously measured. You'll learn that quantum randomness is fundamental, not a deficiency in our knowledge.

## The Eigenvalue Problem

An **eigenstate** of operator $\hat{A}$ satisfies:

$$\hat{A}|\psi_n\rangle = a_n|\psi_n\rangle$$

where $a_n$ is the **eigenvalue** (measurement outcome) and $|\psi_n\rangle$ is the **eigenvector** (state after measurement).

For Hermitian operators, eigenvalues are real and eigenvectors can be chosen orthonormal.

### Example 1: Momentum Eigenstates

Find eigenstates and eigenvalues of $\hat{p} = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}$.

**Solution:**

Eigenvalue equation: $-i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi_p}{\partial x} = p\psi_p$

This is separable ODE:

$$\frac{\partial\psi_p}{\partial x} = \frac{ip}{\hbar}\psi_p$$

Solution: $\psi_p(x) = Ae^{ipx/\hbar}$

**Normalization**: These are not normalizable in usual sense (extend to infinity). Use Dirac delta normalization:

$$\langle p'|p\rangle = \delta(p' - p)$$

**Answer:** Plane waves $\psi_p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}e^{ipx/\hbar}$ with eigenvalue $p$

### Example 2: Position Eigenstates

What are eigenstates of $\hat{x}$ (multiplication by $x$)?

**Solution:**

Eigenvalue equation: $x\psi_x(x') = x'\psi_x(x')$

This requires $\psi_x(x') = 0$ unless $x' = x$.

Solution: $\psi_x(x') = \delta(x' - x)$ (Dirac delta function)

Interpretation: Particle localized exactly at $x$ (infinitely sharp).

**Normalization**: $\langle x'|x\rangle = \delta(x' - x)$

**Answer:** $|x\rangle$ corresponds to $\delta$-function at position $x$

### Example 3: Energy Eigenstate (Particle in Box)

For infinite square well with $V = 0$ for $0 < x < L$, solve $\hat{H}\psi = E\psi$ where $\hat{H} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2}$.

**Solution:**

Inside well: $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' = E\psi$

$$\psi'' = -\frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\psi = -k^2\psi$$ where $k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$

General solution: $\psi = A\sin(kx) + B\cos(kx)$

Boundary conditions: $\psi(0) = \psi(L) = 0$

$\psi(0) = 0 \Rightarrow B = 0$

$\psi(L) = 0 \Rightarrow A\sin(kL) = 0 \Rightarrow kL = n\pi$ for $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$

**Eigenvalues**: $E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$

**Eigenstates**: $\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$

**Answer:** Quantized energy levels with discrete spectrum

## The Measurement Postulate

**Postulate**: When measuring observable $\hat{A}$ on state $|\psi\rangle = \sum_n c_n|\psi_n\rangle$ (expanded in eigenbasis of $\hat{A}$):

1. Possible outcomes: eigenvalues $a_n$
2. Probability of outcome $a_n$: $P(a_n) = |c_n|^2 = |\langle\psi_n|\psi\rangle|^2$
3. After measurement yielding $a_n$, state becomes $|\psi_n\rangle$ (**collapse**)

### Example 4: Computing Measurement Probabilities

A particle is in state $|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}|\psi_1\rangle + \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}|\psi_2\rangle$ where $|\psi_1\rangle$ and $|\psi_2\rangle$ are energy eigenstates with energies $E_1$ and $E_2$.

What are probabilities of measuring $E_1$ or $E_2$?

**Solution:**

$c_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$, $c_2 = \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}$

$$P(E_1) = |c_1|^2 = \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right|^2 = \frac{1}{3}$$

$$P(E_2) = |c_2|^2 = \left|\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\right|^2 = \frac{2}{3}$$

Check: $P(E_1) + P(E_2) = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{2}{3} = 1$ ✓

**Answer:** $P(E_1) = 1/3$, $P(E_2) = 2/3$

### Example 5: State After Measurement

In Example 4, if we measure energy and obtain $E_2$, what is the state immediately after measurement?

**Solution:**

**Wavefunction collapse**: State becomes eigenstate corresponding to measured eigenvalue.

Before measurement: $|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}|\psi_1\rangle + \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}|\psi_2\rangle$

After measurement of $E_2$: $|\psi\rangle \to |\psi_2\rangle$

**Answer:** State collapses to $|\psi_2\rangle$ (superposition destroyed)

### Example 6: Repeated Measurements

If we immediately measure energy again (on state $|\psi_2\rangle$ from Example 5), what result do we get?

**Solution:**

$|\psi_2\rangle$ is eigenstate of $\hat{H}$ with eigenvalue $E_2$.

Measurement of eigenstate always gives its eigenvalue with probability 1.

**Answer:** Certainly get $E_2$ again (state already in eigenstate)

## Expectation Values and Variance

**Expectation value** (average over many measurements):

$$\langle\hat{A}\rangle = \langle\psi|\hat{A}|\psi\rangle = \sum_n a_n|c_n|^2$$

**Variance** (spread of measurement outcomes):

$$(\Delta A)^2 = \langle(\hat{A} - \langle\hat{A}\rangle)^2\rangle = \langle\hat{A}^2\rangle - \langle\hat{A}\rangle^2$$

### Example 7: Computing Expectation Value

For particle in ground state of infinite well: $\psi_1(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin(\pi x/L)$

Find $\langle x \rangle$ (average position).

**Solution:**

$$\langle x \rangle = \int_0^L \psi_1^*(x) \cdot x \cdot \psi_1(x) dx$$

$$= \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L x\sin^2(\pi x/L) dx$$

Use $\sin^2\theta = \frac{1 - \cos(2\theta)}{2}$:

$$= \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L x\left[\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2}\cos(2\pi x/L)\right] dx$$

$$= \frac{1}{L}\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^L - \frac{1}{L}\int_0^L x\cos(2\pi x/L) dx$$

Second integral vanishes (integration by parts, symmetric limits):

$$= \frac{L}{2}$$

**Answer:** $\langle x \rangle = L/2$ (center of box, as expected by symmetry)

### Example 8: Computing Variance

For same ground state, compute $(\Delta x)^2$.

**Solution:**

Need $\langle x^2 \rangle$:

$$\langle x^2 \rangle = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L x^2\sin^2(\pi x/L) dx$$

Using integral tables or integration by parts repeatedly:

$$\langle x^2 \rangle = \frac{L^2}{3} - \frac{L^2}{2\pi^2}$$

$$(\Delta x)^2 = \langle x^2 \rangle - \langle x \rangle^2 = \frac{L^2}{3} - \frac{L^2}{2\pi^2} - \frac{L^2}{4}$$

$$= L^2\left(\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\right) = L^2\left(\frac{1}{12} - \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\right)$$

**Answer:** $\Delta x = L\sqrt{\frac{1}{12} - \frac{1}{2\pi^2}} \approx 0.18L$

### Example 9: Zero Uncertainty in Eigenstate

If particle is in momentum eigenstate $\psi_p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}e^{ipx/\hbar}$, what is $\Delta p$?

**Solution:**

Eigenstate has definite momentum $p$:

$$\langle\hat{p}\rangle = p$$ (eigenvalue)

$$\langle\hat{p}^2\rangle = p^2$$ (eigenvalue squared)

$$(\Delta p)^2 = \langle\hat{p}^2\rangle - \langle\hat{p}\rangle^2 = p^2 - p^2 = 0$$

**Answer:** $\Delta p = 0$ (no uncertainty in eigenstates)

## The Uncertainty Principle

**Generalized uncertainty relation**: For any two observables,

$$\Delta A \Delta B \geq \frac{1}{2}|\langle[\hat{A}, \hat{B}]\rangle|$$

**Heisenberg uncertainty principle** (from $[\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = i\hbar$):

$$\Delta x \Delta p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}$$

### Example 10: Deriving Heisenberg Uncertainty

Apply generalized uncertainty to $\hat{x}$ and $\hat{p}$.

**Solution:**

$$\Delta x \Delta p \geq \frac{1}{2}|\langle[\hat{x}, \hat{p}]\rangle|$$

$$[\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = i\hbar$$

$$|\langle i\hbar \rangle| = |\langle\psi|i\hbar|\psi\rangle| = |i\hbar\langle\psi|\psi\rangle| = |i\hbar| = \hbar$$

**Answer:** $\Delta x \Delta p \geq \hbar/2$ (fundamental limit)

### Example 11: Minimum Uncertainty State

Gaussian wavepacket: $\psi(x) = \left(\frac{1}{\pi a^2}\right)^{1/4}e^{-x^2/2a^2}$

This satisfies $\Delta x \Delta p = \hbar/2$ (equality). Verify $\Delta x = a/\sqrt{2}$.

**Solution:**

By symmetry: $\langle x \rangle = 0$

$$\langle x^2 \rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^2|\psi(x)|^2 dx = \sqrt{\frac{1}{\pi a^2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^2 e^{-x^2/a^2} dx$$

Gaussian integral: $\int x^2 e^{-x^2/a^2} dx = \frac{a^3\sqrt{\pi}}{2}$

$$\langle x^2 \rangle = \frac{a^2}{2}$$

$$\Delta x = \sqrt{\langle x^2 \rangle} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{2}}$$ ✓

**Answer:** Gaussian minimizes uncertainty product

## Commuting Observables

**Theorem**: If $[\hat{A}, \hat{B}] = 0$, then $\hat{A}$ and $\hat{B}$ have simultaneous eigenstates.

**Physical meaning**: Commuting observables can be measured simultaneously with no uncertainty trade-off.

### Example 12: Commuting Operators

Show that $\hat{p}_x$ and $\hat{p}_y$ commute (for 3D system).

**Solution:**

$$[\hat{p}_x, \hat{p}_y] = \left[-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}, -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right]$$

$$= (-i\hbar)^2\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right]$$

Partial derivatives commute: $\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x \partial y} = \frac{\partial^2}{\partial y \partial x}$

$$[\hat{p}_x, \hat{p}_y] = 0$$ ✓

**Answer:** Different momentum components commute (can measure simultaneously)

### Example 13: Non-Commuting Example

Why can't we simultaneously know exact position and momentum?

**Solution:**

$[\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = i\hbar \neq 0$ (don't commute)

By uncertainty relation:

$$\Delta x \Delta p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2} > 0$$

Cannot both be zero simultaneously.

If $\Delta x = 0$ (exact position): $\Delta p \to \infty$ (momentum completely uncertain)

If $\Delta p = 0$ (exact momentum): $\Delta x \to \infty$ (position completely uncertain)

**Answer:** Non-commuting observables are incompatible (fundamental quantum limitation)

**Problem 3:** The uncertainty principle $\Delta x \Delta p \geq \hbar/2$ arises from:

A) Measurement errors
B) Commutator $[\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = i\hbar$
C) Wave-particle duality
D) Observer effect

A) Measuring one destroys the other
B) They have simultaneous eigenstates
C) Uncertainty relation forbids measurement
D) They are the same operator
E) One must be zero

**Problem 5:** The variance $(\Delta A)^2$ equals:

A) $\langle\hat{A}\rangle$
B) $\langle\hat{A}^2\rangle$
C) $\langle\hat{A}^2\rangle - \langle\hat{A}\rangle^2$
D) $\langle\hat{A}\rangle^2$
E) $\langle\hat{A}\rangle - \langle\hat{A}^2\rangle$

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Eigenstate $\hat{A}|\psi_n\rangle = a_n|\psi_n\rangle$ gives definite result $a_n$
Probability = $|\langle\psi_n|\psi_n\rangle|^2 = 1$ (certainty)
No uncertainty: $\Delta A = 0$
Example: momentum eigenstate has $\Delta p = 0$
Measurement doesn't change eigenstate

**Problem 2: C**
Wavefunction collapse (von Neumann projection)
Superposition $|\psi\rangle = \sum c_n|\psi_n\rangle$ → single term $|\psi_n\rangle$
Measurement is non-unitary (irreversible)
Subsequent measurement gives same result
Fundamental postulate of QM

**Problem 3: B**
Mathematical origin: commutator $[\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = i\hbar$
Generalized: $\Delta A \Delta B \geq \frac{1}{2}|\langle[\hat{A}, \hat{B}]\rangle|$
Not about measurement precision (fundamental limit)
Not observer effect (intrinsic to nature)
Can't be improved with better instruments

**Problem 4: B**
Commuting operators share complete set of eigenstates
Can diagonalize simultaneously
No uncertainty trade-off
Example: $\hat{p}_x$ and $\hat{p}_y$ commute
Can measure both exactly

**Problem 5: C**
Variance: $(\Delta A)^2 = \langle(\hat{A} - \langle\hat{A}\rangle)^2\rangle$
Expanding: $= \langle\hat{A}^2 - 2\hat{A}\langle\hat{A}\rangle + \langle\hat{A}\rangle^2\rangle$
$= \langle\hat{A}^2\rangle - \langle\hat{A}\rangle^2$
Measures spread of distribution
Standard deviation: $\Delta A = \sqrt{(\Delta A)^2}$
