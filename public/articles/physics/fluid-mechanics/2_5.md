# Article 2.5: Conservation of Energy

Energy conservation in fluid mechanics couples thermal and mechanical processes. Unlike solids where energy analysis is often purely mechanical, fluids exchange energy through **work** (pressure, viscous), **heat transfer** (conduction, convection), and **internal energy changes** (temperature, phase). The **energy equation** derived from the first law of thermodynamics tracks how kinetic energy, potential energy, and internal energy evolve as fluid moves. A critical distinction emerges between **reversible** processes (inviscid flow, Bernoulli's equation) and **irreversible** processes (viscous dissipation converting mechanical energy to heat). The **viscous dissipation function** $\Phi = \tau_{ij}\dot{\epsilon}_{ij}$ quantifies this irreversible loss—crucial in high-speed flows, lubrication, and polymer processing.

For most incompressible flows, we can separate mechanical and thermal energy equations. The mechanical energy equation (derived from Navier-Stokes) governs kinetic and potential energy, while the thermal energy equation governs temperature via heat conduction and viscous heating. At high speeds or in compressible flow, these couple strongly: compression heating (adiabatic temperature rise) and expansion cooling become important, and **enthalpy** $h = e + P/\rho$ replaces internal energy as the natural variable. **Bernoulli's equation** emerges as a special case: for steady, inviscid, incompressible flow along a streamline, mechanical energy is conserved. This powerful result connects pressure, velocity, and elevation—the basis for countless engineering applications from Venturi meters to airfoil lift estimation.

This article derives the general energy equation from the first law, explores the viscous dissipation function and its physical meaning, derives Bernoulli's equation as a mechanical energy balance, treats the thermal energy equation with conduction and dissipation, discusses coupling between flow and temperature fields, explores simplified forms (constant density, negligible dissipation), and works through extensive examples involving energy balances, temperature rise from dissipation, Bernoulli applications, and enthalpy calculations. The mathematics involves vector calculus operations on scalar and vector fields, tensor contractions, integration, and careful accounting of energy transfer mechanisms.

## First Law of Thermodynamics for Control Volume

For control volume $V$ with surface $S$:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\int_V \rho(e + \mathbf{u}^2/2) \, dV = \dot{Q} - \dot{W} + \int_S \rho(e + \mathbf{u}^2/2)\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dA$$

**Terms**:
- Left side: rate of change of total energy (internal + kinetic)
- $\dot{Q}$: heat transfer rate into volume
- $\dot{W}$: work done by fluid
- Last term: energy flux through surface

**Heat transfer**: $\dot{Q} = -\int_S \mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dA$ where $\mathbf{q}$ is heat flux

Fourier's law: $\mathbf{q} = -k\nabla T$

**Work**: Pressure work + viscous work
$$\dot{W} = \int_S (P\mathbf{u} + \boldsymbol{\tau} \cdot \mathbf{u}) \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dA$$

## Differential Form: Energy Equation

After applying divergence theorem and material derivative:

$$\rho\frac{De}{Dt} = -P\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} + \nabla \cdot (k\nabla T) + \Phi$$

**Terms**:
- $\rho\frac{De}{Dt}$: rate of internal energy change following particle
- $-P\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u}$: pressure work (compression/expansion)
- $\nabla \cdot (k\nabla T)$: heat conduction
- $\Phi$: viscous dissipation (mechanical → thermal energy)

## Viscous Dissipation Function

$$\boxed{\Phi = \tau_{ij}\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} = \tau_{ij}\dot{\epsilon}_{ij}}$$

**Physical meaning**: Rate of mechanical energy dissipation per unit volume.

**Always positive**: Second law ensures $\Phi \geq 0$ (irreversible).

For **Newtonian fluid**:

$$\Phi = \mu\left[\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right)\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}\right] - \frac{2}{3}\mu(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u})^2$$

**Incompressible** ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$):

$$\boxed{\Phi = 2\mu\dot{\epsilon}_{ij}\dot{\epsilon}_{ij} = 2\mu\left[\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}\right)^2 + \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\right)^2 + \cdots\right]}$$

## Enthalpy Form

Define **specific enthalpy**: $h = e + P/\rho$

$$\frac{Dh}{Dt} = \frac{De}{Dt} + \frac{1}{\rho}\frac{DP}{Dt} - \frac{P}{\rho^2}\frac{D\rho}{Dt}$$

Using continuity $\frac{D\rho}{Dt} = -\rho\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u}$:

$$\boxed{\rho\frac{Dh}{Dt} = \frac{DP}{Dt} + \nabla \cdot (k\nabla T) + \Phi}$$

**Enthalpy form**: Often more convenient, especially for compressible flow.

## Thermal Energy Equation

For incompressible flow with $e = c_p T$ (or $c_v T$ for solids):

$$\boxed{\rho c_p\frac{DT}{Dt} = \nabla \cdot (k\nabla T) + \Phi}$$

**Simplified** (constant $k$):

$$\boxed{\rho c_p\frac{DT}{Dt} = k\nabla^2 T + \Phi}$$

**Expanded**:

$$\rho c_p\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial T}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial T}{\partial y} + w\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\right) = k\nabla^2 T + \Phi$$

**Physical interpretation**:
- Left: rate of temperature change following particle (advection)
- $k\nabla^2 T$: heat diffusion
- $\Phi$: viscous heating

## Mechanical Energy Equation

Dot Navier-Stokes with $\mathbf{u}$:

$$\rho\mathbf{u} \cdot \frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} = -\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla P + \mu\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla^2\mathbf{u}$$

Left side: $\frac{D}{Dt}(\mathbf{u}^2/2)$ (kinetic energy rate)

After manipulation:

$$\rho\frac{D}{Dt}\left(\frac{\mathbf{u}^2}{2}\right) = -\nabla \cdot (P\mathbf{u}) + P\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} + \nabla \cdot (\boldsymbol{\tau} \cdot \mathbf{u}) - \Phi$$

**Interpretation**: $\Phi$ appears with negative sign—mechanical energy lost to heat.

## Bernoulli's Equation (Preview)

For **steady, inviscid, incompressible flow** along streamline:

From Euler equation: $(\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla P + \mathbf{g}$

Dot with $\mathbf{u}$:

$$\mathbf{u} \cdot [(\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u}] = \frac{1}{2}\nabla(\mathbf{u}^2) - \mathbf{u} \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{u})$$

For **irrotational flow** ($\nabla \times \mathbf{u} = 0$) or along streamline:

$$\frac{1}{2}\nabla(\mathbf{u}^2) = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla P - \nabla(\gz)$$

Integrate:

$$\boxed{\frac{P}{\rho} + \frac{\mathbf{u}^2}{2} + gz = \text{constant}}$$

**Bernoulli's equation**: mechanical energy per unit mass conserved.

Multiplying by $\rho$:

$$\boxed{P + \frac{1}{2}\rho\mathbf{u}^2 + \rho g z = \text{constant}}$$

Units: pressure (energy per unit volume).

## Example 1: Dissipation in Couette Flow

For $u = Uy/h$ between parallel plates, calculate $\Phi$.

**Solution:**

Only non-zero derivative: $\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = U/h$

$$\Phi = \mu\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)^2 = \mu\left(\frac{U}{h}\right)^2$$

**Heat generation rate** per unit volume from shearing.

Total power dissipated per unit area:
$$\dot{q} = \int_0^h \Phi \, dy = \mu\left(\frac{U}{h}\right)^2 h = \frac{\mu U^2}{h}$$

**Answer:** $\Phi = \mu U^2/h^2$; total $\dot{q} = \mu U^2/h$

## Example 2: Temperature Rise in Poiseuille Flow

For pipe flow $u(r) = \frac{G}{4\mu}(R^2 - r^2)$, find $\Phi(r)$.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{du}{dr} = -\frac{Gr}{2\mu}$$

$$\Phi = \mu\left(\frac{du}{dr}\right)^2 = \mu\left(\frac{Gr}{2\mu}\right)^2 = \frac{G^2r^2}{4\mu}$$

**Maximum at wall** ($r = R$): $\Phi_{max} = \frac{G^2R^2}{4\mu}$

**Average**:
$$\langle\Phi\rangle = \frac{\int_0^R \Phi \cdot 2\pi r \, dr}{\pi R^2} = \frac{G^2R^2}{6\mu}$$

**Answer:** $\Phi(r) = \frac{G^2r^2}{4\mu}$

## Example 3: Bernoulli for Flow from Tank

Water flows from large tank (surface at $P_{atm}$, $u \approx 0$) through orifice at depth $h$.

Find exit velocity.

**Solution:**

Apply Bernoulli between surface (1) and exit (2):

$$P_1 + \frac{1}{2}\rho u_1^2 + \rho g z_1 = P_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho u_2^2 + \rho g z_2$$

With $P_1 = P_2 = P_{atm}$, $u_1 \approx 0$, $z_1 - z_2 = h$:

$$\rho g h = \frac{1}{2}\rho u_2^2$$

$$u_2 = \sqrt{2gh}$$

**(Torricelli's theorem)**

**Answer:** $u = \sqrt{2gh}$

## Example 4: Venturi Meter Pressure Drop

Flow through Venturi: inlet area $A_1$, throat area $A_2 = A_1/2$, velocity $U_1$.

Find pressure drop $P_1 - P_2$.

**Solution:**

Continuity: $A_1 U_1 = A_2 U_2$

$$U_2 = U_1\frac{A_1}{A_2} = 2U_1$$

Bernoulli (horizontal):

$$P_1 + \frac{1}{2}\rho U_1^2 = P_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho U_2^2$$

$$P_1 - P_2 = \frac{1}{2}\rho(U_2^2 - U_1^2) = \frac{1}{2}\rho(4U_1^2 - U_1^2) = \frac{3}{2}\rho U_1^2$$

**Answer:** $\Delta P = \frac{3}{2}\rho U_1^2$

## Example 5: Stagnation Temperature

For compressible flow at Ma = 2, $T_\infty = 300$ K, find stagnation temperature $T_0$.

**Solution:**

For ideal gas, isentropic relation:

$$T_0 = T_\infty\left(1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2}\text{Ma}^2\right)$$

With $\gamma = 1.4$:

$$T_0 = 300\left(1 + \frac{0.4}{2} \cdot 4\right) = 300(1 + 0.8) = 540 \text{ K}$$

**Significant heating** from kinetic energy!

**Answer:** $T_0 = 540$ K

## Example 6: Power Loss in Pipe

Oil ($\mu = 0.1$ Pa·s, $\rho = 900$ kg/m³) flows through pipe ($D = 0.05$ m, $L = 10$ m) at $Q = 0.001$ m³/s.

Estimate power dissipated by viscosity.

**Solution:**

Average velocity: $U = Q/A = \frac{0.001}{\pi(0.025)^2} = 0.51$ m/s

Reynolds number: $\text{Re} = \frac{\rho U D}{\mu} = \frac{(900)(0.51)(0.05)}{0.1} = 230$ (laminar)

Pressure drop (Hagen-Poiseuille):

$$\Delta P = \frac{32\mu UL}{D^2} = \frac{32(0.1)(0.51)(10)}{(0.05)^2} = 6528 \text{ Pa}$$

Power: $\dot{W} = Q\Delta P = (0.001)(6528) = 6.5$ W

**Answer:** $\dot{W} \approx 6.5$ W

## Example 7: Thermal Entry Length

For pipe flow with wall heating, estimate thermal entry length $L_t$.

**Solution:**

Heat diffuses from wall: $\delta_T \sim \sqrt{\alpha x/U}$ where $\alpha = k/(\rho c_p)$

Fully developed when $\delta_T \sim R$:

$$R \sim \sqrt{\alpha L_t/U}$$

$$L_t \sim \frac{UR^2}{\alpha} = \frac{UD}{\alpha} \cdot \frac{D}{4} = \text{Pe} \cdot \frac{D}{4}$$

where **Péclet number** $\text{Pe} = UD/\alpha = \text{Re} \cdot \text{Pr}$ (Prandtl number $\text{Pr} = \nu/\alpha$).

For $\text{Pe} = 1000$, $D = 0.05$ m:

$$L_t \sim 250 \cdot 0.05 = 12.5 \text{ m}$$

**Answer:** $L_t \sim \text{Pe} \cdot D/4$

## Example 8: Adiabatic Compression Heating

Air compressed isentropically from $P_1 = 1$ bar, $T_1 = 300$ K to $P_2 = 10$ bar.

Find $T_2$.

**Solution:**

Isentropic relation:

$$\frac{T_2}{T_1} = \left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}$$

$$T_2 = 300 \left(10\right)^{0.4/1.4} = 300(10)^{0.286} = 300 \cdot 1.93 = 579 \text{ K}$$

**Significant heating** without heat transfer!

**Answer:** $T_2 = 579$ K

---

## Example 9: Energy Equation Simplification

For low-speed, incompressible flow with negligible dissipation, what simplifies?

**Solution:**

Energy equation:

$$\rho c_p\frac{DT}{Dt} = k\nabla^2 T + \Phi$$

If $\Phi \approx 0$ (low speed, $\text{Ma} \ll 1$):

$$\rho c_p\frac{DT}{Dt} = k\nabla^2 T$$

**Heat equation** with advection (convection-diffusion).

If additionally $\frac{DT}{Dt} \approx 0$ (isothermal):

Temperature field decouples from flow—solve Navier-Stokes without energy equation.

**Answer:** Reduces to convection-diffusion equation

## Example 10: Brinkman Number

The **Brinkman number** measures viscous dissipation vs. conduction:

$$\text{Br} = \frac{\mu U^2}{k\Delta T}$$

For $\mu = 0.001$ Pa·s, $U = 10$ m/s, $k = 0.6$ W/(m·K), $\Delta T = 50$ K:

$$\text{Br} = \frac{(0.001)(100)}{(0.6)(50)} = \frac{0.1}{30} = 0.0033$$

**Interpretation**: $\text{Br} \ll 1$ → dissipation negligible compared to imposed temperature difference.

For $\text{Br} \gg 1$, dissipation dominates (e.g., high-speed lubrication).

**Answer:** Br = 0.0033 (dissipation negligible)

## Example 11: Rankine Vortex Energy

For vortex with $u_\theta = \Gamma/(2\pi r)$ (potential vortex), kinetic energy per unit length?

**Solution:**

$$E = \int_0^\infty \frac{1}{2}\rho u_\theta^2 \cdot 2\pi r \, dr = \pi\rho\int_0^\infty \frac{\Gamma^2}{4\pi^2 r^2} \cdot 2\pi r \, dr$$

$$= \frac{\rho\Gamma^2}{4\pi}\int_0^\infty \frac{dr}{r}$$

**Diverges!** (logarithmic singularity)

**Physical**: Potential vortex has infinite energy—must have core with different structure.

**Rankine vortex**: solid body rotation for $r < a$, potential vortex for $r > a$ (finite energy).

**Answer:** Infinite for pure potential vortex

---

## Example 12: Conduction vs. Convection

Estimate relative importance of conduction $k\nabla^2 T$ vs. advection $\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla T$.

**Solution:**

**Péclet number**:

$$\text{Pe} = \frac{UL}{\alpha} = \frac{\text{advection}}{\text{diffusion}}$$

where $\alpha = k/(\rho c_p)$ is thermal diffusivity.

For air ($\alpha \sim 2 \times 10^{-5}$ m²/s), $U = 1$ m/s, $L = 0.1$ m:

$$\text{Pe} = \frac{(1)(0.1)}{2 \times 10^{-5}} = 5000$$

**Convection dominates** (heat carried by flow $\gg$ conducted).

For $\text{Pe} \ll 1$: conduction dominates (e.g., low speeds, small scales).

**Answer:** Pe = 5000; convection dominates

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C** - $\Phi = \tau_{ij}\dot{\epsilon}_{ij}$ is rate of irreversible conversion of mechanical energy to heat.

**Problem 4: C** - $-P\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u}$ is work done by pressure forces during compression/expansion.
