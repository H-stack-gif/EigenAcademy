# Article 4.4: Viscous Flow in Pipes and Channels

The transition from smooth, predictable laminar flow to chaotic turbulent flow fundamentally changes pipe flow characteristics. At **critical Reynolds number** $\text{Re}_D \approx 2300$, instabilities amplify and laminar pipe flow becomes turbulent—mixing dramatically increases, velocity profiles fatten, and pressure drop rises sharply. **Laminar flow** ($\text{Re}_D < 2300$) obeys Hagen-Poiseuille law with pressure drop proportional to flow rate: $\Delta P \propto Q$. **Turbulent flow** ($\text{Re}_D > 4000$) exhibits rough proportionality $\Delta P \propto Q^{1.75-2}$ and depends on surface roughness. The **friction factor** $f$ quantifies resistance: $\Delta P = f\frac{L}{D}\frac{1}{2}\rho V^2$, with laminar giving $f = 64/\text{Re}_D$ and turbulent requiring empirical correlations (Colebrook equation, Moody diagram). **Entry length** $L_e$ for flow development scales differently: $L_e/D \approx 0.06\text{Re}_D$ (laminar) vs. $L_e/D \approx 10$ (turbulent, nearly Re-independent).

The **Moody diagram** masterfully consolidates friction factor data across Reynolds numbers and relative roughness $\epsilon/D$, showing laminar regime's single curve $f = 64/\text{Re}_D$, turbulent smooth-pipe curves decreasing with Re, and fully rough asymptotes where $f$ becomes Re-independent. The **Colebrook equation** $\frac{1}{\sqrt{f}} = -2\log_{10}\left(\frac{\epsilon/D}{3.7} + \frac{2.51}{\text{Re}_D\sqrt{f}}\right)$ implicitly relates $f$ to Re and $\epsilon/D$, requiring iteration but capturing smooth-to-rough transition. **Non-circular ducts** use **hydraulic diameter** $D_h = 4A/P$ (4 times cross-sectional area divided by wetted perimeter) to collapse rectangular, annular, and other geometries onto circular-pipe correlations. **Major losses** (friction over length) combine with **minor losses** (bends, valves, contractions) where $\Delta P_{minor} = K_L\frac{1}{2}\rho V^2$.

This article reviews Hagen-Poiseuille law from Unit 4.1, derives friction factor definition and laminar result $f = 64/\text{Re}_D$, discusses transition to turbulence and critical Reynolds number, introduces Moody diagram structure and usage, develops Colebrook equation and iterative solution methods, treats hydraulic diameter for non-circular geometries, analyzes entry length development ($L_e/D \approx 0.06\text{Re}_D$ laminar), covers minor losses with loss coefficients $K_L$, discusses network analysis basics, and works through extensive examples involving pressure drop calculations, friction factor determination, flow rate solutions, pump power requirements, transition prediction, entry length estimates, minor loss quantification, and pipe network problems. The mathematics involves **empirical correlations, implicit equation solution (Newton-Raphson for Colebrook), logarithmic relationships, and systems of equations for networks**.

## Hagen-Poiseuille Law Review

**Laminar pipe flow** ($\text{Re}_D < 2300$):

$$\boxed{Q = \frac{\pi R^4}{8\mu}\left(-\frac{dP}{dx}\right) = \frac{\pi D^4\Delta P}{128\mu L}}$$

**Pressure drop**:
$$\Delta P = \frac{128\mu LQ}{\pi D^4} = \frac{8\mu L\bar{V}}{\pi R^2}$$

where $\bar{V} = Q/(\pi R^2)$ is average velocity.

**Linear dependence**: $\Delta P \propto Q$ (laminar characteristic)

## Friction Factor Definition

**Darcy-Weisbach equation**:

$$\boxed{\Delta P = f\frac{L}{D}\frac{1}{2}\rho \bar{V}^2}$$

where $f$ = **Darcy friction factor** (dimensionless).

**Head loss form**:
$$h_f = \frac{\Delta P}{\rho g} = f\frac{L}{D}\frac{\bar{V}^2}{2g}$$

## Friction Factor: Laminar Flow

From Hagen-Poiseuille:
$$\Delta P = \frac{32\mu \bar{V} L}{D^2}$$

Compare to Darcy-Weisbach:
$$\Delta P = f\frac{L}{D}\frac{1}{2}\rho \bar{V}^2$$

Equate:
$$f\frac{1}{2}\rho \bar{V}^2 = \frac{32\mu \bar{V}}{D}$$

$$\boxed{f = \frac{64\mu}{\rho \bar{V} D} = \frac{64}{\text{Re}_D}}$$

**Independent of roughness** (viscosity dominates).

## Transition to Turbulence

**Critical Reynolds number**: $\text{Re}_{D,crit} \approx 2300$

**Transition range**: $2000 < \text{Re}_D < 4000$ (unstable, intermittent)

For $\text{Re}_D > 4000$: Fully turbulent

**Physics**: Laminar flow becomes unstable → small disturbances amplify → transition to chaotic turbulent motion.

**Factors affecting transition**:
- Pipe roughness (promotes earlier transition)
- Inlet conditions (smooth inlet delays transition)
- Vibrations (promote transition)

## Friction Factor: Turbulent Flow

### Smooth Pipes

**Blasius correlation** ($4000 < \text{Re}_D < 10^5$):
$$f = \frac{0.316}{\text{Re}_D^{1/4}}$$

**Prandtl-von Kármán** (higher Re):
$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{f}} = 2.0\log_{10}(\text{Re}_D\sqrt{f}) - 0.8$$

(implicit in $f$, requires iteration)

### Rough Pipes

**Colebrook equation** (general, valid for all regimes):

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{\sqrt{f}} = -2.0\log_{10}\left(\frac{\epsilon/D}{3.7} + \frac{2.51}{\text{Re}_D\sqrt{f}}\right)}$$

where $\epsilon$ = **absolute roughness** (sand-grain equivalent).

**Implicit** in $f$ → solve iteratively (Newton-Raphson) or use Moody diagram.

**Fully rough regime** ($\text{Re}_D \to \infty$, roughness dominates):

$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{f}} = 2.0\log_{10}\left(\frac{3.7D}{\epsilon}\right)$$

$f$ independent of Re!

**Typical roughness values**:
- Smooth pipes (drawn tubing): $\epsilon < 0.001$ mm
- Commercial steel: $\epsilon \approx 0.045$ mm
- Cast iron: $\epsilon \approx 0.26$ mm
- Concrete: $\epsilon \approx 1-3$ mm
- Riveted steel: $\epsilon \approx 1-10$ mm

## Moody Diagram

**Graphical representation** of $f$ vs. $\text{Re}_D$ for various $\epsilon/D$.

**Features**:
1. **Laminar zone**: $f = 64/\text{Re}_D$ (straight line, slope −1 on log-log)
2. **Transition**: $2000 < \text{Re}_D < 4000$ (shaded, uncertain)
3. **Turbulent smooth**: Curves decreasing with Re
4. **Turbulent rough**: Curves flatten (fully rough asymptote)

**Usage**:
1. Calculate $\text{Re}_D$ and $\epsilon/D$
2. Locate on diagram
3. Read $f$
4. Calculate $\Delta P$ from Darcy-Weisbach

## Entry Length

**Distance for velocity profile to fully develop**:

**Laminar**:
$$\boxed{\frac{L_e}{D} \approx 0.06\text{Re}_D}$$

For $\text{Re}_D = 2000$: $L_e \approx 120D$ (long!)

**Turbulent**:
$$\boxed{\frac{L_e}{D} \approx 10}$$

Much shorter, nearly independent of Re.

**In entry region**: Higher pressure drop (developing profiles have steeper gradients).

## Hydraulic Diameter (Non-Circular Ducts)

For arbitrary cross-section:

$$\boxed{D_h = \frac{4A}{P}}$$

where $A$ = cross-sectional area, $P$ = wetted perimeter.

**Circular pipe check**: $D_h = \frac{4(\pi D^2/4)}{\pi D} = D$ ✓

**Examples**:
- Square ($a \times a$): $D_h = \frac{4a^2}{4a} = a$
- Rectangle ($a \times b$): $D_h = \frac{4ab}{2(a+b)} = \frac{2ab}{a+b}$
- Annulus ($D_o$, $D_i$): $D_h = D_o - D_i$

**Use $D_h$ in place of $D$** for Re, $f$, and pressure drop calculations.

## Minor Losses

**Caused by**: Bends, valves, contractions, expansions, fittings.

**Loss coefficient** $K_L$:

$$\boxed{\Delta P_{minor} = K_L \cdot \frac{1}{2}\rho \bar{V}^2}$$

**Typical values**:
- **90° elbow**: $K_L \approx 0.9$
- **45° elbow**: $K_L \approx 0.4$
- **Globe valve** (open): $K_L \approx 10$
- **Gate valve** (open): $K_L \approx 0.2$
- **Sudden expansion**: $K_L = \left(1 - \frac{A_1}{A_2}\right)^2$
- **Sudden contraction**: $K_L \approx 0.5\left(1 - \frac{A_2}{A_1}\right)$

**Total head loss**:
$$h_{total} = f\frac{L}{D}\frac{\bar{V}^2}{2g} + \sum K_L\frac{\bar{V}^2}{2g}$$

**Equivalent length**: $L_{eq} = K_L D/f$ (length of pipe with same loss)

## Example 1: Laminar Friction Factor

Water flows in pipe $D = 0.02$ m at $\bar{V} = 0.05$ m/s ($\nu = 10^{-6}$ m²/s).

Find $f$ and verify laminar.

**Solution:**

$$\text{Re}_D = \frac{\bar{V}D}{\nu} = \frac{(0.05)(0.02)}{10^{-6}} = 1000 < 2300$$ ✓ (laminar)

$$f = \frac{64}{1000} = 0.064$$

**Answer:** $f = 0.064$ (laminar)

---

## Example 2: Turbulent Pressure Drop

Water pipe $D = 0.05$ m, $L = 100$ m, $\bar{V} = 2$ m/s, smooth.

Find $\Delta P$ ($\rho = 1000$ kg/m³, $\nu = 10^{-6}$ m²/s).

**Solution:**

$$\text{Re}_D = \frac{(2)(0.05)}{10^{-6}} = 10^5$$ (turbulent)

Blasius: $f = \frac{0.316}{(10^5)^{0.25}} = \frac{0.316}{17.8} = 0.0178$

$$\Delta P = (0.0178)\frac{100}{0.05}\frac{1}{2}(1000)(4)$$

$$= (0.0178)(2000)(2000) = 71,200 \text{ Pa} = 71.2 \text{ kPa}$$

**Answer:** $\Delta P = 71.2$ kPa

---

## Example 3: Transition Reynolds Number Check

Flow in pipe $D = 0.03$ m, $\bar{V} = 0.08$ m/s (oil $\nu = 10^{-5}$ m²/s).

Is flow laminar or turbulent?

**Solution:**

$$\text{Re}_D = \frac{(0.08)(0.03)}{10^{-5}} = 240 \ll 2300$$

**Definitely laminar.**

**Answer:** Laminar ($\text{Re}_D = 240$)

## Example 4: Moody Diagram Usage

Rough pipe: $\epsilon = 0.26$ mm, $D = 0.1$ m, $\text{Re}_D = 10^6$.

Find $f$ from Moody diagram.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{\epsilon}{D} = \frac{0.26}{100} = 0.0026$$

From Moody diagram at $\text{Re}_D = 10^6$, $\epsilon/D = 0.0026$:

$f \approx 0.020$

(Alternatively, solve Colebrook numerically)

**Answer:** $f \approx 0.020$

## Example 5: Colebrook Iteration

Solve Colebrook for $f$: $\epsilon/D = 0.001$, $\text{Re}_D = 10^5$.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{f}} = -2\log_{10}\left(\frac{0.001}{3.7} + \frac{2.51}{10^5\sqrt{f}}\right)$$

**Initial guess** (Blasius): $f_0 = 0.018$

**Iteration 1**:
$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{0.018}} = 7.45$$

RHS: $-2\log_{10}(2.7 \times 10^{-4} + 1.87 \times 10^{-4}) = -2\log_{10}(4.57 \times 10^{-4}) = 6.72$

**New**: $f_1 = 1/(6.72)^2 = 0.0221$

**Iteration 2**: Repeat... converges to $f \approx 0.0226$

**Answer:** $f \approx 0.023$

## Example 6: Entry Length Calculation

Laminar flow, $\text{Re}_D = 1500$, $D = 0.04$ m.

Find $L_e$.

**Solution:**

$$L_e = 0.06\text{Re}_D \cdot D = 0.06(1500)(0.04) = 3.6 \text{ m}$$

**Long entry region**: $L_e/D = 90$!

**Answer:** $L_e = 3.6$ m

## Example 7: Hydraulic Diameter (Rectangle)

Duct $20$ cm × $10$ cm.

Find $D_h$.

**Solution:**

$$A = (0.2)(0.1) = 0.02 \text{ m}^2$$

$$P = 2(0.2 + 0.1) = 0.6 \text{ m}$$

$$D_h = \frac{4(0.02)}{0.6} = \frac{0.08}{0.6} = 0.133 \text{ m} = 13.3 \text{ cm}$$

**Use in Re**: $\text{Re}_{D_h} = \frac{\bar{V}D_h}{\nu}$

**Answer:** $D_h = 13.3$ cm

## Example 8: Minor Loss (Globe Valve)

Flow $\bar{V} = 3$ m/s through globe valve ($K_L = 10$).

Find pressure drop.

**Solution:**

$$\Delta P = K_L \cdot \frac{1}{2}\rho \bar{V}^2 = (10) \cdot \frac{1}{2}(1000)(9)$$

$$= 10 \cdot 4500 = 45,000 \text{ Pa} = 45 \text{ kPa}$$

**Significant loss** from valve!

**Equivalent length**: For $f = 0.02$, $D = 0.05$ m:
$$L_{eq} = \frac{K_L D}{f} = \frac{10(0.05)}{0.02} = 25 \text{ m}$$

Valve equivalent to 25 m of straight pipe!

**Answer:** $\Delta P = 45$ kPa

---

## Example 9: Sudden Expansion Loss

Pipe expands from $D_1 = 0.05$ m to $D_2 = 0.1$ m, $\bar{V}_1 = 4$ m/s.

Find pressure change.

**Solution:**

$$K_L = \left(1 - \frac{A_1}{A_2}\right)^2 = \left(1 - \frac{D_1^2}{D_2^2}\right)^2 = \left(1 - \frac{1}{4}\right)^2 = 0.5625$$

Continuity: $\bar{V}_2 = \bar{V}_1(A_1/A_2) = 4/4 = 1$ m/s

Bernoulli with loss:
$$P_1 + \frac{1}{2}\rho \bar{V}_1^2 = P_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho \bar{V}_2^2 + K_L\frac{1}{2}\rho \bar{V}_1^2$$

$$P_2 - P_1 = \frac{1}{2}\rho(\bar{V}_1^2 - \bar{V}_2^2) - K_L\frac{1}{2}\rho \bar{V}_1^2$$

$$= \frac{1}{2}(1000)(16 - 1) - 0.5625 \cdot \frac{1}{2}(1000)(16)$$

$$= 7500 - 4500 = 3000 \text{ Pa}$$

**Pressure rises** but not fully (loss prevents full recovery).

**Answer:** $\Delta P = +3.0$ kPa (increase)

## Example 10: Pump Power Requirement

Pipe system: $L = 200$ m, $D = 0.08$ m, $Q = 0.01$ m³/s, $f = 0.02$, plus 5 elbows ($K_L = 0.9$ each).

Find pump power ($\eta = 0.75$).

**Solution:**

$$\bar{V} = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{0.01}{\pi(0.04)^2} = \frac{0.01}{0.00503} = 1.99 \text{ m/s}$$

Major loss:
$$\Delta P_{major} = (0.02)\frac{200}{0.08}\frac{1}{2}(1000)(1.99)^2 = (0.02)(2500)(1980) = 99,000 \text{ Pa}$$

Minor loss:
$$\Delta P_{minor} = 5(0.9) \cdot \frac{1}{2}(1000)(1.99)^2 = 4.5(1980) = 8910 \text{ Pa}$$

Total: $\Delta P = 99,000 + 8910 = 107,910$ Pa

Pump power:
$$\dot{W} = \frac{Q\Delta P}{\eta} = \frac{(0.01)(107,910)}{0.75} = 1439 \text{ W} = 1.44 \text{ kW}$$

**Answer:** $\dot{W} = 1.44$ kW

---

## Example 11: Fully Rough Regime

Very rough pipe: $\epsilon = 3$ mm, $D = 0.1$ m, $\text{Re}_D = 10^7$.

Is flow fully rough? Find $f$.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{\epsilon}{D} = \frac{3}{100} = 0.03$$

**Fully rough criterion**: Check if roughness term $\gg$ viscous term in Colebrook.

$$\frac{\epsilon/D}{3.7} = \frac{0.03}{3.7} = 0.0081$$

$$\frac{2.51}{\text{Re}_D\sqrt{f}} = \frac{2.51}{10^7\sqrt{0.04}} \approx 1.3 \times 10^{-6}$$

$0.0081 \gg 1.3 \times 10^{-6}$ ✓ **Fully rough!**

$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{f}} = 2\log_{10}\left(\frac{3.7(0.1)}{0.003}\right) = 2\log_{10}(123.3) = 2(2.091) = 4.18$$

$$f = 1/(4.18)^2 = 0.0572$$

**Answer:** Yes, fully rough; $f = 0.057$

---

## Example 12: Laminar vs. Turbulent Scaling

If flow rate doubles in laminar vs. turbulent regime, how does $\Delta P$ change?

**Solution:**

**Laminar**: $\Delta P \propto Q$ (Hagen-Poiseuille)
→ $Q$ doubles → $\Delta P$ doubles

**Turbulent**: $\Delta P \propto \bar{V}^2 \propto Q^2$ (roughly)
→ $Q$ doubles → $\Delta P$ quadruples

**Answer:** Laminar: 2×; Turbulent: 4×
