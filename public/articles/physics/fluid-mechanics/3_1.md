# Article 3.1: Bernoulli's Equation and Applications

The Euler equation governs inviscid flow—fluid motion without viscosity. While all real fluids possess some viscosity, the inviscid approximation becomes remarkably accurate at high Reynolds numbers outside boundary layers, and provides powerful analytical tools unavailable for viscous flows. From the Euler equation emerges **Bernoulli's equation**, perhaps the most celebrated result in fluid mechanics: along a streamline in steady, inviscid, incompressible flow, the sum of pressure, kinetic energy per unit volume, and potential energy per unit volume remains constant. This elegant principle—essentially energy conservation for inviscid flow—connects pressure drops to velocity increases, explains lift on airfoils, and enables countless practical applications from Venturi meters to Pitot tubes.

**Bernoulli's equation** takes multiple forms depending on assumptions. The standard form $P + \frac{1}{2}\rho u^2 + \rho gz = \text{constant}$ applies along streamlines in steady flow. For **irrotational flow** (zero vorticity), the constant is the same everywhere, not just along streamlines—a much stronger statement. The **unsteady Bernoulli equation** adds a time-dependent term $\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t}$ involving the velocity potential. Understanding when Bernoulli applies (and crucially, when it fails) is essential: viscous losses, unsteady effects, and compressibility all violate its assumptions. **Stagnation pressure** $P_0 = P + \frac{1}{2}\rho u^2$ represents total pressure when fluid is brought to rest isentropically—a key concept for flow measurement and high-speed aerodynamics.

This article derives Bernoulli's equation from the Euler equation along streamlines, extends it to irrotational flows, introduces the unsteady form with velocity potential, explores stagnation pressure and its measurement, demonstrates applications including Pitot tubes, Venturi meters, and orifices, discusses limitations and validity carefully, derives energy interpretation, and works through extensive examples involving pressure-velocity relationships, flow measurements, siphons, jets, and lift estimation. The mathematics involves vector calculus (line integrals, gradients), ordinary differential equations along streamlines, and algebraic manipulations connecting pressure and velocity.

## Euler Equation Review

For **inviscid flow** ($\mu = 0$), Navier-Stokes reduces to **Euler equation**:

$$\boxed{\rho\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} = -\nabla P + \rho \mathbf{g}}$$

Expanded:
$$\rho\left(\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u}\right) = -\nabla P + \rho \mathbf{g}$$

**Key feature**: No viscous dissipation → flow is **reversible**.

## Bernoulli Equation Derivation (Steady Flow)

For **steady flow** ($\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} = 0$), Euler becomes:

$$\rho(\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u} = -\nabla P + \rho \mathbf{g}$$

**Along a streamline**, parameterized by arc length $s$:

$$\rho \mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla(\mathbf{u}^2/2) = -\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla P + \rho \mathbf{g} \cdot \mathbf{u}$$

Using $\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla(\mathbf{u}^2/2) = u\frac{d(\mathbf{u}^2/2)}{ds}$ and $\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla P = u\frac{dP}{ds}$:

$$\rho u\frac{d(\mathbf{u}^2/2)}{ds} = -u\frac{dP}{ds} - \rho g u\frac{dz}{ds}$$

Divide by $\rho u$:

$$\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{\mathbf{u}^2}{2}\right) = -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{dP}{ds} - g\frac{dz}{ds}$$

**Integrate along streamline** (constant $\rho$):

$$\boxed{P + \frac{1}{2}\rho \mathbf{u}^2 + \rho gz = \text{constant (along streamline)}}$$

This is **Bernoulli's equation** in its standard form.

**Alternative form** (dividing by $\rho g$):

$$\frac{P}{\rho g} + \frac{\mathbf{u}^2}{2g} + z = H$$

where $H$ is **total head** (units: length).

**Terms**:
- $P/(\rho g)$: **pressure head**
- $\mathbf{u}^2/(2g)$: **velocity head** (dynamic head)
- $z$: **elevation head**

## Stagnation Pressure

At a **stagnation point** where flow velocity $\mathbf{u} = 0$, pressure reaches maximum:

$$\boxed{P_0 = P_\infty + \frac{1}{2}\rho U_\infty^2}$$

**Terms**:
- $P_0$: **stagnation pressure** (total pressure)
- $P_\infty$: **static pressure** (ambient pressure)
- $\frac{1}{2}\rho U_\infty^2$: **dynamic pressure**

**Physical meaning**: Kinetic energy converted to pressure when fluid decelerates to rest.

**Applications**: 
- Pitot tube measures $P_0$
- Static port measures $P_\infty$
- Difference gives velocity: $U_\infty = \sqrt{2(P_0 - P_\infty)/\rho}$

## Irrotational Flow Extension

For **irrotational flow** ($\nabla \times \mathbf{u} = 0$), introduce **velocity potential** $\phi$:

$$\mathbf{u} = \nabla \phi$$

Euler equation becomes:

$$\nabla\left(\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} + \frac{(\nabla\phi)^2}{2} + \frac{P}{\rho} + gz\right) = 0$$

This implies:

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} + \frac{\mathbf{u}^2}{2} + \frac{P}{\rho} + gz = f(t)}$$

**Unsteady Bernoulli equation** for irrotational flow.

For **steady, irrotational flow**: $\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} = 0$, $f(t) = C$

$$\boxed{P + \frac{1}{2}\rho \mathbf{u}^2 + \rho gz = C \quad \text{(everywhere!)}$$

**Crucial difference**: Constant is the **same throughout flow field**, not just along streamlines.

## Limitations and Validity

Bernoulli's equation **requires**:
1. **Inviscid**: $\mu = 0$ (or high Re, outside boundary layers)
2. **Incompressible**: $\rho = \text{const}$ (or Ma $< 0.3$)
3. **Steady**: $\frac{\partial}{\partial t} = 0$ (for standard form)
4. **Along streamline**: (unless irrotational)

**Failures**:
- Viscous losses (pipe friction, wakes)
- Unsteady flow (unless using $\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t}$ form)
- Compressible flow (shock waves, high Mach)
- Across streamlines in rotational flow

**Engineering practice**: Often applied with **loss terms** added empirically.

## Pitot-Static Tube

**Pitot tube**: Measures stagnation pressure $P_0$

**Static port**: Measures static pressure $P$

**Velocity**:
$$U = \sqrt{\frac{2(P_0 - P)}{\rho}}$$

**Pressure coefficient**: $C_P = \frac{P - P_\infty}{\frac{1}{2}\rho U_\infty^2}$

At stagnation point: $C_P = 1$

## Example 1: Flow from Tank

Water drains from large tank through orifice at depth $h = 5$ m below surface.

Find exit velocity (neglect losses).

**Solution:**

Apply Bernoulli between surface (1) and orifice (2):

$$P_1 + \frac{1}{2}\rho u_1^2 + \rho g z_1 = P_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho u_2^2 + \rho g z_2$$

- $P_1 = P_2 = P_{atm}$ (atmospheric)
- $u_1 \approx 0$ (large tank)
- $z_1 - z_2 = h$

$$\rho g h = \frac{1}{2}\rho u_2^2$$

$$u_2 = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2(9.81)(5)} = \sqrt{98.1} = 9.90 \text{ m/s}$$

**Torricelli's theorem**: Same as free fall from height $h$.

**Answer:** $u = 9.90$ m/s

## Example 2: Venturi Meter

Venturi meter: inlet diameter $D_1 = 0.1$ m, throat $D_2 = 0.05$ m.

Water flows at $Q = 0.02$ m³/s. Find pressure drop $P_1 - P_2$.

**Solution:**

Velocities:
$$u_1 = \frac{Q}{A_1} = \frac{0.02}{\pi(0.05)^2} = 2.55 \text{ m/s}$$

$$u_2 = \frac{Q}{A_2} = \frac{0.02}{\pi(0.025)^2} = 10.19 \text{ m/s}$$

Bernoulli (horizontal):
$$P_1 + \frac{1}{2}\rho u_1^2 = P_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho u_2^2$$

$$P_1 - P_2 = \frac{1}{2}\rho(u_2^2 - u_1^2)$$

$$= \frac{1}{2}(1000)(10.19^2 - 2.55^2)$$

$$= 500(103.8 - 6.5) = 48,650 \text{ Pa} = 48.7 \text{ kPa}$$

**Answer:** $\Delta P = 48.7$ kPa

## Example 3: Pitot Tube in Air

Aircraft Pitot tube measures $P_0 - P = 2500$ Pa. Air density $\rho = 1.2$ kg/m³.

Find airspeed.

**Solution:**

$$U = \sqrt{\frac{2(P_0 - P)}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(2500)}{1.2}}$$

$$= \sqrt{4167} = 64.6 \text{ m/s}$$

Convert to km/h: $64.6 \times 3.6 = 233$ km/h

**Answer:** $U = 64.6$ m/s (233 km/h)

## Example 4: Siphon Maximum Height

Siphon tube diameter $d$, lifts water over height $h$ above reservoir.

Maximum $h$ before cavitation? (Vapor pressure $P_v = 2$ kPa, $P_{atm} = 101$ kPa)

**Solution:**

At highest point (2), Bernoulli from surface (1):

$$P_1 + \rho g(0) = P_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho u^2 + \rho g h$$

$$P_{atm} = P_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho u^2 + \rho g h$$

Minimum pressure at top: $P_2 \geq P_v$ to avoid cavitation.

For small velocity (large reservoir, thin tube): $u^2$ term small.

$$P_{atm} - \rho g h_{max} \approx P_v$$

$$h_{max} = \frac{P_{atm} - P_v}{\rho g} = \frac{101,000 - 2,000}{(1000)(9.81)} = \frac{99,000}{9,810} = 10.1 \text{ m}$$

**Answer:** $h_{max} \approx 10$ m (theoretical)

## Example 5: Jet Trajectory

Water jet exits horizontally at $u_0 = 10$ m/s from height $h = 2$ m.

Find horizontal range $R$.

**Solution:**

Vertical motion (free fall):
$$h = \frac{1}{2}gt^2 \implies t = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(2)}{9.81}} = 0.639 \text{ s}$$

Horizontal motion (constant $u_0$):
$$R = u_0 t = (10)(0.639) = 6.39 \text{ m}$$

**Note**: Bernoulli gives initial velocity; kinematics gives trajectory.

**Answer:** $R = 6.4$ m

## Example 6: Pressure Coefficient

In flow past cylinder, at angle $\theta = 90°$ from stagnation point, velocity is $u = 2U_\infty$.

Find pressure coefficient $C_P$.

**Solution:**

$$C_P = \frac{P - P_\infty}{\frac{1}{2}\rho U_\infty^2}$$

Bernoulli:
$$P_\infty + \frac{1}{2}\rho U_\infty^2 = P + \frac{1}{2}\rho u^2$$

$$P - P_\infty = \frac{1}{2}\rho(U_\infty^2 - u^2) = \frac{1}{2}\rho(U_\infty^2 - 4U_\infty^2) = -\frac{3}{2}\rho U_\infty^2$$

$$C_P = \frac{-\frac{3}{2}\rho U_\infty^2}{\frac{1}{2}\rho U_\infty^2} = -3$$

**Negative**: Pressure drop (suction) where velocity increases.

**Answer:** $C_P = -3$

## Example 7: Nozzle Flow Rate

Converging nozzle: inlet $D_1 = 0.1$ m at $P_1 = 200$ kPa, exit $D_2 = 0.05$ m at $P_2 = 101$ kPa.

Find flow rate (water, $\rho = 1000$ kg/m³).

**Solution:**

Bernoulli:
$$P_1 + \frac{1}{2}\rho u_1^2 = P_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho u_2^2$$

Continuity: $A_1 u_1 = A_2 u_2 \implies u_1 = u_2(A_2/A_1) = u_2/4$

Substitute:
$$P_1 + \frac{1}{2}\rho(u_2/4)^2 = P_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho u_2^2$$

$$P_1 - P_2 = \frac{1}{2}\rho u_2^2\left(1 - \frac{1}{16}\right) = \frac{15}{32}\rho u_2^2$$

$$u_2 = \sqrt{\frac{32(P_1 - P_2)}{15\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{32(99,000)}{15(1000)}} = \sqrt{211.2} = 14.5 \text{ m/s}$$

$$Q = A_2 u_2 = \pi(0.025)^2(14.5) = 0.0285 \text{ m}^3\text{/s}$$

**Answer:** $Q = 28.5$ L/s

## Example 8: Unsteady Draining Tank

Tank area $A_t = 1$ m², orifice area $A_o = 0.001$ m², initial depth $h_0 = 4$ m.

Find time to drain to $h = 1$ m (use Bernoulli + continuity).

**Solution:**

Unsteady: tank level drops at rate $\frac{dh}{dt}$.

Continuity: $A_t\frac{dh}{dt} = -A_o u$

Bernoulli (instantaneous): $\rho g h = \frac{1}{2}\rho u^2$ (neglect $\dot{u}$ term)

$$u = \sqrt{2gh}$$

$$A_t\frac{dh}{dt} = -A_o\sqrt{2gh}$$

$$\frac{dh}{\sqrt{h}} = -\frac{A_o}{A_t}\sqrt{2g} \, dt$$

Integrate:
$$2(\sqrt{h_f} - \sqrt{h_0}) = -\frac{A_o}{A_t}\sqrt{2g} \, t$$

$$t = \frac{2A_t}{A_o\sqrt{2g}}(\sqrt{h_0} - \sqrt{h_f})$$

$$= \frac{2(1)}{0.001\sqrt{2(9.81)}}(\sqrt{4} - \sqrt{1})$$

$$= \frac{2000}{4.43}(2 - 1) = 452 \text{ s} = 7.5 \text{ min}$$

**Answer:** $t = 7.5$ min

## Example 9: Wind Pressure on Building

Wind at $U = 30$ m/s hits building wall (stagnation).

Estimate pressure on wall (air $\rho = 1.2$ kg/m³).

**Solution:**

Stagnation pressure:
$$P_0 = P_\infty + \frac{1}{2}\rho U^2$$

Pressure rise:
$$\Delta P = \frac{1}{2}\rho U^2 = \frac{1}{2}(1.2)(30)^2 = 0.6(900) = 540 \text{ Pa}$$

**Force per unit area**: $F/A = 540$ Pa (about 1% of atmospheric pressure)

**Answer:** $\Delta P = 540$ Pa

---

## Example 10: Free Jet from Nozzle

Nozzle diameter $D = 0.02$ m, exit velocity $u = 20$ m/s.

Find force required to hold nozzle (neglect atmospheric pressure).

**Solution:**

**Momentum analysis** (not Bernoulli directly):

Rate of momentum flux: $\dot{m} u = \rho A u^2$

$$F = \rho A u^2 = (1000)\pi(0.01)^2(20)^2$$

$$= 1000(3.14 \times 10^{-4})(400) = 125.6 \text{ N}$$

**Alternative via Bernoulli**: Pressure at inlet (if from reservoir) provides thrust.

**Answer:** $F \approx 126$ N

---

## Example 11: Rotating Cylinder Paradox

Cylinder rotates in flow: inviscid theory predicts no drag (d'Alembert's paradox).

Why does real cylinder experience drag?

**Solution:**

**Inviscid theory**: Pressure distribution symmetric → net force zero.

**Reality**: 
- Boundary layer separates due to **viscosity**
- Low-pressure wake behind cylinder
- Pressure drag results

**Lesson**: Inviscid theory accurate for attached flow but **fails at separation**.

**Answer:** Viscosity causes separation; inviscid theory wrong for separated flows

## Example 12: Compressed Air Tank

Tank at $P_1 = 10$ bar vents through valve to atmosphere ($P_2 = 1$ bar).

Estimate initial exit velocity (air $\gamma = 1.4$, $R = 287$ J/(kg·K), $T_1 = 300$ K).

**Solution:**

For **compressible flow**, use isentropic relation:

$$T_2 = T_1\left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} = 300\left(0.1\right)^{0.286} = 300(0.519) = 156 \text{ K}$$

Energy balance (steady flow energy equation):

$$h_1 = h_2 + \frac{u_2^2}{2}$$

$$c_p(T_1 - T_2) = \frac{u_2^2}{2}$$

$$u_2 = \sqrt{2c_p(T_1 - T_2)} = \sqrt{2(1005)(300 - 156)}$$

$$= \sqrt{2(1005)(144)} = \sqrt{289,440} = 538 \text{ m/s}$$

**Note**: Compressible effects important! (Ma $> 1$ at exit)

**Answer:** $u \approx 538$ m/s (supersonic)
