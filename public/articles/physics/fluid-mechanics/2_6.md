# Article 2.6: Dimensional Analysis and Similarity

Dimensional analysis is one of the most powerful tools in fluid mechanics—it reduces complex problems involving many variables to relationships between a few **dimensionless groups**. The **Buckingham Pi theorem** provides the mathematical foundation: if a physical problem involves $n$ variables with $k$ independent dimensions, the problem can be recast in terms of $n - k$ dimensionless parameters. These dimensionless numbers—**Reynolds**, **Froude**, **Mach**, **Weber**—are not arbitrary mathematical constructs but express fundamental physical balances: inertia vs. viscosity, inertia vs. gravity, flow speed vs. sound speed, inertia vs. surface tension. Understanding which dimensionless numbers govern a flow regime provides immediate physical insight and guides experimental design, numerical simulation, and scaling analysis.

**Nondimensionalization** of the Navier-Stokes equations reveals how dimensionless groups emerge naturally from the governing equations. By choosing characteristic scales (length $L$, velocity $U$, time $T = L/U$), we transform dimensional variables to dimensionless form. The resulting equations contain dimensionless parameters whose values determine the flow regime. For example, the **Reynolds number** $\text{Re} = \rho UL/\mu$ measures inertia relative to viscous forces: at low Re, viscous forces dominate (Stokes flow, creeping flow); at high Re, inertial forces dominate (boundary layers, turbulence). **Dynamic similarity** means two flows with the same dimensionless parameters exhibit identical dimensionless behavior—the basis for model testing in wind tunnels and water channels. **Scaling laws** derived from dimensional analysis predict how forces, power, and other quantities scale with size and speed.

This article develops the Buckingham Pi theorem and its application, introduces major dimensionless numbers and their physical interpretations, demonstrates nondimensionalization of Navier-Stokes equations, explores dynamic similarity and model testing, discusses dominant force balances in different flow regimes, derives scaling laws for drag and lift, and works through extensive examples involving dimensional analysis for various problems, calculation of dimensionless numbers, scaling between models and prototypes, and identification of dominant physics. The mathematics involves linear algebra (rank of dimensional matrix), algebraic manipulation, and systematic scaling arguments.

## Buckingham Pi Theorem

**Statement**: If physical problem involves $n$ variables $Q_1, Q_2, \ldots, Q_n$ with $k$ independent dimensions, it can be reduced to relationship among $n - k$ dimensionless groups $\Pi_1, \Pi_2, \ldots, \Pi_{n-k}$:

$$f(Q_1, Q_2, \ldots, Q_n) = 0 \implies F(\Pi_1, \Pi_2, \ldots, \Pi_{n-k}) = 0$$

**Procedure**:
1. List all variables: $Q_1, Q_2, \ldots, Q_n$
2. Identify fundamental dimensions: M (mass), L (length), T (time), $\Theta$ (temperature)
3. Count: $k$ = number of independent dimensions
4. Choose $k$ **repeating variables** (dimensional independent set)
5. Form $n - k$ dimensionless groups by combining each remaining variable with repeating variables
6. Check: each $\Pi_i$ is dimensionless

## Major Dimensionless Numbers

**Reynolds number**:
$$\boxed{\text{Re} = \frac{\rho U L}{\mu} = \frac{UL}{\nu}}$$

Physical meaning: $\frac{\text{inertial forces}}{\text{viscous forces}}$

- Low Re ($\ll 1$): viscous flow (Stokes flow, creeping flow, lubrication)
- High Re ($\gg 1$): inertial flow (boundary layers, turbulence)
- Transition: Re $\approx$ 2300 (pipe flow), varies by geometry

**Froude number**:
$$\boxed{\text{Fr} = \frac{U}{\sqrt{gL}}}$$

Physical meaning: $\frac{\text{inertial forces}}{\text{gravitational forces}}$

- Important when free surfaces present (ships, open channels)
- Fr < 1: subcritical (waves can propagate upstream)
- Fr > 1: supercritical (wave information cannot go upstream)

**Mach number**:
$$\boxed{\text{Ma} = \frac{U}{c}}$$

where $c = \sqrt{\gamma RT}$ is speed of sound.

Physical meaning: $\frac{\text{flow speed}}{\text{information speed}}$

- Ma < 0.3: incompressible approximation valid
- Ma < 1: subsonic
- Ma > 1: supersonic (shocks, expansion waves)

**Weber number**:
$$\boxed{\text{We} = \frac{\rho U^2 L}{\gamma}}$$

Physical meaning: $\frac{\text{inertial forces}}{\text{surface tension forces}}$

- Important for droplets, bubbles, capillary flows
- We $\ll$ 1: surface tension dominates
- We $\gg$ 1: inertia dominates (droplet breakup)

**Euler number**:
$$\boxed{\text{Eu} = \frac{\Delta P}{\rho U^2}}$$

Physical meaning: $\frac{\text{pressure forces}}{\text{inertial forces}}$

- Relates pressure drop to dynamic pressure

**Strouhal number**:
$$\boxed{\text{St} = \frac{fL}{U}}$$

Physical meaning: $\frac{\text{oscillation frequency}}{\text{flow frequency}}$

- Characterizes unsteady, periodic flows
- Vortex shedding: St $\approx$ 0.2 for cylinder

**Prandtl number**:
$$\boxed{\text{Pr} = \frac{\nu}{\alpha} = \frac{\mu c_p}{k}}$$

Physical meaning: $\frac{\text{momentum diffusivity}}{\text{thermal diffusivity}}$

- Pr $\ll$ 1: heat diffuses faster than momentum (liquid metals)
- Pr $\gg$ 1: momentum diffuses faster than heat (oils)

## Nondimensionalization of Navier-Stokes

Define **characteristic scales**:
- Length: $L$ (e.g., chord, diameter, height)
- Velocity: $U$ (e.g., freestream, inlet velocity)
- Time: $T = L/U$ (advective time scale)
- Pressure: $\rho U^2$ (dynamic pressure)

**Dimensionless variables**:
$$\mathbf{r}^* = \frac{\mathbf{r}}{L}, \quad \mathbf{u}^* = \frac{\mathbf{u}}{U}, \quad t^* = \frac{tU}{L}, \quad P^* = \frac{P}{\rho U^2}$$

**Navier-Stokes** (incompressible):
$$\rho\left(\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u}\right) = -\nabla P + \mu\nabla^2\mathbf{u}$$

Substitute and simplify (dropping stars):

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u} = -\nabla P + \frac{1}{\text{Re}}\nabla^2\mathbf{u}}$$

**Result**: Only one parameter—Reynolds number!

**Physical insight**:
- $\text{Re} \gg 1$: $\frac{1}{\text{Re}} \to 0$ → viscous term negligible (Euler equation valid outside BL)
- $\text{Re} \ll 1$: inertia negligible → Stokes equation $0 = -\nabla P + \mu\nabla^2\mathbf{u}$

**With gravity** (add Froude number):
$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u} = -\nabla P + \frac{1}{\text{Re}}\nabla^2\mathbf{u} + \frac{1}{\text{Fr}^2}\mathbf{g}^*$$

## Dynamic Similarity

**Definition**: Two flows are **dynamically similar** if:
1. Geometric similarity (same shape, different size)
2. Kinematic similarity (velocity patterns geometrically similar)
3. **Dynamic similarity**: same dimensionless numbers

**Consequence**: All dimensionless quantities (e.g., $C_D$, $C_L$) are equal.

**Model testing**: Match dimensionless numbers between model and prototype:
$$\text{Re}_m = \text{Re}_p, \quad \text{Fr}_m = \text{Fr}_p, \quad \ldots$$

**Problem**: Often impossible to match all! (e.g., Re and Fr simultaneously in water tunnel)

**Solution**: Match most important number for the phenomenon studied.

## Scaling Laws

From dimensional analysis, derive how quantities scale:

**Drag force**: $F_D = f(\rho, U, L, \mu)$

By Pi theorem: $F_D = \rho U^2 L^2 f(\text{Re})$

Define **drag coefficient**:
$$\boxed{C_D = \frac{F_D}{\frac{1}{2}\rho U^2 A}}$$

where $A \sim L^2$ is reference area.

**Result**: $C_D = C_D(\text{Re})$ only (for given shape).

**Scaling**: At fixed Re, $F_D \propto \rho U^2 L^2$

For geometrically similar bodies: $F_D$ scales as $L^2$ (area scaling).

**Power**: $P = F_D \cdot U \propto U^3 L^2$

For same $U$, $P$ scales as $L^2$; for same Re (different sizes), more complex.

## Example 1: Drag on Sphere

Variables: $F_D$, $\rho$, $U$, $D$ (diameter), $\mu$

Dimensions: $n = 5$, $k = 3$ (M, L, T) → $n - k = 2$ Pi groups

**Solution:**

Choose repeating: $\rho$, $U$, $D$

$\Pi_1$: Combine $F_D$ with $\rho^a U^b D^c$

$$[F_D] = [M L T^{-2}]$$

$$[\rho^a U^b D^c] = [M L^{-3}]^a [L T^{-1}]^b [L]^c = [M^a L^{-3a+b+c} T^{-b}]$$

Match: $a = 1$, $-b = -2 \implies b = 2$, $-3a + b + c = 1 \implies c = 2$

$$\Pi_1 = \frac{F_D}{\rho U^2 D^2}$$

$\Pi_2$: Combine $\mu$ with $\rho^a U^b D^c$

$$[\mu] = [M L^{-1} T^{-1}]$$

Match: $a = -1$, $-b = -1 \implies b = -1$, $-3a + b + c = -1 \implies c = -1$

$$\Pi_2 = \frac{\mu}{\rho U D} = \frac{1}{\text{Re}}$$

**Result**: $\frac{F_D}{\rho U^2 D^2} = f\left(\frac{1}{\text{Re}}\right)$ or $C_D = C_D(\text{Re})$

**Answer:** $F_D = \rho U^2 D^2 f(\text{Re})$

## Example 2: Stokes' Law from Scaling

For very low Re ($\text{Re} \ll 1$), viscous forces dominate.

Scale: inertia $\sim \rho U^2 L^2$, viscous $\sim \mu U L$

At low Re: $F_D \sim \mu U L$ (viscous scale)

From dimensional analysis: $F_D = C \mu U D$ for some constant $C$.

Exact solution (Stokes): $C = 3\pi$ → $F_D = 3\pi\mu UD = 6\pi\mu aU$ (radius $a = D/2$)

**Answer:** $F_D \propto \mu U D$ at low Re

## Example 3: Wind Tunnel Model Scaling

Prototype: airplane wing, $L_p = 30$ m, $U_p = 100$ m/s in air ($\nu_p = 1.5 \times 10^{-5}$ m²/s)

Model: $L_m = 1$ m in same air. Find $U_m$ for same Re.

**Solution:**

$$\text{Re}_p = \frac{U_p L_p}{\nu_p} = \frac{(100)(30)}{1.5 \times 10^{-5}} = 2 \times 10^8$$

$$\text{Re}_m = \frac{U_m L_m}{\nu_m} = 2 \times 10^8$$

$$U_m = \frac{\text{Re}_m \nu_m}{L_m} = \frac{(2 \times 10^8)(1.5 \times 10^{-5})}{1} = 3000 \text{ m/s}$$

**Problem**: $U_m$ too high (supersonic)! Cannot match Re in air at model scale.

**Solution**: Use pressurized wind tunnel (higher density) or water tunnel (lower $\nu$).

**Answer:** $U_m = 3000$ m/s (impractical; need different fluid)

## Example 4: Ship Model Testing

Prototype: ship $L_p = 100$ m, $U_p = 10$ m/s
Model: $L_m = 5$ m (scale 1:20)

Match Froude number: $\text{Fr}_m = \text{Fr}_p$

Find $U_m$.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{U_p}{\sqrt{g L_p}} = \frac{U_m}{\sqrt{g L_m}}$$

$$U_m = U_p\sqrt{\frac{L_m}{L_p}} = 10\sqrt{\frac{5}{100}} = 10 \times 0.224 = 2.24 \text{ m/s}$$

**Check Reynolds numbers**:
$$\text{Re}_p = \frac{U_p L_p}{\nu} = \frac{(10)(100)}{10^{-6}} = 10^9$$

$$\text{Re}_m = \frac{U_m L_m}{\nu} = \frac{(2.24)(5)}{10^{-6}} = 1.12 \times 10^7$$

**Different Re!** Cannot match both Fr and Re simultaneously.

**Engineering solution**: Match Fr (wave resistance), correct viscous drag using empirical correlations.

**Answer:** $U_m = 2.24$ m/s

## Example 5: Capillary Number

For flow involving surface tension, define **capillary number**:

$$\text{Ca} = \frac{\mu U}{\gamma}$$

Physical meaning: $\frac{\text{viscous forces}}{\text{surface tension forces}}$

For $\mu = 0.001$ Pa·s, $U = 0.1$ m/s, $\gamma = 0.07$ N/m:

$$\text{Ca} = \frac{(0.001)(0.1)}{0.07} = 0.0014$$

**Interpretation**: Surface tension dominates ($\text{Ca} \ll 1$) → interfaces remain smooth.

**Answer:** Ca = 0.0014

---

## Example 6: Dominant Balance Identification

For Re = 0.01, Fr = 10, We = 100, which force balance dominates?

**Solution:**

$\text{Re}^{-1} = 100 \gg \text{Fr}^{-2} = 0.01, \text{We}^{-1} = 0.01$

**Viscous forces dominate** over inertia.

Gravity and surface tension much weaker than viscous.

**Flow regime**: Stokes flow (creeping flow).

**Answer:** Viscous forces dominate

---

## Example 7: Pipe Flow Reynolds Number

Water ($\nu = 10^{-6}$ m²/s) flows in pipe $D = 0.05$ m at $Q = 0.001$ m³/s.

Calculate Re and determine flow regime.

**Solution:**

$$U = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{0.001}{\pi(0.025)^2} = 0.51 \text{ m/s}$$

$$\text{Re} = \frac{UD}{\nu} = \frac{(0.51)(0.05)}{10^{-6}} = 25,500$$

**Regime**: Re > 2300 → **turbulent**

**Answer:** Re = 25,500 (turbulent)

## Example 8: Raindrop Terminal Velocity Scaling

Raindrops fall at terminal velocity when drag = weight.

For large drop (Re high), $F_D \propto \rho U^2 D^2$ (inertial drag).

Balance: $\rho_w g \frac{\pi D^3}{6} = C_D \frac{1}{2}\rho_a U^2 \pi D^2/4$

$$U^2 \propto \frac{\rho_w g D}{\rho_a} \implies U \propto \sqrt{D}$$

For small drop (Stokes), $F_D \propto \mu U D$:

$$\rho_w g \frac{\pi D^3}{6} = 3\pi\mu U D$$

$$U \propto D^2$$

**Transition** at Re $\approx 1$: crossover in scaling.

**Answer:** Large: $U \propto \sqrt{D}$; Small: $U \propto D^2$

---

## Example 9: Microfluidic Channel

For $L = 100$ μm, $U = 1$ mm/s, $\nu = 10^{-6}$ m²/s:

$$\text{Re} = \frac{UL}{\nu} = \frac{(10^{-3})(10^{-4})}{10^{-6}} = 0.1$$

**Very low Re** → Stokes flow, reversible (time-reversible!), no turbulence.

Mixing requires diffusion or chaotic advection.

**Answer:** Re = 0.1 (Stokes regime)

## Example 10: Cavitation Number

**Cavitation number**:

$$\sigma = \frac{P_\infty - P_v}{\frac{1}{2}\rho U^2}$$

where $P_v$ is vapor pressure.

If $\sigma < \sigma_{crit}$, cavitation occurs (liquid vaporizes).

For water at 20°C: $P_v \approx 2300$ Pa, $P_\infty = 10^5$ Pa, $U = 20$ m/s, $\rho = 1000$ kg/m³:

$$\sigma = \frac{10^5 - 2300}{\frac{1}{2}(1000)(400)} = \frac{97,700}{200,000} = 0.49$$

Typical $\sigma_{crit} \approx 0.5$ → **near cavitation threshold**.

**Answer:** $\sigma = 0.49$ (cavitation likely)

---

## Example 11: Similarity Parameters

Two flows with same Re, Fr, Ma are **completely similar** (for Newtonian, incompressible).

Example: Cannot achieve this in practice—different fluids/conditions needed.

**Water tunnel**: High Re, low Ma (incompressible)
**Wind tunnel**: Moderate Re, potentially high Ma (compressibility)

**Trade-off**: Match most critical parameter for physics studied.

**Answer:** Complete similarity requires matching all dimensionless numbers

## Example 12: Scaling of Turbine Power

For geometrically similar turbines, power $P = f(\rho, U, D, \omega)$ where $\omega$ is rotation rate.

Dimensional analysis gives:

$$P = \rho U^3 D^2 f\left(\frac{\omega D}{U}\right)$$

**Power coefficient**:

$$C_P = \frac{P}{\rho U^3 D^2} = f(\lambda)$$

where $\lambda = \omega D/U$ is **tip-speed ratio**.

At fixed $\lambda$:

$$P \propto \rho U^3 D^2$$

**Scaling**: Double size ($D \to 2D$) at same $U$ → $P \to 4P$ (quadruples).

**Answer:** $P \propto D^2$ at fixed $U$, $\lambda$
