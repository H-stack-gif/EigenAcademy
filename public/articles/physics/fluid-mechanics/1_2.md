# Fluid Mechanics Topic 1.2: Viscosity and Non-Newtonian Fluids

Stir honey and water with the same spoon at the same speed. Honey resists far more—it's "thicker," more viscous. But what exactly is viscosity? At the molecular level, it's momentum transfer between adjacent fluid layers moving at different speeds. Fast-moving molecules diffuse into slower regions, dragging them along; slow molecules diffuse into faster regions, holding them back. This microscopic momentum exchange manifests macroscopically as a tangential force resisting relative motion between layers—the viscous force. For most simple fluids like water and air, this force is linearly proportional to the velocity gradient: double the gradient, double the force. But not all fluids are so simple.

Blood shear-thins: its apparent viscosity decreases as flow rate increases, a lifesaving feature that reduces resistance in high-flow situations. Cornstarch suspension shear-thickens: stir slowly and it flows; punch it hard and it behaves like a solid. Toothpaste requires a threshold stress before flowing at all—a Bingham plastic. These non-Newtonian behaviors arise from complex microstructures: polymers, particles, or biological cells that respond to flow. Understanding viscosity—both Newtonian and non-Newtonian—is essential for describing momentum transport, predicting flow resistance, and modeling everything from blood circulation to industrial polymer processing.

This article explores viscosity as the fundamental transport property of fluids. We'll define dynamic and kinematic viscosity precisely, develop Newton's law of viscosity for simple shear flows, examine the molecular origin and temperature dependence of viscosity, introduce the stress and strain rate tensors that generalize viscosity to three dimensions, explore non-Newtonian constitutive relations including power-law and Bingham models, and survey rheological behaviors from shear-thinning to viscoelasticity. You'll learn to calculate viscous stresses, classify fluid behaviors, and apply constitutive equations to predict flow response.

## Dynamic and Kinematic Viscosity

**Dynamic viscosity** $\mu$ quantifies momentum diffusivity—the fluid's resistance to shear deformation.

**Newton's law of viscosity** for simple shear flow (fluid between parallel plates):
$$\displaystyle \tau = \mu \frac{du}{dy}$$

where:
- $\tau$ is shear stress (force per unit area, Pa)
- $\mu$ is dynamic viscosity (Pa·s or kg/(m·s))
- $du/dy$ is velocity gradient (shear rate, s⁻¹)

**Kinematic viscosity** $\nu$ is the ratio of dynamic viscosity to density:
$$\displaystyle \nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho}$$

Units: m²/s (like thermal or mass diffusivity). Kinematic viscosity appears naturally in the Navier-Stokes equations for incompressible flow.

**Representative values at 20°C**:
- Water: $\mu = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ Pa·s, $\nu = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}$ m²/s
- Air: $\mu = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ Pa·s, $\nu = 1.5 \times 10^{-5}$ m²/s
- Glycerin: $\mu = 1.5$ Pa·s, $\nu = 1.2 \times 10^{-3}$ m²/s
- SAE 30 oil: $\mu = 0.3$ Pa·s, $\nu = 3 \times 10^{-4}$ m²/s

### Example 1: Computing Shear Stress

A Newtonian fluid with $\mu = 0.02$ Pa·s flows between parallel plates separated by $h = 2$ mm. The bottom plate is stationary; the top plate moves at $U = 0.5$ m/s. Assuming linear velocity profile $u(y) = Uy/h$, calculate the shear stress.

**Solution:**

Velocity gradient:
$$\displaystyle \frac{du}{dy} = \frac{U}{h} = \frac{0.5}{0.002} = 250 \text{ s}^{-1}$$

Shear stress:
$$\tau = \mu \frac{du}{dy} = (0.02)(250) = 5.0 \text{ Pa}$$

This stress acts on both plates: upper plate experiences drag opposing motion; lower plate experiences force in direction of motion (Newton's third law).

**Answer:** $\tau = 5.0$ Pa

### Example 2: Kinematic Viscosity Calculation

For water at 20°C: $\mu = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ Pa·s and $\rho = 998$ kg/m³. Calculate kinematic viscosity.

**Solution:**

$$\displaystyle \nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-3}}{998} = 1.00 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$$

Often approximated as $\nu = 10^{-6}$ m²/s for water at room temperature.

**Answer:** $\nu = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}$ m²/s

### Example 3: Viscosity Ratio

Compare the kinematic viscosities of air and water at 20°C. Which diffuses momentum more readily?

**Solution:**

Air: $\nu_{air} = 1.5 \times 10^{-5}$ m²/s
Water: $\nu_{water} = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}$ m²/s

$$\displaystyle \frac{\nu_{air}}{\nu_{water}} = \frac{1.5 \times 10^{-5}}{1.0 \times 10^{-6}} = 15$$

Air has 15× larger kinematic viscosity despite being less dense. Momentum diffuses faster in air—velocity perturbations spread more quickly.

**Answer:** Air's kinematic viscosity is 15× larger than water's

## Molecular Origin of Viscosity

Viscosity arises from momentum transport at the molecular level.

**In gases**: Molecules moving between layers carry momentum. Faster-moving molecules from upper layer transfer momentum to lower layer (and vice versa). Collision frequency determines viscosity.

**Kinetic theory** gives for gases:
$$\displaystyle \mu \sim \rho \bar{v} \lambda$$

where $\bar{v}$ is mean molecular speed $\propto \sqrt{T}$ and $\lambda$ is mean free path. For ideal gases:
$$\displaystyle \mu \propto \sqrt{T}$$

Viscosity increases with temperature (more vigorous collisions).

**In liquids**: Viscosity arises from intermolecular forces. Molecules must overcome attractive forces to slide past each other. Activation energy $E_a$ governs rate:
$$\displaystyle \mu \propto e^{E_a/(RT)}$$

Viscosity decreases with temperature (thermal energy helps overcome intermolecular forces). Liquids thin when heated; gases thicken.

### Example 4: Temperature Dependence in Air

For air, $\mu \propto T^{3/2}$ (Sutherland's formula simplification). If $\mu = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ Pa·s at $T_1 = 300$ K, estimate $\mu$ at $T_2 = 400$ K.

**Solution:**

$$\displaystyle \frac{\mu_2}{\mu_1} = \left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right)^{3/2}$$

$$\displaystyle \mu_2 = \mu_1 \left(\frac{400}{300}\right)^{3/2} = (1.8 \times 10^{-5})(1.333)^{1.5}$$

$$\displaystyle \mu_2 = (1.8 \times 10^{-5})(1.54) = 2.77 \times 10^{-5} \text{ Pa·s}$$

Viscosity increases by ~50% when temperature increases by 100 K.

**Answer:** $\mu_2 \approx 2.8 \times 10^{-5}$ Pa·s

### Example 5: Arrhenius Behavior in Liquids

A liquid's viscosity follows $\mu = A e^{B/T}$ where $B = 2000$ K. Calculate the ratio $\mu(280\text{ K})/\mu(300\text{ K})$.

**Solution:**

$$\displaystyle \frac{\mu(280)}{\mu(300)} = \frac{A e^{2000/280}}{A e^{2000/300}} = e^{2000(1/280 - 1/300)}$$

$$\displaystyle = e^{2000(0.003571 - 0.003333)} = e^{2000(0.000238)} = e^{0.476}$$

$$\displaystyle = 1.61$$

Viscosity increases by ~60% when temperature drops by 20 K. Liquids become significantly more viscous when cooled.

**Answer:** Viscosity ratio = 1.61

## Stress and Strain Rate Tensors

For general three-dimensional flow, we need tensor formalism.

**Velocity gradient tensor**:
$$\displaystyle \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}$$

This decomposes into symmetric (strain rate) and antisymmetric (rotation) parts:
$$\displaystyle \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right)}_{\text{strain rate } \dot{\epsilon}_{ij}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} - \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right)}_{\text{rotation } \omega_{ij}}$$

**Strain rate tensor** (rate of deformation):
$$\displaystyle \dot{\epsilon}_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right)$$

This is symmetric: $\dot{\epsilon}_{ij} = \dot{\epsilon}_{ji}$

**Physical meaning**: 
- Diagonal components: extensional strain rates
- Off-diagonal components: shear strain rates

**Stress tensor** for Newtonian fluid:
$$\displaystyle \sigma_{ij} = -P\delta_{ij} + \tau_{ij}$$

where the viscous stress is:
$$\displaystyle \tau_{ij} = 2\mu\dot{\epsilon}_{ij} - \frac{2}{3}\mu(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u})\delta_{ij}$$

For incompressible flow ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$):
$$\displaystyle \tau_{ij} = 2\mu\dot{\epsilon}_{ij} = \mu\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right)$$

### Example 6: Strain Rate for Simple Shear

For simple shear flow $u = \dot{\gamma} y$, $v = 0$, $w = 0$, calculate all components of $\dot{\epsilon}_{ij}$.

**Solution:**

Velocity gradients:
$$\displaystyle \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 0, \quad \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = \dot{\gamma}, \quad \frac{\partial u}{\partial z} = 0$$
$$\displaystyle \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = 0, \quad \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = 0, \quad \frac{\partial v}{\partial z} = 0$$
$$\displaystyle \frac{\partial w}{\partial x} = 0, \quad \frac{\partial w}{\partial y} = 0, \quad \frac{\partial w}{\partial z} = 0$$

Strain rate components:
$$\displaystyle \dot{\epsilon}_{11} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 0$$
$$\displaystyle \dot{\epsilon}_{22} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = 0$$
$$\displaystyle \dot{\epsilon}_{33} = \frac{\partial w}{\partial z} = 0$$
$$\displaystyle \dot{\epsilon}_{12} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\right) = \frac{\dot{\gamma}}{2}$$
$$\displaystyle \dot{\epsilon}_{13} = \dot{\epsilon}_{23} = 0$$

Only shear component $\dot{\epsilon}_{12}$ is nonzero.

**Answer:** $\dot{\epsilon}_{12} = \dot{\epsilon}_{21} = \dot{\gamma}/2$; all other components zero

### Example 7: Viscous Stress in Simple Shear

Using the strain rate from Example 6, calculate the viscous stress tensor $\tau_{ij}$ for $\mu = 0.05$ Pa·s and $\dot{\gamma} = 100$ s⁻¹.

**Solution:**

For incompressible flow:
$$\displaystyle \tau_{ij} = 2\mu\dot{\epsilon}_{ij}$$

$$\displaystyle \tau_{12} = 2\mu \cdot \frac{\dot{\gamma}}{2} = \mu\dot{\gamma} = (0.05)(100) = 5.0 \text{ Pa}$$

All other components zero (since corresponding $\dot{\epsilon}_{ij} = 0$).

This recovers Newton's law: $\tau = \mu \dot{\gamma}$ where $\dot{\gamma} = du/dy$ is the shear rate.

**Answer:** $\tau_{12} = \tau_{21} = 5.0$ Pa; all other components zero

## Non-Newtonian Fluids: Classification

Many fluids exhibit nonlinear stress-strain rate relationships.

**Shear-thinning (pseudoplastic)**: Apparent viscosity decreases with shear rate
- Examples: Blood, polymer solutions, paint, ketchup
- Mechanism: Microstructure (polymers, cells) aligns with flow, reducing resistance

**Shear-thickening (dilatant)**: Apparent viscosity increases with shear rate
- Examples: Cornstarch suspension, some colloids
- Mechanism: Flow disrupts particle packing, increasing resistance

**Bingham plastic**: Requires yield stress $\tau_y$ to initiate flow
- Examples: Toothpaste, mayonnaise, drilling mud
- Below $\tau_y$: behaves like solid
- Above $\tau_y$: flows like viscous fluid

**Time-dependent**: Viscosity depends on deformation history
- Thixotropic: viscosity decreases with time under constant shear (yogurt)
- Rheopectic: viscosity increases with time under constant shear (gypsum paste)

**Viscoelastic**: Exhibits both viscous and elastic behavior
- Examples: Silly Putty, polymer melts
- Stress depends on both strain and strain rate

### Example 8: Identifying Fluid Type from Data

Measure shear stress at different shear rates:

| $\dot{\gamma}$ (s⁻¹) | 1 | 10 | 100 | 1000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\tau$ (Pa) | 2 | 8 | 32 | 128 |

Determine fluid type.

**Solution:**

Calculate apparent viscosity $\mu_{app} = \tau/\dot{\gamma}$:

| $\dot{\gamma}$ | 1 | 10 | 100 | 1000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\mu_{app}$ (Pa·s) | 2 | 0.8 | 0.32 | 0.128 |

Viscosity decreases as shear rate increases → **shear-thinning** (pseudoplastic)

Check power-law fit: $\tau = K\dot{\gamma}^n$
Taking logs: $\ln\tau = \ln K + n\ln\dot{\gamma}$

From first and last points:
$$\displaystyle n = \frac{\ln(128/2)}{\ln(1000/1)} = \frac{\ln 64}{\ln 1000} = \frac{4.159}{6.908} = 0.60$$

Power-law index $n < 1$ confirms shear-thinning.

**Answer:** Shear-thinning fluid with power-law index $n \approx 0.6$

## Power-Law Model

For many non-Newtonian fluids:
$$\displaystyle \tau = K\dot{\gamma}^n$$

where:
- $K$ is consistency index (Pa·s^n)
- $n$ is power-law index (dimensionless)

**Classification**:
- $n = 1$: Newtonian ($K = \mu$)
- $n < 1$: Shear-thinning
- $n > 1$: Shear-thickening

Apparent viscosity:
$$\displaystyle \mu_{app} = \frac{\tau}{\dot{\gamma}} = K\dot{\gamma}^{n-1}$$

For shear-thinning ($n < 1$): $\mu_{app}$ decreases as $\dot{\gamma}$ increases
For shear-thickening ($n > 1$): $\mu_{app}$ increases as $\dot{\gamma}$ increases

### Example 9: Power-Law Viscosity

A power-law fluid has $K = 3.0$ Pa·s^n and $n = 0.7$. Calculate apparent viscosity at $\dot{\gamma} = 10$ s⁻¹ and $\dot{\gamma} = 100$ s⁻¹.

**Solution:**

At $\dot{\gamma} = 10$ s⁻¹:
$$\displaystyle \mu_{app} = K\dot{\gamma}^{n-1} = 3.0 \times 10^{0.7-1} = 3.0 \times 10^{-0.3}$$
$$= 3.0 \times 0.501 = 1.50 \text{ Pa·s}$$

At $\dot{\gamma} = 100$ s⁻¹:
$$\displaystyle \mu_{app} = 3.0 \times 100^{-0.3} = 3.0 \times 0.251 = 0.75 \text{ Pa·s}$$

Apparent viscosity halves when shear rate increases 10-fold—characteristic of shear-thinning.

**Answer:** $\mu_{app}(10) = 1.5$ Pa·s; $\mu_{app}(100) = 0.75$ Pa·s

### Example 10: Shear Stress Calculation

For the fluid in Example 9, calculate shear stress at $\dot{\gamma} = 50$ s⁻¹.

**Solution:**

$$\displaystyle \tau = K\dot{\gamma}^n = 3.0 \times 50^{0.7}$$

$$\displaystyle 50^{0.7} = e^{0.7 \ln 50} = e^{0.7 \times 3.912} = e^{2.738} = 15.5$$

$$\tau = 3.0 \times 15.5 = 46.5 \text{ Pa}$$

**Answer:** $\tau = 46.5$ Pa

## Bingham Plastic Model

Bingham plastics require yield stress $\tau_y$ before flowing:

$$\displaystyle \tau = \tau_y + \mu_p \dot{\gamma} \quad \text{for } \tau > \tau_y$$

$$\displaystyle \dot{\gamma} = 0 \quad \text{for } \tau \leq \tau_y$$

where $\mu_p$ is plastic viscosity. Below yield stress, material behaves like rigid solid.

### Example 11: Bingham Plastic Flow

Toothpaste has $\tau_y = 100$ Pa and $\mu_p = 50$ Pa·s. Calculate:
(a) Minimum stress to make it flow
(b) Shear rate at $\tau = 200$ Pa

**Solution:**

(a) Minimum stress = yield stress = $100$ Pa

(b) For $\tau = 200$ Pa $> \tau_y$:
$$\displaystyle \dot{\gamma} = \frac{\tau - \tau_y}{\mu_p} = \frac{200 - 100}{50} = 2.0 \text{ s}^{-1}$$

**Answer:** (a) $\tau_y = 100$ Pa; (b) $\dot{\gamma} = 2.0$ s⁻¹

## Viscoelasticity

Viscoelastic fluids exhibit both:
- **Viscous behavior**: Stress depends on strain rate (dissipates energy)
- **Elastic behavior**: Stress depends on strain (stores energy)

**Maxwell model** (simple viscoelastic model):
$$\displaystyle \tau + \lambda \frac{d\tau}{dt} = \mu \frac{d\gamma}{dt}$$

where $\lambda$ is relaxation time.

**Physical interpretation**:
- Short times ($t \ll \lambda$): Elastic response dominates
- Long times ($t \gg \lambda$): Viscous response dominates

**Deborah number**:
$$\displaystyle \text{De} = \frac{\lambda}{t_{obs}}$$

- De $\gg 1$: Behaves elastically
- De $\ll 1$: Behaves viscously

### Example 12: Relaxation Time Interpretation

A polymer solution has relaxation time $\lambda = 0.1$ s. Characterize its behavior for:
(a) Rapid deformation ($t_{obs} = 0.01$ s)
(b) Slow deformation ($t_{obs} = 10$ s)

**Solution:**

(a) $\text{De} = 0.1/0.01 = 10 \gg 1$ → Behaves elastically (like rubber)

(b) $\text{De} = 0.1/10 = 0.01 \ll 1$ → Behaves viscously (like liquid)

Same material behaves differently depending on deformation timescale!

**Answer:** (a) Elastic; (b) Viscous

### Example 13: Steady Shear in Maxwell Fluid

For steady shear ($d\tau/dt = 0$ and $d\gamma/dt = \dot{\gamma} = \text{constant}$), show that Maxwell model reduces to Newtonian behavior.

**Solution:**

Maxwell equation: $\tau + \lambda \frac{d\tau}{dt} = \mu\dot{\gamma}$

At steady state: $\frac{d\tau}{dt} = 0$

$$\tau = \mu\dot{\gamma}$$

Recovers Newton's law of viscosity. Elastic effects vanish in steady flow—only viscous dissipation remains.

**Answer:** Steady Maxwell fluid is Newtonian with viscosity $\mu$

## Energy Dissipation

Viscous flow dissipates mechanical energy as heat. **Dissipation function**:
$$\displaystyle \Phi = \tau_{ij}\dot{\epsilon}_{ij}$$

For Newtonian fluid:
$$\displaystyle \Phi = 2\mu\dot{\epsilon}_{ij}\dot{\epsilon}_{ij}$$

This is always positive (second law of thermodynamics)—viscosity irreversibly converts kinetic energy to internal energy.

### Example 14: Dissipation in Simple Shear

For simple shear with $\dot{\epsilon}_{12} = \dot{\gamma}/2$ (Example 6), calculate dissipation rate per unit volume if $\mu = 0.1$ Pa·s and $\dot{\gamma} = 50$ s⁻¹.

**Solution:**

$$\displaystyle \Phi = 2\mu\dot{\epsilon}_{ij}\dot{\epsilon}_{ij}$$

For simple shear, only $\dot{\epsilon}_{12} = \dot{\epsilon}_{21} = \dot{\gamma}/2$ are nonzero:
$$\displaystyle \Phi = 2\mu[2(\dot{\gamma}/2)^2] = 2\mu \cdot 2 \cdot \frac{\dot{\gamma}^2}{4} = \mu\dot{\gamma}^2$$

$$\Phi = (0.1)(50)^2 = 250 \text{ W/m}^3$$

This energy dissipates as heat, warming the fluid.

**Answer:** $\Phi = 250$ W/m³

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: D**
$\tau = \mu\dot{\gamma}$
$\tau = (0.8)(20) = 16$ Pa
Newton's law of viscosity for simple shear
Units check: Pa·s × s⁻¹ = Pa

**Problem 2: C**
In gases, $\mu \propto \sqrt{T}$ (kinetic theory)
Higher temperature → faster molecular motion → more momentum transfer
Gas viscosity increases with T (unlike liquids)
Molecular collision frequency actually increases (not decreases)

**Problem 3: A**
$\mu_{app} = \tau/\dot{\gamma} = 2\dot{\gamma}^{0.5}/\dot{\gamma} = 2\dot{\gamma}^{-0.5}$
$\mu_{app} = 2/(100)^{0.5} = 2/10 = 0.2$ Pa·s
Shear-thinning fluid (n = 0.5 < 1)
Apparent viscosity decreases with shear rate

**Problem 4: C**
For Newtonian fluid: $\tau_{ij} = 2\mu\dot{\epsilon}_{ij}$
Strain rate: $\dot{\epsilon}_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right)$
Therefore: $\tau_{ij} = \mu\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right)$
Symmetric tensor (viscous stress depends on symmetric part of velocity gradient)

**Problem 5: B**
Bingham plastic requires $\tau > \tau_y$ to flow
Here $\tau = 40$ Pa $< \tau_y = 50$ Pa
Below yield stress → behaves as rigid solid
$\dot{\gamma} = 0$ (no flow)
Material deforms elastically but does not flow
