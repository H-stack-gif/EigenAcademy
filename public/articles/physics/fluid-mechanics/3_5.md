# Article 3.5: Lifting Surfaces and Added Mass

Lift generation—the force perpendicular to flow direction—is central to aeronautics, wind turbines, and marine propulsion. **Thin airfoil theory** provides analytical predictions: for small angles of attack, lift coefficient varies linearly with $\alpha$, giving $C_L \approx 2\pi\alpha$ (radians) for flat plates and symmetric airfoils. **Kutta condition**—requiring smooth flow departure from the trailing edge—uniquely determines circulation and thus lift. For finite-span wings, three-dimensional effects become crucial: **lifting line theory** (Prandtl) accounts for induced downwash from trailing vortices, which creates **induced drag** even at zero angle of attack. The **Kutta-Joukowski theorem** $L' = \rho U\Gamma$ remains valid locally along the span, but circulation varies with position, strongest at wing center and vanishing at tips.

**Added mass** represents the inertial reaction of fluid as bodies accelerate through it. When a submerged sphere accelerates, surrounding fluid must accelerate too, effectively adding to the body's inertia. The **added mass tensor** $M_{ij}^{\text{added}}$ gives the force $F_i = -M_{ij}^{\text{added}}\frac{dU_j}{dt}$ opposing acceleration. For sphere, $M^{\text{added}} = \frac{1}{2}\rho V$ (half the displaced fluid mass). Added mass effects are crucial for ship maneuvering, submarine dynamics, and vibrating structures in fluid. **Unsteady airfoils** (flapping wings, helicopter rotors) require accounting for both circulatory (bound vortex) and non-circulatory (added mass) forces, described by **Theodorsen theory**. The Wagner function governs lift development as circulation adjusts to sudden angle changes.

This article develops thin airfoil theory and lift prediction, explains the Kutta condition and circulation determination, introduces lifting line theory for finite wings and induced drag, derives added mass concepts and calculations for simple bodies, treats unsteady airfoils and Theodorsen's theory, explores multi-body hydrodynamic interactions, discusses applications to aircraft, propellers, and marine vehicles, and works through extensive examples involving lift calculations, circulation distributions, added mass for various shapes, unsteady force predictions, and wing performance analysis. The mathematics involves integral equations (for vortex distributions), Fourier series, linear algebra (added mass tensors), and differential equations for unsteady motion.

## Thin Airfoil Theory (Steady)

**Assumptions**:
- Thin airfoil ($t/c \ll 1$, where $t$ = thickness, $c$ = chord)
- Small angle of attack ($\alpha \ll 1$)
- Incompressible, inviscid flow

**Model**: Replace airfoil with **vortex sheet** on camber line.

**Vorticity distribution** $\gamma(x)$ (circulation per unit length) along chord $0 < x < c$.

**Boundary condition**: Flow tangent to camber line.

**Kutta condition**: $\gamma(c) = 0$ (circulation smooth at trailing edge).

**Result**: Total circulation

$$\Gamma = \int_0^c \gamma(x) \, dx$$

**Lift** (Kutta-Joukowski):

$$L' = \rho U\Gamma$$

**Lift coefficient**:

$$\boxed{C_L = \frac{L'}{\frac{1}{2}\rho U^2 c} = 2\pi\alpha}$$

(for flat plate or symmetric airfoil at small $\alpha$)

**Cambered airfoil**: $C_L = 2\pi(\alpha - \alpha_{L=0})$ where $\alpha_{L=0}$ is zero-lift angle.

## Kutta Condition

**Physical reasoning**: Real fluid (with viscosity) cannot flow around sharp trailing edge with infinite velocity.

**Inviscid model**: Circulation adjusts so that **rear stagnation point** moves to trailing edge.

**Mathematical implementation**: $\gamma(c) = 0$ (finite velocity at trailing edge).

**Consequence**: Uniquely determines $\Gamma$ for given $\alpha$.

**Historical**: Without Kutta condition, potential flow predicts zero lift (d'Alembert's paradox).

## Lifting Line Theory (Finite Wing)

**Problem**: Finite-span wing (tips at $y = \pm b/2$) has 3D flow—vorticity extends downstream as **trailing vortices**.

**Prandtl's model**:
- **Bound vortex** along wing span with circulation $\Gamma(y)$
- **Trailing vortices** (from span-wise variation of $\Gamma$) induce **downwash** $w(y)$

**Downwash**:

$$w(y) = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{-b/2}^{b/2} \frac{d\Gamma/dy'}{y - y'} dy'$$

**Effective angle of attack** reduced:

$$\alpha_{\text{eff}}(y) = \alpha - \alpha_i(y)$$

where $\alpha_i = w/U$ is **induced angle**.

**Local lift**:

$$L'(y) = \rho U\Gamma(y)$$

**Induced drag**:

$$D_i = \rho U\int_{-b/2}^{b/2} \Gamma(y)\alpha_i(y) \, dy$$

**Elliptical circulation distribution** minimizes induced drag:

$$\Gamma(y) = \Gamma_0\sqrt{1 - (2y/b)^2}$$

**Induced drag coefficient**:

$$\boxed{C_{D,i} = \frac{C_L^2}{\pi AR}}$$

where $AR = b^2/S$ is **aspect ratio** (span²/area).

## Added Mass Concept

**Definition**: When body accelerates, must accelerate surrounding fluid too → effective inertia increase.

**Force** on body:

$$F_i = -M_{ij}^{\text{added}}\frac{dU_j}{dt}$$

(negative sign: opposes acceleration)

**Added mass tensor** $M_{ij}^{\text{added}}$ depends on body shape and orientation.

**Units**: Mass (kg)

**Physical interpretation**: Fraction of displaced fluid mass "carried along" by body.

## Added Mass for Simple Shapes

**Sphere** (radius $a$):

$$M^{\text{added}} = \frac{1}{2}\rho \frac{4\pi a^3}{3} = \frac{2\pi\rho a^3}{3}$$

(Half the displaced mass)

**Circular cylinder** (2D, radius $a$, per unit length):

$$M^{\text{added}} = \rho\pi a^2$$

(Equal to displaced mass)

**Flat plate** (perpendicular motion, width $c$, per unit span):

$$M^{\text{added}} = \frac{\pi\rho c^2}{4}$$

**Ellipsoid**: More complex (depends on axes)

## Unsteady Airfoils: Theodorsen Theory

**Problem**: Airfoil suddenly changes angle of attack—how does lift develop?

**Two contributions**:
1. **Non-circulatory** (added mass): Instantaneous force from fluid acceleration
2. **Circulatory** (bound vortex): Develops over time as vorticity adjusts

**Wagner function** $\Phi(s)$ describes lift development:

$$C_L(s) = 2\pi\alpha\Phi(s)$$

where $s = Ut/c$ is non-dimensional time.

**Wagner function**:
- $\Phi(0) = 0.5$ (immediate response from added mass)
- $\Phi(s) \to 1$ as $s \to \infty$ (steady state)

**Theodorsen function** $C(k)$ for oscillating airfoils (frequency $\omega$):

$$C(k) = F(k) + iG(k)$$

where $k = \omega c/(2U)$ is **reduced frequency**.

## Example 1: Flat Plate Lift

Flat plate chord $c = 1$ m, angle of attack $\alpha = 5° = 0.0873$ rad, $U = 50$ m/s (air $\rho = 1.2$ kg/m³).

Find lift per unit span.

**Solution:**

$$C_L = 2\pi\alpha = 2\pi(0.0873) = 0.548$$

$$L' = \frac{1}{2}\rho U^2 c \cdot C_L$$

$$= \frac{1}{2}(1.2)(50)^2(1)(0.548)$$

$$= 0.6(2500)(0.548) = 822 \text{ N/m}$$

**Answer:** $L' = 822$ N/m

## Example 2: Wing Induced Drag

Wing: span $b = 10$ m, area $S = 20$ m², $C_L = 0.5$.

Find induced drag coefficient.

**Solution:**

Aspect ratio:
$$AR = \frac{b^2}{S} = \frac{100}{20} = 5$$

Induced drag coefficient:
$$C_{D,i} = \frac{C_L^2}{\pi AR} = \frac{(0.5)^2}{\pi(5)} = \frac{0.25}{15.7} = 0.0159$$

**At $U = 50$ m/s**:

$$D_i = \frac{1}{2}\rho U^2 S \cdot C_{D,i}$$

$$= \frac{1}{2}(1.2)(2500)(20)(0.0159) = 477 \text{ N}$$

**Answer:** $C_{D,i} = 0.0159$, $D_i = 477$ N

---

## Example 3: Added Mass of Sphere

Sphere radius $a = 0.1$ m accelerates at $\dot{U} = 2$ m/s² in water ($\rho = 1000$ kg/m³).

Find added mass force.

**Solution:**

Added mass:
$$M^{\text{added}} = \frac{2\pi\rho a^3}{3} = \frac{2\pi(1000)(0.1)^3}{3} = \frac{2\pi(1)}{3} = 2.09 \text{ kg}$$

Force:
$$F = M^{\text{added}}\dot{U} = (2.09)(2) = 4.19 \text{ N}$$

**Opposes acceleration**.

**Answer:** $M^{\text{added}} = 2.09$ kg, $F = 4.19$ N

---

## Example 4: Cambered Airfoil

NACA 2412 airfoil: maximum camber 2% at 40% chord.

Zero-lift angle $\alpha_{L=0} = -2°$.

Find $C_L$ at $\alpha = 5°$.

**Solution:**

$$C_L = 2\pi(\alpha - \alpha_{L=0})$$

$$= 2\pi(5° - (-2°)) = 2\pi(7°)$$

$$= 2\pi(0.122) = 0.767$$

**Higher lift** than flat plate due to camber.

**Answer:** $C_L = 0.77$

---

## Example 5: Circulation Distribution (Elliptical)

Wing span $b = 10$ m with elliptical circulation distribution $\Gamma(y) = \Gamma_0\sqrt{1 - (2y/b)^2}$.

If $\Gamma_0 = 20$ m²/s, find total lift at $U = 50$ m/s (air).

**Solution:**

Total circulation:
$$\Gamma_{total} = \int_{-b/2}^{b/2} \Gamma(y) \, dy$$

Substitute:
$$= \Gamma_0\int_{-b/2}^{b/2} \sqrt{1 - (2y/b)^2} \, dy$$


For elliptical distribution: Average $\Gamma = \frac{2}{\pi}\Gamma_0$

Total: $L = \rho U b \cdot \frac{2\Gamma_0}{\pi} = (1.2)(50)(10)\frac{2(20)}{\pi} = 600 \cdot 12.73 = 7,638$ N

**Answer:** $L \approx 7,640$ N

---

## Example 6: Wagner Function Initial Response

Airfoil suddenly pitched to $\alpha = 5°$. Estimate immediate lift coefficient.

**Solution:**

From Wagner function: $\Phi(0) = 0.5$

$$C_L(0) = 2\pi\alpha\Phi(0) = 2\pi(0.0873)(0.5) = 0.274$$

**Half of steady-state** lift ($C_L(\infty) = 0.548$).

Non-circulatory (added mass) contribution dominates initially.

**Answer:** $C_L(0) = 0.27$

## Example 7: Aspect Ratio Effect

Compare induced drag for two wings with same $S = 20$ m², $C_L = 0.5$:
(a) $b = 10$ m (AR = 5)
(b) $b = 14.1$ m (AR = 10)

**Solution:**

(a) $C_{D,i} = \frac{0.25}{\pi(5)} = 0.0159$

(b) $C_{D,i} = \frac{0.25}{\pi(10)} = 0.00796$

**Ratio**: (a)/(b) = 2 → **Doubling AR halves induced drag**!

**Answer:** Higher AR reduces induced drag significantly

## Example 8: Oscillating Airfoil

Airfoil oscillates: $\alpha(t) = \alpha_0\sin(\omega t)$.

Reduced frequency $k = 0.1$ (low).

Compare Theodorsen function to quasi-steady.

**Solution:**

For $k = 0.1$: $C(k) \approx 0.99 - 0.01i$ (nearly real)

**Quasi-steady** ($k \to 0$): $C(k) = 1$

**Lift** nearly in phase with angle, minimal lag.

For $k \gg 1$ (high frequency): $|C(k)|$ drops significantly → reduced lift amplitude.

**Answer:** Low $k$ → quasi-steady valid

## Example 9: Added Mass Cylinder

Circular cylinder (radius $a = 0.5$ m) accelerates horizontally in water.

Added mass per unit length?

**Solution:**

$$M^{\text{added}} = \rho\pi a^2 = (1000)\pi(0.5)^2 = 785 \text{ kg/m}$$

**Equal to displaced fluid mass** (per unit length).

**Answer:** $M^{\text{added}} = 785$ kg/m

## Example 10: Downwash Angle

Wing with elliptical circulation: $C_L = 0.6$, $AR = 8$.

Estimate average downwash angle.

**Solution:**

Induced drag coefficient:
$$C_{D,i} = \frac{C_L^2}{\pi AR} = \frac{0.36}{\pi(8)} = 0.0143$$

Average downwash angle:
$$\alpha_i = \frac{C_L}{\pi AR} = \frac{0.6}{\pi(8)} = 0.0239 \text{ rad} = 1.37°$$

**Small but significant** reduction in effective angle.

**Answer:** $\alpha_i \approx 1.4°$

---

## Example 11: Propeller Thrust (Momentum Theory)

Propeller disk area $A = 2$ m², increases air velocity from $U_\infty = 0$ to $U_e = 20$ m/s in slipstream.

Estimate thrust.

**Solution:**

**Momentum theory**: Velocity through disk $U_d = (U_\infty + U_e)/2 = 10$ m/s

Mass flow: $\dot{m} = \rho A U_d = (1.2)(2)(10) = 24$ kg/s

Thrust:
$$T = \dot{m}(U_e - U_\infty) = 24(20) = 480 \text{ N}$$

**Answer:** $T = 480$ N

---

## Example 12: Flapping Wing

Hummingbird wing: span $b = 0.08$ m, flaps at $f = 50$ Hz, stroke amplitude $\theta_0 = 30° = 0.52$ rad.

Estimate mean velocity and lift (very simplified).

**Solution:**

**Mean tip velocity**:
$$U_{tip} = 2f b\theta_0 = 2(50)(0.08)(0.52) = 4.16 \text{ m/s}$$

**Circulation** (order of magnitude): $\Gamma \sim U_{tip} \cdot c$ (chord, say $c = 0.01$ m)

$$\Gamma \sim (4.16)(0.01) = 0.042 \text{ m}^2\text{/s}$$

**Lift** (crude): $L \sim \rho U_{tip}\Gamma b \sim (1.2)(4.16)(0.042)(0.08) \sim 0.017$ N

**Weight of hummingbird**: ~0.03 N → **reasonable order of magnitude**!

(Real analysis much more complex: unsteady effects, vortex formation, etc.)

**Answer:** $L \sim 0.02$ N (order of magnitude)
