# Article 2.2: Rate-of-Strain and Vorticity

Fluid motion involves both deformation and rotation. A small fluid element can stretch, compress, shear, and rotate—all simultaneously. Separating these effects mathematically requires decomposing the **velocity gradient tensor** $\partial u_i/\partial x_j$ into symmetric and antisymmetric parts. The symmetric part, the **rate-of-strain tensor** $\dot{\epsilon}_{ij}$, describes pure deformation (stretching and shearing). The antisymmetric part corresponds to **rotation**, characterized by the **vorticity vector** $\boldsymbol{\omega} = \nabla \times \mathbf{u}$. This decomposition is fundamental—strain causes viscous stress, while vorticity indicates rotational motion.

Vorticity measures the local spinning of fluid elements. A fluid element with nonzero vorticity rotates about its center of mass; one with zero vorticity undergoes pure deformation without rotation (**irrotational flow**). The curl of velocity, $\boldsymbol{\omega} = \nabla \times \mathbf{u}$, quantifies this rotation—its magnitude equals twice the angular velocity of the fluid element. **Circulation** $\Gamma = \oint_C \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$, the line integral of velocity around a closed curve, provides an integral measure of vorticity via **Stokes' theorem**: $\Gamma = \int_S \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot d\mathbf{A}$.

This article develops the velocity gradient tensor decomposition, derives the physical interpretation of strain rate components, introduces vorticity and its calculation, explores circulation and Kelvin's theorem (preview), connects vorticity to rotation rate, treats irrotational flow as a special case, and demonstrates applications through extensive examples involving shear flows, vortices, and complex velocity fields. The mathematics involves heavy tensor calculus, vector operations (curl, line integrals), and geometric interpretation of tensor components.

## Velocity Gradient Tensor

The **velocity gradient tensor** is:

$$\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}$$

In matrix form:
$$\nabla \mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix}
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial u}{\partial z} \\
\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial v}{\partial z} \\
\frac{\partial w}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial w}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial w}{\partial z}
\end{pmatrix}$$

This tensor contains all information about local deformation and rotation.

## Decomposition into Strain and Rotation

Any tensor can be written as sum of symmetric and antisymmetric parts:

$$\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right)}_{\text{symmetric: } \dot{\epsilon}_{ij}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} - \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right)}_{\text{antisymmetric: } \omega_{ij}}$$

**Rate-of-strain tensor** (symmetric):
$$\boxed{\dot{\epsilon}_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right)}$$

**Rotation tensor** (antisymmetric):
$$\boxed{\omega_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} - \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right)}$$

**Physical interpretation**:
- $\dot{\epsilon}_{ij}$: describes **deformation** (stretching, compression, shearing)
- $\omega_{ij}$: describes **rigid-body rotation**

## Rate-of-Strain Tensor Components

In 3D:
$$\dot{\epsilon}_{ij} = \begin{pmatrix}
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} & \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\right) & \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} + \frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\right) \\
\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right) & \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} & \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial z} + \frac{\partial w}{\partial y}\right) \\
\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial u}{\partial z}\right) & \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial z}\right) & \frac{\partial w}{\partial z}
\end{pmatrix}$$

**Diagonal components**: Normal strain rates (elongation/compression)
- $\dot{\epsilon}_{11} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}$: rate of stretching in $x$-direction
- $\dot{\epsilon}_{22} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y}$: rate of stretching in $y$-direction
- $\dot{\epsilon}_{33} = \frac{\partial w}{\partial z}$: rate of stretching in $z$-direction

**Off-diagonal components**: Shear strain rates
- $\dot{\epsilon}_{12} = \dot{\epsilon}_{21} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\right)$: shearing in $x$-$y$ plane

**Trace** (sum of diagonal elements):
$$\text{tr}(\dot{\epsilon}_{ij}) = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial w}{\partial z} = \nabla \cdot \mathbf{u}$$

This is the **volumetric strain rate** (rate of volume change per unit volume).

**Incompressible flow**: $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0 \implies \text{tr}(\dot{\epsilon}_{ij}) = 0$

## Vorticity Vector

The **vorticity** is the curl of velocity:

$$\boxed{\boldsymbol{\omega} = \nabla \times \mathbf{u}}$$

In Cartesian coordinates:
$$\boldsymbol{\omega} = \begin{vmatrix}
\mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\
\frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\
u & v & w
\end{vmatrix}$$

Components:
$$\omega_x = \frac{\partial w}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial v}{\partial z}$$

$$\omega_y = \frac{\partial u}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial w}{\partial x}$$

$$\omega_z = \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}$$

**Relationship to rotation tensor**: The antisymmetric tensor $\omega_{ij}$ is dual to vorticity vector:
$$\omega_x = 2\omega_{23}, \quad \omega_y = 2\omega_{31}, \quad \omega_z = 2\omega_{12}$$

**Physical meaning**: $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ measures twice the local angular velocity of fluid element.

If fluid element rotates with angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$, then $\boldsymbol{\omega} = 2\boldsymbol{\Omega}$.

## Irrotational Flow

**Definition**: Flow with zero vorticity everywhere.

$$\nabla \times \mathbf{u} = 0$$

**Consequence**: Velocity can be expressed as gradient of a scalar potential $\phi$:

$$\mathbf{u} = \nabla \phi$$

This is **potential flow** (covered in Unit 3).

**Examples**:
- Uniform flow: $\mathbf{u} = (U, 0, 0)$ → $\boldsymbol{\omega} = 0$
- Stagnation point flow: $\mathbf{u} = (kx, -ky, 0)$ → $\boldsymbol{\omega} = 0$

**Counterexample**:
- Solid body rotation: $\mathbf{u} = (-\Omega y, \Omega x, 0)$ → $\boldsymbol{\omega} = (0, 0, 2\Omega) \neq 0$

## Circulation

**Definition**: Line integral of velocity around closed curve $C$:

$$\boxed{\Gamma = \oint_C \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l}}$$

**Stokes' theorem** relates circulation to vorticity:

$$\Gamma = \oint_C \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{u}) \cdot d\mathbf{A} = \int_S \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot d\mathbf{A}$$

where $S$ is any surface bounded by $C$.

**Physical interpretation**: Circulation measures net rotation around curve.

**Properties**:
- If $\boldsymbol{\omega} = 0$ everywhere in region → $\Gamma = 0$ for any curve in region (irrotational)
- If $\boldsymbol{\omega} \neq 0$, circulation depends on curve

**Kelvin's circulation theorem** (preview): For inviscid, barotropic flow, $\frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = 0$—circulation conserved following fluid.

## Example 1: Strain Rate Tensor for Simple Shear

For $\mathbf{u} = (ky, 0, 0)$ (shear flow), calculate $\dot{\epsilon}_{ij}$.

**Solution:**

Velocity gradients:
- $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 0$, $\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = k$, $\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} = 0$
- $\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = 0$, $\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = 0$, $\frac{\partial v}{\partial z} = 0$
- All $w$ derivatives zero

$$\dot{\epsilon}_{11} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 0$$

$$\dot{\epsilon}_{22} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = 0$$

$$\dot{\epsilon}_{33} = \frac{\partial w}{\partial z} = 0$$

$$\dot{\epsilon}_{12} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\right) = \frac{k}{2}$$

$$\dot{\epsilon}_{13} = \dot{\epsilon}_{23} = 0$$

$$\dot{\epsilon}_{ij} = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & k/2 & 0 \\
k/2 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Pure shear deformation in $x$-$y$ plane, no normal strains.

**Answer:** $\dot{\epsilon}_{ij}$ as above; pure shear with rate $k/2$

## Example 2: Vorticity in Simple Shear

For same flow $\mathbf{u} = (ky, 0, 0)$, calculate vorticity.

**Solution:**

$$\omega_z = \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = 0 - k = -k$$

$$\omega_x = \omega_y = 0$$

$$\boldsymbol{\omega} = (0, 0, -k)$$

**Magnitude**: $|\boldsymbol{\omega}| = k$

**Angular velocity**: $\Omega = |\boldsymbol{\omega}|/2 = k/2$

**Physical interpretation**: Fluid elements rotate with angular velocity $k/2$ (clockwise if $k > 0$) while being sheared.

**Answer:** $\boldsymbol{\omega} = -k\hat{\mathbf{z}}$

---

## Example 3: Vorticity of Solid Body Rotation

For $\mathbf{u} = \boldsymbol{\Omega} \times \mathbf{r} = (-\Omega y, \Omega x, 0)$, calculate vorticity.

**Solution:**

$$\omega_z = \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = \Omega - (-\Omega) = 2\Omega$$

$$\omega_x = \omega_y = 0$$

$$\boldsymbol{\omega} = (0, 0, 2\Omega)$$

**Physical meaning**: Vorticity equals twice the angular velocity—confirms general relationship.

**Answer:** $\boldsymbol{\omega} = 2\Omega\hat{\mathbf{z}}$

## Example 4: Circulation Around Circle

For solid body rotation $\mathbf{u} = (-\Omega y, \Omega x, 0)$, calculate circulation around circle $x^2 + y^2 = R^2$.

**Solution:**

**Method 1: Direct integration**

Parameterize circle: $x = R\cos\theta$, $y = R\sin\theta$, $d\mathbf{l} = R(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0)d\theta$

$$\mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = (-\Omega R\sin\theta, \Omega R\cos\theta, 0) \cdot R(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0)d\theta$$

$$= R^2\Omega(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta)d\theta = R^2\Omega \, d\theta$$

$$\Gamma = \int_0^{2\pi} R^2\Omega \, d\theta = 2\pi R^2\Omega$$

**Method 2: Stokes' theorem**

$$\Gamma = \int_S \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot d\mathbf{A} = \int_S 2\Omega \, dA = 2\Omega \cdot \pi R^2 = 2\pi R^2\Omega$$

Both methods agree ✓

**Answer:** $\Gamma = 2\pi R^2\Omega$

## Example 5: Irrotational vs. Rotational Flow

Compare:
(a) $\mathbf{u} = (x, -y, 0)$
(b) $\mathbf{u} = (-y, x, 0)$

**Solution:**

**(a)** $\mathbf{u} = (x, -y, 0)$:

$$\omega_z = \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = 0 - 0 = 0$$

Irrotational ✓

**(b)** $\mathbf{u} = (-y, x, 0)$:

$$\omega_z = \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = 1 - (-1) = 2$$

Rotational ✓ (this is solid body rotation with $\Omega = 1$)

**Answer:** (a) irrotational; (b) rotational with $\boldsymbol{\omega} = 2\hat{\mathbf{z}}$

## Example 6: Point Vortex

For $\mathbf{u} = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0)$ in cylindrical coordinates (or $u_\theta = \Gamma/(2\pi r)$), calculate vorticity.

**Solution:**

In cylindrical coordinates:
$$\omega_z = \frac{1}{r}\left[\frac{\partial(ru_\theta)}{\partial r} - \frac{\partial u_r}{\partial \theta}\right]$$

Here $u_r = 0$, $u_\theta = \Gamma/(2\pi r)$:

$$\omega_z = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r \cdot \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}\right) = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\right) = 0$$

**Paradox?** Flow circulates, but $\boldsymbol{\omega} = 0$ for $r > 0$!

**Resolution**: Vorticity concentrated at origin (singular point). For any curve enclosing origin:
$$\Gamma = \oint \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \Gamma$$ (constant)

This is an **irrotational vortex** everywhere except at the singular core.

**Answer:** $\boldsymbol{\omega} = 0$ for $r > 0$; singular at $r = 0$

## Example 7: Strain Rate in Extension

For $\mathbf{u} = (\epsilon x, -\epsilon y/2, -\epsilon z/2)$ (extensional flow), find strain rate tensor.

**Solution:**

Velocity gradients (diagonal only nonzero):
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \epsilon, \quad \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = -\epsilon/2, \quad \frac{\partial w}{\partial z} = -\epsilon/2$$

$$\dot{\epsilon}_{ij} = \begin{pmatrix}
\epsilon & 0 & 0 \\
0 & -\epsilon/2 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & -\epsilon/2
\end{pmatrix}$$

**Trace**: $\epsilon - \epsilon/2 - \epsilon/2 = 0$ → incompressible ✓

**Physical interpretation**: Stretching in $x$, compression in $y$ and $z$, volume-preserving.

**Answer:** Diagonal tensor with eigenvalues $\epsilon, -\epsilon/2, -\epsilon/2$

## Example 8: Circulation in Corner Flow

For $\mathbf{u} = (x, -y, 0)$, calculate circulation around rectangle with corners $(0,0)$, $(a,0)$, $(a,b)$, $(0,b)$.

**Solution:**

**Four sides**:

(1) Bottom $(0,0) \to (a,0)$: $y = 0$, $dx$ from $0$ to $a$
$$\int u \, dx = \int_0^a x \, dx = \frac{a^2}{2}$$

(2) Right $(a,0) \to (a,b)$: $x = a$, $dy$ from $0$ to $b$
$$\int v \, dy = \int_0^b (-y) \, dy = -\frac{b^2}{2}$$

(3) Top $(a,b) \to (0,b)$: $y = b$, $dx$ from $a$ to $0$
$$\int u \, dx = \int_a^0 x \, dx = -\frac{a^2}{2}$$

(4) Left $(0,b) \to (0,0)$: $x = 0$, $dy$ from $b$ to $0$
$$\int v \, dy = \int_b^0 (-y) \, dy = \frac{b^2}{2}$$

Total: $\Gamma = \frac{a^2}{2} - \frac{b^2}{2} - \frac{a^2}{2} + \frac{b^2}{2} = 0$

**Verification**: Flow is irrotational ($\omega_z = 0$), so $\Gamma = 0$ ✓

**Answer:** $\Gamma = 0$

## Example 9: Maximum Shear Rate

For $\mathbf{u} = (ky, kx, 0)$, find principal strain rates.

**Solution:**

Strain rate tensor:
$$\dot{\epsilon}_{ij} = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & k & 0 \\
k & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}$$

**Eigenvalues** (principal strain rates):

Det$(\dot{\epsilon}_{ij} - \lambda \delta_{ij}) = 0$:
$$-\lambda(k^2 - \lambda^2) = 0$$

Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = k$, $\lambda_2 = -k$, $\lambda_3 = 0$

**Physical interpretation**: Maximum extension rate $k$ along one diagonal, maximum compression rate $-k$ along other diagonal.

**Answer:** Principal strain rates: $k, -k, 0$

---

## Example 10: Vorticity in Cylindrical Shear

For $u_r = 0$, $u_\theta = f(r)$, $u_z = 0$, find vorticity.

**Solution:**

In cylindrical coordinates:
$$\omega_z = \frac{1}{r}\left[\frac{\partial(ru_\theta)}{\partial r} - \frac{\partial u_r}{\partial \theta}\right] = \frac{1}{r}\frac{d(rf)}{dr}$$

$$= \frac{1}{r}(f + r\frac{df}{dr}) = \frac{f}{r} + \frac{df}{dr}$$

**Special cases**:

(a) Solid body: $f = \Omega r$ → $\omega_z = \Omega + \Omega = 2\Omega$ ✓

(b) Potential vortex: $f = \Gamma/(2\pi r)$ → $\omega_z = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r^2} + \frac{-\Gamma}{2\pi r^2} = 0$ ✓

**Answer:** $\omega_z = \frac{f}{r} + \frac{df}{dr}$

## Example 11: Enstrophy

The **enstrophy** is $\mathcal{E} = \frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \boldsymbol{\omega} = \frac{1}{2}\omega^2$ (vorticity squared).

For $\mathbf{u} = (-\Omega y, \Omega x, 0)$, calculate enstrophy.

**Solution:**

Vorticity: $\boldsymbol{\omega} = 2\Omega\hat{\mathbf{z}}$

$$\mathcal{E} = \frac{1}{2}|\boldsymbol{\omega}|^2 = \frac{1}{2}(2\Omega)^2 = 2\Omega^2$$

**Physical meaning**: Enstrophy measures intensity of rotation, related to energy dissipation in viscous flows.

**Answer:** $\mathcal{E} = 2\Omega^2$

## Example 12: Vorticity Generation

In flow $\mathbf{u} = (U_0, 0, 0)$ past stationary cylinder, boundary layer develops with vorticity.

If wall shear stress $\tau_w = \mu\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)_{y=0} = 0.5$ Pa and $\mu = 0.001$ Pa·s, estimate vorticity at wall.

**Solution:**

At wall:
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\bigg|_{y=0} = \frac{\tau_w}{\mu} = \frac{0.5}{0.001} = 500 \text{ s}^{-1}$$

Assuming $v$ negligible near wall:
$$\omega_z \approx -\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -500 \text{ s}^{-1}$$

**Physical interpretation**: No-slip condition creates strong vorticity at solid surfaces.

**Answer:** $|\omega_z| \approx 500$ s⁻¹ at wall

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B** - Rate-of-strain tensor $\dot{\epsilon}_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}(\partial u_i/\partial x_j + \partial u_j/\partial x_i)$ is symmetric by construction.

**Problem 2: B** - Vorticity $\boldsymbol{\omega} = \nabla \times \mathbf{u}$ equals twice the local angular velocity of fluid elements.

**Problem 3: A** - Irrotational flow defined by zero vorticity: $\boldsymbol{\omega} = \nabla \times \mathbf{u} = 0$.

**Problem 4: B** - By Stokes' theorem, $\Gamma = \int_S \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot d\mathbf{A} = 0$ if $\boldsymbol{\omega} = 0$ everywhere in region.

**Problem 5: C** - $\text{tr}(\dot{\epsilon}_{ij}) = \partial u/\partial x + \partial v/\partial y + \partial w/\partial z = \nabla \cdot \mathbf{u}$ (volumetric strain rate).
