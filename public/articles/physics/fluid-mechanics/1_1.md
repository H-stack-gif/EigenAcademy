# Fluid Mechanics Topic 1.1: Continuum Hypothesis and Fluid Properties

When you pour water into a glass, you're witnessing the behavior of approximately $10^{25}$ molecules—yet you never think about individual H₂O molecules bouncing around. Instead, you see a continuous substance with well-defined properties like density and pressure at every point. This is the essence of the continuum hypothesis: treating fluids as continuous media rather than collections of discrete molecules. But when does this approximation break down? At what scale must we abandon continuum mechanics and return to molecular dynamics?

The continuum hypothesis isn't just mathematical convenience—it's a profound statement about scale separation. When the characteristic length scale of your problem $L$ is much larger than the molecular mean free path $\lambda$, the molecular graininess of matter becomes irrelevant. The Knudsen number $\text{Kn} = \lambda/L$ quantifies this separation: for $\text{Kn} \ll 1$, continuum mechanics reigns; for $\text{Kn} \gtrsim 1$, we enter the realm of rarefied gas dynamics where individual molecular collisions matter. Understanding where this boundary lies is crucial for everything from atmospheric re-entry (where spacecraft experience both regimes) to microfluidics (where nanoscale channels challenge the continuum assumption).

This article develops the foundation of continuum fluid mechanics. We'll establish when the continuum hypothesis applies, define fundamental fluid properties as continuous field variables, distinguish fluids from solids through their response to stress, explore compressibility and its role in flow behavior, and introduce the scalar fields that describe fluid state. You'll learn to evaluate when continuum mechanics is appropriate, compute fluid properties from field definitions, and understand the fundamental difference between Newtonian and non-Newtonian behavior. These concepts form the essential foundation for all of fluid mechanics.

## The Continuum Hypothesis

A fluid consists of molecules separated by distances comparable to molecular sizes, yet we treat it as a continuous medium. This works because:

**Continuum hypothesis**: Fluid properties (density, pressure, temperature) can be defined as continuous functions of position $\mathbf{r}$ and time $t$.

**Scale requirement**: The Knudsen number must be small:
$$\displaystyle \text{Kn} = \frac{\lambda}{L} \ll 1$$
where $\lambda$ is the molecular mean free path and $L$ is the characteristic length scale of the problem.

For air at standard conditions: $\lambda \approx 68$ nm. For water: $\lambda \approx 0.3$ nm. Most engineering flows have $L \gg \lambda$, making continuum mechanics applicable.

**Representative Elementary Volume (REV)**: We define fluid properties by taking limits over small volumes $\delta V$:
$$\displaystyle \rho(\mathbf{r}, t) = \lim_{\Delta V \to \delta V} \frac{\Delta m}{\Delta V}$$

The volume $\delta V$ must be:
- Large enough to contain many molecules (smooth out statistical fluctuations)
- Small enough that macroscopic properties don't vary significantly across it

Typically: $\lambda^3 \ll \delta V \ll L^3$

### Example 1: Knudsen Number Evaluation

Air at sea level has mean free path $\lambda = 68$ nm. Evaluate the Knudsen number for: (a) flow past an aircraft wing ($L = 2$ m), (b) flow in a microchannel ($L = 10$ μm).

**Solution:**

(a) Aircraft wing:
$$\displaystyle \text{Kn} = \frac{68 \times 10^{-9} \text{ m}}{2 \text{ m}} = 3.4 \times 10^{-8}$$

This is $\ll 1$, so continuum mechanics applies perfectly.

(b) Microchannel:
$$\displaystyle \text{Kn} = \frac{68 \times 10^{-9} \text{ m}}{10 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}} = 6.8 \times 10^{-3}$$

Still $< 0.01$, continuum mechanics applies but we're approaching the regime where slip boundary conditions matter.

**Answer:** Aircraft Kn = $3.4 \times 10^{-8}$ (continuum valid); microchannel Kn = $6.8 \times 10^{-3}$ (continuum valid with care)

### Example 2: REV for Water

For liquid water with molecular spacing $\approx 0.3$ nm, estimate the minimum REV size that contains enough molecules for statistical averaging.

**Solution:**

Require at least $N \sim 10^6$ molecules for good statistical averaging.

Molecular volume: $v_{mol} \sim (0.3 \times 10^{-9})^3 = 2.7 \times 10^{-29}$ m³

$$\displaystyle \delta V = N \cdot v_{mol} = 10^6 \times 2.7 \times 10^{-29} = 2.7 \times 10^{-23} \text{ m}^3$$

Characteristic length: $(\delta V)^{1/3} = (2.7 \times 10^{-23})^{1/3} \approx 3 \times 10^{-8}$ m = 30 nm

This is much smaller than typical flow length scales, confirming continuum validity.

**Answer:** REV $\sim 30$ nm, containing $\sim 10^6$ molecules

## Density and Pressure as Field Variables

**Density** is mass per unit volume:
$$\displaystyle \rho(\mathbf{r}, t) = \lim_{\Delta V \to \delta V} \frac{\Delta m}{\Delta V}$$

Units: kg/m³. For water: $\rho \approx 1000$ kg/m³. For air at STP: $\rho \approx 1.2$ kg/m³.

**Pressure** is normal force per unit area:
$$\displaystyle P(\mathbf{r}, t) = \lim_{\Delta A \to \delta A} \frac{\Delta F_n}{\Delta A}$$

Units: Pa (N/m²). Pressure is a scalar field—it has no direction. At any point in a fluid at rest, pressure acts equally in all directions (Pascal's principle).

**Key distinction**: In fluids at rest, stress is purely normal (pressure). Tangential stresses (shear) only appear when the fluid is moving.

### Example 3: Density Field

Consider a stratified fluid where density varies with height: $\rho(z) = \rho_0 e^{-z/H}$ where $H = 8000$ m (scale height). Calculate the density at $z = 0$, $z = H$, and $z = 2H$ if $\rho_0 = 1.2$ kg/m³.

**Solution:**

At $z = 0$:
$$\rho(0) = \rho_0 e^{0} = 1.2 \text{ kg/m}^3$$

At $z = H$:
$$\rho(H) = \rho_0 e^{-1} = 1.2 \times 0.368 = 0.44 \text{ kg/m}^3$$

At $z = 2H$:
$$\rho(2H) = \rho_0 e^{-2} = 1.2 \times 0.135 = 0.16 \text{ kg/m}^3$$

Density decreases exponentially with altitude.

**Answer:** $\rho(0) = 1.2$ kg/m³, $\rho(H) = 0.44$ kg/m³, $\rho(2H) = 0.16$ kg/m³

### Example 4: Pressure from Molecular Kinetics

For an ideal gas, pressure arises from molecular collisions with walls. The kinetic theory result is $P = \frac{1}{3}\rho \langle v^2 \rangle$ where $\langle v^2 \rangle$ is mean squared molecular speed. For air at 300 K with $\rho = 1.2$ kg/m³ and $\langle v^2 \rangle = 2.5 \times 10^5$ m²/s², calculate pressure.

**Solution:**

$$\displaystyle P = \frac{1}{3}\rho \langle v^2 \rangle = \frac{1}{3}(1.2)(2.5 \times 10^5)$$
$$\displaystyle P = 1.0 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa} = 1.0 \text{ atm}$$

This connects microscopic (molecular motion) to macroscopic (pressure field) descriptions.

**Answer:** $P = 1.0 \times 10^5$ Pa

## Distinguishing Fluids from Solids

The fundamental distinction:
- **Solid**: Resists shear stress by deforming elastically to a fixed strain
- **Fluid**: Cannot resist shear stress—it continuously deforms (flows) under any shear

**Material frame indifference**: Fluid behavior doesn't depend on the reference frame's motion or rotation. Properties are objective, not observer-dependent.

**Isotropic stress at rest**: In a static fluid, stress is the same in all directions:
$$\displaystyle \sigma_{ij} = -P\delta_{ij}$$

where $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta. The negative sign is convention: pressure is positive when compressive.

### Example 5: Stress Tensor in Static Fluid

For a fluid at rest with pressure $P = 2 \times 10^5$ Pa, write the stress tensor in Cartesian coordinates.

**Solution:**

$$\displaystyle \sigma_{ij} = -P\delta_{ij} = \begin{pmatrix} -P & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -P & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -P \end{pmatrix}$$

$$\displaystyle \sigma_{ij} = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \times 10^5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \times 10^5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -2 \times 10^5 \end{pmatrix} \text{ Pa}$$

All normal stresses equal (isotropy), all shear stresses zero (no motion).

**Answer:** Diagonal stress tensor with $\sigma_{11} = \sigma_{22} = \sigma_{33} = -2 \times 10^5$ Pa, all off-diagonal terms zero

## Compressibility

Fluids compress under pressure. **Bulk modulus** $K$ quantifies resistance to compression:
$$\displaystyle K = -V\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\right)_T$$

Alternatively, in terms of density:
$$\displaystyle K = \rho\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial \rho}\right)_T$$

**Incompressible approximation**: When density changes are negligible, treat $\rho = \text{constant}$. This applies when:
- Flow speeds $\ll$ speed of sound (Mach number Ma $\ll 1$)
- Pressure changes $\ll$ bulk modulus

For water: $K \approx 2.2 \times 10^9$ Pa (very stiff). For air at constant temperature: $K = P$ (much more compressible).

### Example 6: Compressibility of Water

Water has bulk modulus $K = 2.2 \times 10^9$ Pa. If pressure increases by $\Delta P = 1 \times 10^7$ Pa (100 atm), what is the fractional density change?

**Solution:**

From $K = \rho \frac{\partial P}{\partial \rho}$, for small changes:
$$\displaystyle K \approx \rho \frac{\Delta P}{\Delta \rho}$$

$$\displaystyle \frac{\Delta \rho}{\rho} = \frac{\Delta P}{K} = \frac{1 \times 10^7}{2.2 \times 10^9} = 4.5 \times 10^{-3}$$

Only 0.45% density change despite 100 atm pressure increase. Water is nearly incompressible.

**Answer:** $\Delta \rho / \rho = 0.45\%$

### Example 7: Isothermal Compressibility of Air

For an ideal gas at constant temperature, $P = \rho RT/M$ where $R$ is gas constant and $M$ is molar mass. Show that $K = P$.

**Solution:**

$$\displaystyle \frac{\partial P}{\partial \rho} = \frac{RT}{M}$$

But $P = \rho RT/M$, so $RT/M = P/\rho$.

$$\displaystyle K = \rho\frac{\partial P}{\partial \rho} = \rho \cdot \frac{P}{\rho} = P$$

For air at 1 atm: $K = 1 \times 10^5$ Pa—much more compressible than water ($K = 2.2 \times 10^9$ Pa).

**Answer:** For ideal gas, $K = P$; air is $\sim 20,000$ times more compressible than water

## Temperature and Thermal Properties

**Temperature** $T(\mathbf{r}, t)$ is another scalar field characterizing fluid state.

For ideal gases: $P = \rho RT/M$ (equation of state relating $P$, $\rho$, $T$)

**Thermal expansion coefficient**:
$$\displaystyle \beta = \frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P = -\frac{1}{\rho}\left(\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial T}\right)_P$$

For liquids: $\beta \sim 10^{-4}$ K⁻¹. For ideal gases: $\beta = 1/T$.

### Example 8: Thermal Expansion of Water

Water at 20°C has $\beta = 2.1 \times 10^{-4}$ K⁻¹ and $\rho_0 = 998$ kg/m³. Estimate the density at 30°C.

**Solution:**

$$\displaystyle \frac{\Delta \rho}{\rho_0} \approx -\beta \Delta T = -(2.1 \times 10^{-4})(10) = -2.1 \times 10^{-3}$$

$$\Delta \rho = -2.1 \text{ kg/m}^3$$

$$\rho(30°C) = 998 - 2.1 = 995.9 \text{ kg/m}^3$$

Water becomes slightly less dense when heated.

**Answer:** $\rho(30°C) \approx 996$ kg/m³

## Newtonian vs. Non-Newtonian Fluids

**Newtonian fluid**: Shear stress is linearly proportional to shear rate:
$$\displaystyle \tau = \mu \frac{du}{dy}$$

where $\mu$ is dynamic viscosity (constant for Newtonian fluids).

Examples: Water, air, most simple fluids

**Non-Newtonian fluid**: Shear stress has nonlinear relationship with shear rate. Categories include:
- **Shear-thinning** (pseudoplastic): $\mu$ decreases with shear rate (paint, blood)
- **Shear-thickening** (dilatant): $\mu$ increases with shear rate (cornstarch suspension)
- **Bingham plastic**: Requires yield stress to flow (toothpaste, mayonnaise)

Constitutive equations describe stress-strain rate relationships and define fluid types.

### Example 9: Classifying Fluid Behavior

A fluid's shear stress is measured at different shear rates:
| $\dot{\gamma}$ (s⁻¹) | 1 | 10 | 100 |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\tau$ (Pa) | 0.5 | 5.0 | 50.0 |

Is this Newtonian?

**Solution:**

Calculate apparent viscosity $\mu_{app} = \tau/\dot{\gamma}$:

At $\dot{\gamma} = 1$: $\mu_{app} = 0.5/1 = 0.5$ Pa·s
At $\dot{\gamma} = 10$: $\mu_{app} = 5.0/10 = 0.5$ Pa·s
At $\dot{\gamma} = 100$: $\mu_{app} = 50.0/100 = 0.5$ Pa·s

Viscosity constant across shear rates → Newtonian fluid.

**Answer:** Newtonian (constant viscosity $\mu = 0.5$ Pa·s)

### Example 10: Dimensional Analysis of Viscosity

Dynamic viscosity $\mu$ has units Pa·s. Show this equals kg/(m·s).

**Solution:**

$$\text{Pa} \cdot \text{s} = \frac{\text{N}}{\text{m}^2} \cdot \text{s} = \frac{\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}^2}{\text{m}^2} \cdot \text{s}$$

$$= \frac{\text{kg}}{\text{m} \cdot \text{s}}$$

Kinematic viscosity $\nu = \mu/\rho$ has units m²/s (like diffusivity).

**Answer:** $\mu$ in kg/(m·s); $\nu$ in m²/s

## Order of Magnitude Analysis

Estimating scales helps determine which effects matter.

**Example scaling**: For water flow at 1 m/s through 1 m pipe:
- Density: $\rho \sim 10^3$ kg/m³
- Viscosity: $\mu \sim 10^{-3}$ Pa·s
- Velocity scale: $U \sim 1$ m/s
- Length scale: $L \sim 1$ m

Reynolds number:
$$\displaystyle \text{Re} = \frac{\rho U L}{\mu} \sim \frac{(10^3)(1)(1)}{10^{-3}} = 10^6$$

High Re means inertia dominates viscosity—expect turbulent flow.

### Example 11: Scale Analysis for Microfluidics

In a microfluidic device: $L = 100$ μm, $U = 1$ mm/s, fluid is water. Estimate Reynolds number and determine if viscous or inertial effects dominate.

**Solution:**

$$\displaystyle \text{Re} = \frac{\rho U L}{\mu} = \frac{(1000)(10^{-3})(10^{-4})}{10^{-3}}$$

$$\displaystyle \text{Re} = \frac{10^{-4}}{10^{-3}} = 0.1$$

Since Re $\ll 1$, viscous forces dominate—flow is laminar and reversible (Stokes flow regime).

**Answer:** Re = 0.1, viscous forces dominate

### Example 12: Mach Number and Compressibility

Air flows at $U = 100$ m/s. Speed of sound in air is $c = 340$ m/s. Calculate Mach number and assess compressibility.

**Solution:**

$$\displaystyle \text{Ma} = \frac{U}{c} = \frac{100}{340} = 0.29$$

Since Ma $< 0.3$, density changes are small ($\Delta \rho/\rho < 5\%$). Incompressible flow approximation is valid.

**Answer:** Ma = 0.29, treat as incompressible

C) $10^{-4}$

A) Newtonian
B) Shear-thinning
C) Shear-thickening
D) Bingham plastic
E) Inviscid

**Problem 5:** For an ideal gas at constant temperature, doubling the pressure causes the density to:

A) Remain unchanged
B) Increase by $\sqrt{2}$
C) Double
D) Quadruple
E) Decrease by half

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
Continuum hypothesis requires length scale $L \gg$ mean free path $\lambda$
Therefore Kn $= \lambda/L \ll 1$
When Kn $\sim 1$ or larger, molecular effects dominate and continuum mechanics breaks down
Typical requirement: Kn $< 0.01$ for continuum validity

**Problem 2: C**
In a static fluid, stress tensor is $\sigma_{ij} = -P\delta_{ij}$
Off-diagonal terms ($i \neq j$) are shear stresses
$\delta_{xy} = 0$, so $\sigma_{xy} = -P \cdot 0 = 0$
Shear stresses only exist in moving fluids

**Problem 3: B**
$\displaystyle K = -V\frac{dP}{dV}$, so $\displaystyle \frac{|\Delta V|}{V} = \frac{\Delta P}{K}$
$\displaystyle \frac{|\Delta V|}{V} = \frac{2.2 \times 10^6}{2.2 \times 10^9} = 10^{-3}$
Water is nearly incompressible (small volume change for large pressure change)

**Problem 4: B**
Newtonian: $\tau \propto \dot{\gamma}^1$ (linear, exponent = 1)
Given: $\tau \propto \dot{\gamma}^{0.8}$ (exponent $< 1$)
Shear-thinning: apparent viscosity decreases with shear rate
$\mu_{app} = \tau/\dot{\gamma} = 5\dot{\gamma}^{-0.2}$ decreases as $\dot{\gamma}$ increases

**Problem 5: C**
Ideal gas: $P = \rho RT/M$ at constant $T$
$P \propto \rho$ (linear relationship)
If $P_2 = 2P_1$, then $\rho_2 = 2\rho_1$
Density doubles when pressure doubles (isothermal process)
