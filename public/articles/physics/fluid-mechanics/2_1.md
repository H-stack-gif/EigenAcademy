# Article 2.1: Kinematics: Describing Fluid Motion

Fluid motion presents a fundamental conceptual challenge: how do we describe the movement of a continuous medium composed of countless particles? Unlike solid mechanics where we track individual bodies, fluid mechanics requires describing the motion of deformable, flowing materials. Two complementary viewpoints emerge: the **Lagrangian description**, which follows individual fluid particles as they move, and the **Eulerian description**, which observes fluid properties at fixed points in space. The Eulerian approach dominates modern fluid mechanics because it naturally yields field equations—partial differential equations governing velocity, pressure, and other quantities as functions of position and time.

The key mathematical innovation is the **material derivative** $\frac{D}{Dt} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla$, which captures how properties change following a fluid particle while working in the Eulerian framework. This operator separates **local changes** (time variation at a fixed point) from **convective changes** (spatial variation as particles move). The material derivative appears throughout fluid mechanics: in acceleration $\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt}$, in conservation laws, and in transport equations. Understanding its physical meaning—the rate of change experienced by a moving fluid element—is crucial.

Visualizing fluid motion requires understanding **streamlines** (curves tangent to the velocity field at an instant), **pathlines** (trajectories of individual particles), and **streaklines** (loci of particles that passed through a point). In steady flow, these three coincide, providing powerful visualization. In unsteady flow, they differ, revealing the complexity of time-dependent motion. This article develops both Lagrangian and Eulerian descriptions, derives the material derivative, explores fluid visualization concepts, introduces the velocity gradient tensor and its decomposition into strain and rotation, and demonstrates applications through detailed examples involving acceleration, streamlines, and coordinate transformations.

## Lagrangian Description: Following Particles

The **Lagrangian viewpoint** labels each fluid particle and tracks its position $\mathbf{X}(t)$ as a function of time.

**Initial position**: Label particle by its position at $t = 0$: $\mathbf{X}_0 = \mathbf{X}(0) = (X_0, Y_0, Z_0)$

**Current position**: $\mathbf{X}(t) = (X(t), Y(t), Z(t))$ where $\mathbf{X}(0) = \mathbf{X}_0$

**Velocity**: $\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{X}_0, t) = \frac{d\mathbf{X}}{dt}$

**Acceleration**: $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X}_0, t) = \frac{d^2\mathbf{X}}{dt^2}$

**Example of Lagrangian description**: A particle initially at $(1, 0, 0)$ follows the trajectory:
$$\mathbf{X}(t) = (1 + 2t, 3t^2, 0)$$

Velocity: $\mathbf{V} = (2, 6t, 0)$

Acceleration: $\mathbf{A} = (0, 6, 0)$

**Limitation**: For $N$ particles, we need to track $N$ trajectories—impractical for continua with $N \to \infty$.

## Eulerian Description: Fixed Observation Points

The **Eulerian viewpoint** defines fields at fixed spatial locations.

**Velocity field**: $\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r}, t) = (u, v, w)$ where $\mathbf{r} = (x, y, z)$

At each point $\mathbf{r}$ and time $t$, we observe whatever fluid happens to be there.

**Relationship to Lagrangian**: If we know the Lagrangian paths, we can find Eulerian velocity:
$$\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r}, t) = \left.\frac{d\mathbf{X}}{dt}\right|_{\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{r}}$$

Conversely, pathlines satisfy: $\frac{d\mathbf{X}}{dt} = \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}(t), t)$

**Advantage**: Describes entire flow field with one velocity function $\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r}, t)$, independent of number of particles.

## Material Derivative

**Question**: How fast does a property $\phi$ (temperature, pressure, concentration) change for a moving fluid particle?

In Lagrangian framework: $\frac{d\phi}{dt}$ (ordinary derivative following particle)

In Eulerian framework: Need to account for both local time variation and spatial variation due to motion.

**Derivation**: Consider property $\phi(\mathbf{r}, t)$ in Eulerian description. For particle at $\mathbf{r}(t)$:

$$\frac{d\phi}{dt} = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt} + \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial z}\frac{dz}{dt}$$

Since $\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} = \mathbf{u}$:

$$\boxed{\frac{D\phi}{Dt} = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla\phi}$$

This is the **material derivative** (also called **substantial derivative** or **Lagrangian derivative**).

**Components**:
- $\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t}$: **local derivative** (change at fixed point)
- $\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla\phi = u\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} + w\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial z}$: **convective derivative** (change due to moving to different location)

**Operator notation**: $\frac{D}{Dt} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla$

## Acceleration in Eulerian Frame

Applying material derivative to velocity itself:

$$\boxed{\mathbf{a} = \frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} = \frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u}}$$

**Terms**:
- $\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t}$: **local acceleration** (unsteady effect)
- $(\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u}$: **convective acceleration** (nonlinear term!)

**Expanded in Cartesian coordinates**:
$$a_x = \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + w\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}$$

The nonlinearity of $(\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u}$ makes Navier-Stokes equations fundamentally difficult.

**Physical interpretation**: Even in steady flow ($\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} = 0$), particles accelerate if velocity varies spatially.

## Streamlines, Pathlines, and Streaklines

**Streamlines**: Curves tangent to velocity field at a given instant.

Differential equation: $\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{ds} = \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r}, t)$ at fixed $t$

In component form:
$$\boxed{\frac{dx}{u} = \frac{dy}{v} = \frac{dz}{w}}$$

Solve two of these to find streamline through a point.

**Pathlines**: Actual trajectories of fluid particles.

Differential equation: $\frac{d\mathbf{X}}{dt} = \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}(t), t)$

Initial condition: $\mathbf{X}(t_0) = \mathbf{X}_0$

**Streaklines**: Locus of all particles that passed through a given point $\mathbf{r}_0$ at previous times.

For each $\tau < t$, solve pathline from $(\mathbf{r}_0, \tau)$ to time $t$.

**Steady flow**: All three coincide because velocity field doesn't change with time.

**Unsteady flow**: Three differ—provides rich information about flow structure.

## Coordinate Systems

**Cartesian** $(x, y, z)$: Simplest, used throughout.

**Cylindrical** $(r, \theta, z)$:
$$x = r\cos\theta, \quad y = r\sin\theta, \quad z = z$$

Velocity: $\mathbf{u} = u_r\hat{\mathbf{r}} + u_\theta\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} + u_z\hat{\mathbf{z}}$

Material derivative:
$$\frac{D}{Dt} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + u_r\frac{\partial}{\partial r} + \frac{u_\theta}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} + u_z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}$$

**Spherical** $(r, \theta, \phi)$:
$$x = r\sin\theta\cos\phi, \quad y = r\sin\theta\sin\phi, \quad z = r\cos\theta$$

More complex expressions for material derivative (see standard references).

## Example 1: Material Derivative Calculation

Given velocity field $\mathbf{u} = (2x, -2y, 0)$ and temperature $T = 100 + x^2 + y^2$, find $\frac{DT}{Dt}$ at point $(1, 1, 0)$.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{DT}{Dt} = \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial T}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial T}{\partial y} + w\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}$$

Compute derivatives:
- $\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = 0$ (steady)
- $\frac{\partial T}{\partial x} = 2x$, at $(1,1,0)$: $= 2$
- $\frac{\partial T}{\partial y} = 2y$, at $(1,1,0)$: $= 2$
- $\frac{\partial T}{\partial z} = 0$

Velocity at $(1,1,0)$: $\mathbf{u} = (2, -2, 0)$

$$\frac{DT}{Dt} = 0 + (2)(2) + (-2)(2) + 0 = 4 - 4 = 0$$

**Physical interpretation**: Despite temperature varying in space, particles moving through $(1,1,0)$ experience no temperature change—they move along an isotherm.

**Answer:** $\frac{DT}{Dt} = 0$ K/s

## Example 2: Convective Acceleration

For velocity field $\mathbf{u} = (U_0, 0, 0)$ (uniform flow), calculate acceleration.

**Solution:**

$$\mathbf{a} = \frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u}$$

Since $\mathbf{u}$ is constant in space and time:
- $\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} = 0$
- $\nabla u = 0$, so $(\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u} = 0$

$$\mathbf{a} = 0$$

**Physical meaning**: Uniform flow—particles maintain constant velocity, no acceleration.

**Answer:** $\mathbf{a} = 0$

---

## Example 3: Acceleration in Converging Nozzle

Steady flow in converging nozzle: $u = U_0(1 + x/L)$, $v = w = 0$.

Find acceleration at $x = 0$.

**Solution:**

$$a_x = \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + w\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}$$

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = 0$$ (steady)

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{U_0}{L}$$

At $x = 0$: $u = U_0$, $v = w = 0$

$$a_x = 0 + U_0 \cdot \frac{U_0}{L} + 0 + 0 = \frac{U_0^2}{L}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Even though flow is steady, particles accelerate as they enter the converging section—purely convective acceleration.

**Answer:** $a_x = \frac{U_0^2}{L}$ (positive, accelerating)

## Example 4: Streamlines in 2D Flow

Find streamlines for $\mathbf{u} = (y, -x, 0)$.

**Solution:**

Streamline equations:
$$\frac{dx}{u} = \frac{dy}{v}$$

$$\frac{dx}{y} = \frac{dy}{-x}$$

Cross-multiply:
$$-x \, dx = y \, dy$$

Integrate:
$$-\frac{x^2}{2} = \frac{y^2}{2} + C$$

$$x^2 + y^2 = -2C = \text{constant}$$

**Streamlines are circles** centered at origin.

**Physical interpretation**: Circular streamlines indicate rotational flow (vortex).

**Answer:** Streamlines are circles $x^2 + y^2 = R^2$

---

## Example 5: Pathline Calculation

Given $\mathbf{u} = (1, 2, 0)$ (uniform flow), find pathline starting at $(0, 0, 0)$ at $t = 0$.

**Solution:**

Pathline equations:
$$\frac{dX}{dt} = u = 1, \quad \frac{dY}{dt} = v = 2, \quad \frac{dZ}{dt} = w = 0$$

With $X(0) = Y(0) = Z(0) = 0$:

$$X(t) = t, \quad Y(t) = 2t, \quad Z(t) = 0$$

Or in vector form: $\mathbf{X}(t) = (t, 2t, 0) = t(1, 2, 0)$

**Answer:** $\mathbf{X}(t) = (t, 2t, 0)$ (straight line)

---

## Example 6: Unsteady Flow Pathline

For $\mathbf{u} = (1, \cos(\omega t), 0)$, find pathline from $(0, 0, 0)$ at $t = 0$.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{dX}{dt} = 1, \quad \frac{dY}{dt} = \cos(\omega t), \quad \frac{dZ}{dt} = 0$$

Integrate with initial conditions:

$$X(t) = \int_0^t 1 \, d\tau = t$$

$$Y(t) = \int_0^t \cos(\omega\tau) \, d\tau = \frac{\sin(\omega t)}{\omega}$$

$$Z(t) = 0$$

**Answer:** $\mathbf{X}(t) = \left(t, \frac{\sin(\omega t)}{\omega}, 0\right)$ (oscillating path)

---

## Example 7: Streamlines vs. Pathlines (Unsteady)

For $\mathbf{u} = (1, t, 0)$, compare streamlines at $t = 1$ with pathline from $(0, 0, 0)$ at $t = 0$.

**Solution:**

**Streamlines at $t = 1$**:
$$\frac{dx}{1} = \frac{dy}{1}$$

Integrate: $y = x + C$

Through $(0, 0)$: $y = x$ (straight line at 45°)

**Pathline**:
$$\frac{dX}{dt} = 1, \quad \frac{dY}{dt} = t$$

Integrate from $t = 0$:
$$X(t) = t, \quad Y(t) = \frac{t^2}{2}$$

Eliminate $t$: $Y = \frac{X^2}{2}$ (parabola)

**Conclusion**: Streamline is straight, pathline is parabolic—they differ in unsteady flow.

**Answer:** Streamline: $y = x$; Pathline: $y = x^2/2$

## Example 8: Cylindrical Coordinates Material Derivative

In cylindrical coordinates, for $\phi = r^2\cos\theta$ and $\mathbf{u} = (0, r\omega, 0)$ (solid body rotation), find $\frac{D\phi}{Dt}$.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{D\phi}{Dt} = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} + u_r\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial r} + \frac{u_\theta}{r}\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial \theta} + u_z\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial z}$$

$$\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} = 0$$

$$\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial r} = 2r\cos\theta$$

$$\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial \theta} = -r^2\sin\theta$$

$$\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial z} = 0$$

Velocity: $u_r = 0$, $u_\theta = r\omega$, $u_z = 0$

$$\frac{D\phi}{Dt} = 0 + 0 + \frac{r\omega}{r}(-r^2\sin\theta) + 0 = -r^2\omega\sin\theta$$

**Answer:** $\frac{D\phi}{Dt} = -r^2\omega\sin\theta$

## Example 9: Steady vs. Unsteady Classification

Classify each flow:
(a) $\mathbf{u} = (x, -y, 0)$
(b) $\mathbf{u} = (x\cos t, y\sin t, 0)$
(c) $\mathbf{u} = (1 + 0.1\sin(2\pi t), 0, 0)$

**Solution:**

(a) No explicit time dependence → **steady**
(b) Explicit time dependence ($\cos t$, $\sin t$) → **unsteady**
(c) Explicit time dependence ($\sin(2\pi t)$) → **unsteady**

**Physical distinction**: In steady flow, velocity at any fixed point doesn't change with time; in unsteady flow, it does.

**Answer:** (a) steady; (b) unsteady; (c) unsteady

## Example 10: Acceleration in Rotating Frame

Solid body rotation: $u_r = 0$, $u_\theta = r\omega$, $u_z = 0$ in cylindrical coordinates.

Find acceleration (centripetal).

**Solution:**

In cylindrical coordinates, acceleration has form:
$$a_r = \frac{\partial u_r}{\partial t} + u_r\frac{\partial u_r}{\partial r} + \frac{u_\theta}{r}\frac{\partial u_r}{\partial \theta} - \frac{u_\theta^2}{r} + u_z\frac{\partial u_r}{\partial z}$$

For our velocity:
- All $\frac{\partial u_r}{\partial \cdot} = 0$ since $u_r = 0$
- $u_z = 0$
- Last term: $-\frac{u_\theta^2}{r} = -\frac{(r\omega)^2}{r} = -r\omega^2$

$$a_r = -r\omega^2$$

This is the **centripetal acceleration** pointing inward.

**Answer:** $a_r = -r\omega^2$ (toward center)

---

## Example 11: Local vs. Convective Terms

For $u = U_0(1 + 0.1\sin(\omega t))(1 + x/L)$ with $v = w = 0$, identify local and convective acceleration at $x = 0$.

**Solution:**

$$a_x = \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}$$

**Local term**:
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = U_0(0.1\omega\cos(\omega t))(1 + x/L)$$

At $x = 0$: $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = 0.1U_0\omega\cos(\omega t)$

**Convective term**:
$$u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = U_0(1 + 0.1\sin(\omega t))(1 + x/L) \cdot U_0(1 + 0.1\sin(\omega t))\frac{1}{L}$$

At $x = 0$:
$$u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{U_0^2}{L}(1 + 0.1\sin(\omega t))^2$$

**Answer:** Local: $0.1U_0\omega\cos(\omega t)$; Convective: $\frac{U_0^2}{L}(1 + 0.1\sin(\omega t))^2$

## Example 12: Streamline Through Point

For $\mathbf{u} = (x, -y, 0)$, find streamline through $(2, 1, 0)$.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{dx}{x} = \frac{dy}{-y}$$

$$\frac{dx}{x} + \frac{dy}{y} = 0$$

Integrate:
$$\ln|x| + \ln|y| = C$$

$$xy = \text{constant}$$

Through $(2, 1, 0)$: $xy = 2$

**Answer:** Streamline is hyperbola $xy = 2$

A) Individual particles
B) Properties at fixed spatial locations

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B** - Material derivative $\frac{D\phi}{Dt} = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla\phi$ gives rate of change following a moving particle.

**Problem 2: B** - In steady flow ($\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} = 0$), all three curves coincide.

**Problem 3: B** - Convective acceleration arises from spatial velocity variations: $(\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u} = u\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial x} + \cdots$

**Problem 4: C** - Uniform flow: constant velocity everywhere, so $\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} = 0$ and $\nabla\mathbf{u} = 0$ → $\mathbf{a} = 0$.

**Problem 5: B** - Eulerian description observes fields (velocity, pressure, etc.) at fixed spatial locations as fluid flows past.
