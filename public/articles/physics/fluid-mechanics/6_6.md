# Article 6.6: Multidimensional Supersonic Flow

Beyond 1D analyses, **multidimensional supersonic flows** around wings, bodies, and inlets require methods accounting for spatial variations. **Linearized supersonic theory** applies to thin airfoils at small angles of attack, where perturbations $u', v', p'$ small compared to freestream. The governing equation $(\text{Ma}_\infty^2 - 1)\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2} - \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2} = 0$ (supersonic potential flow PDE) is **hyperbolic** with characteristic slopes $\pm 1/\sqrt{\text{Ma}_\infty^2 - 1}$, fundamentally different from subsonic elliptic equations. **Ackeret formula** for thin airfoil lift coefficient $C_L = \frac{4\alpha}{\sqrt{\text{Ma}_\infty^2 - 1}}$ shows linear dependence on angle of attack $\alpha$ and inverse dependence on $\sqrt{\text{Ma}_\infty^2 - 1}$ (Prandtl-Glauert factor). **Wave drag** $C_D = \frac{4\alpha^2}{\sqrt{\text{Ma}_\infty^2 - 1}}$ arises from shock-expansion system—absent in subsonic flow—scales as $\alpha^2$.

**Shock-expansion theory** combines oblique shock (lower surface compression) and Prandtl-Meyer expansion (upper surface) for more accurate airfoil analysis beyond linearization. The **area rule** (transonic drag reduction) recognizes that wave drag depends on axial distribution of cross-sectional area, not specific geometry—motivates "wasp waist" fuselages near wing junction. **Conical flows** (bodies of revolution, delta wings) admit similarity solutions reducing 3D → 2D via conical coordinates. **Hypersonic similarity** ($\text{Ma}_\infty \gg 1$) introduces parameter $K = \text{Ma}_\infty\theta$ (hypersonic similarity parameter) collapsing solutions: flows with same $K$ exhibit similar pressure distributions despite different Ma and $\theta$. **Real gas effects** (dissociation, ionization) dominate at hypersonic speeds, invalidating perfect gas assumptions and dramatically altering thermodynamics.

This article derives linearized supersonic PDE from small-perturbation Euler equations, develops Ackeret formula for $C_L$ and $C_D$ via surface pressure integration, discusses validity limits of linearization, treats shock-expansion theory for moderate thickness airfoils, introduces area rule and transonic drag considerations, covers conical flow similarity solutions, presents hypersonic similarity parameters and limiting behaviors, mentions real gas effects and high-temperature chemistry, and works through extensive examples involving Ackeret calculations, wave drag estimation, linearization validity checks, conical flow solutions, and hypersonic parameter calculations.

## Linearized Supersonic Flow

**Small perturbations** from uniform stream:

$$u = U_\infty + u', \quad v = v', \quad P = P_\infty + p'$$

where $u', v', p' \ll U_\infty, P_\infty$.

**Velocity potential**: $\mathbf{u} = \nabla\phi$

**Linearized equation** (supersonic):

$$\boxed{(\text{Ma}_\infty^2 - 1)\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2} - \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2} = 0}$$

**Hyperbolic PDE** (wave equation-like) vs. subsonic elliptic.

**Characteristics**: $\frac{dy}{dx} = \pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{\text{Ma}_\infty^2 - 1}}$

## Example 1: Characteristic Slope

$\text{Ma}_\infty = 2.0$.

Find characteristic slopes.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{4-1}} = \pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = \pm 0.577$$

**Mach angle**: $\mu = \tan^{-1}(0.577) = 30°$ ✓

**Characteristics** = Mach lines in linearized theory.

**Answer:** Slopes $\pm 0.577$

---

## Ackeret Formula for Lift

**Thin airfoil** at angle of attack $\alpha$ (small):

**Pressure coefficient**:
$$C_p = \frac{p' - P_\infty}{\frac{1}{2}\rho_\infty U_\infty^2} = \pm\frac{2\theta}{\sqrt{\text{Ma}_\infty^2 - 1}}$$

where $\theta$ = local surface angle (+ compression, - expansion).

**Lift coefficient**:

$$\boxed{C_L = \frac{4\alpha}{\sqrt{\text{Ma}_\infty^2 - 1}}}$$

**Moment** about leading edge: $C_M = -\frac{C_L}{2}$ (center of pressure at mid-chord).

## Example 2: Ackeret Lift Coefficient

Airfoil: $\alpha = 5° = 0.0873$ rad, $\text{Ma}_\infty = 2.5$.

Find $C_L$.

**Solution:**

$$C_L = \frac{4(0.0873)}{\sqrt{6.25-1}} = \frac{0.3492}{\sqrt{5.25}} = \frac{0.3492}{2.291} = 0.1524$$

**Answer:** $C_L = 0.15$

---

## Wave Drag

**Supersonic**: Shock/expansion system creates **wave drag** (absent subsonically).

**Ackeret formula**:

$$\boxed{C_D = \frac{4\alpha^2}{\sqrt{\text{Ma}_\infty^2 - 1}}}$$

**Scales as $\alpha^2$** (induced by lift) + thickness drag.

**No suction force** at leading edge (attached flow).

## Example 3: Wave Drag Calculation

From Example 2: $\alpha = 5°$, $\text{Ma} = 2.5$.

Find $C_D$.

**Solution:**

$$C_D = \frac{4(0.0873)^2}{\sqrt{5.25}} = \frac{4(0.00762)}{2.291} = \frac{0.0305}{2.291} = 0.0133$$

**Wave drag** ~1/10th of $C_L$.

**Additional**: Friction drag ~0.002-0.005 for typical airfoil.

**Answer:** $C_D = 0.013$ (wave only)

---

## Lift-to-Drag Ratio

$$\frac{L}{D} = \frac{C_L}{C_D} = \frac{\frac{4\alpha}{\sqrt{\text{Ma}^2-1}}}{\frac{4\alpha^2}{\sqrt{\text{Ma}^2-1}}} = \frac{1}{\alpha}$$

**For small $\alpha$**: High L/D possible.

**Supersonic transport**: Typically L/D ~ 5-8 (vs. 15-20 subsonic).

## Example 4: Optimal Angle of Attack

Maximize L/D with $C_D = C_{D,wave} + C_{D,friction}$ where $C_{D,f} = 0.005$.

Find optimal $\alpha$ ($\text{Ma} = 2$).

**Solution:**

$$C_D = \frac{4\alpha^2}{\sqrt{3}} + 0.005$$

$$\frac{L}{D} = \frac{4\alpha/\sqrt{3}}{4\alpha^2/\sqrt{3} + 0.005}$$

**Differentiate** and set = 0 (calculus):

Optimal when: $C_{D,wave} = C_{D,f}$

$$\frac{4\alpha^2}{\sqrt{3}} = 0.005$$

$$\alpha = \sqrt{\frac{0.005\sqrt{3}}{4}} = \sqrt{0.00217} = 0.0465 \text{ rad} = 2.67°$$

**Answer:** $\alpha_{opt} \approx 2.7°$

## Linearization Validity

**Requires**:
1. Thin airfoil ($t/c \ll 1$)
2. Small $\alpha$ (typically $\alpha < 10°$)
3. Moderate supersonic Ma (not transonic, not hypersonic)

**Breakdown**: Thick airfoils, large $\alpha$, strong shocks.

## Example 5: Linearization Check

Airfoil: $t/c = 0.15$, $\alpha = 8°$, $\text{Ma} = 1.8$.

Is linearization valid?

**Solution:**

**Thickness**: $t/c = 0.15$ (moderate, acceptable)

**Angle**: $\alpha = 8° = 0.14$ rad (borderline)

**Mach**: 1.8 (not too close to 1)

**Probably acceptable** but approaching limits (15% thickness is high).

**Better**: Use shock-expansion or CFD for higher accuracy.

**Answer:** Marginal (acceptable with caution)

## Shock-Expansion Theory

**Beyond linearization**: Combine oblique shock (compression) and Prandtl-Meyer (expansion).

**Lower surface** (compression): Oblique shock relations.

**Upper surface** (expansion): Prandtl-Meyer function.

**More accurate** for moderate $\alpha$ and thickness.

## Example 6: Shock-Expansion Lift

Flat plate $\alpha = 10°$, $\text{Ma}_\infty = 2.0$.

Compare Ackeret vs. shock-expansion $C_L$.

**Solution:**

**Ackeret**:
$$C_L = \frac{4(0.1745)}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{0.698}{1.732} = 0.403$$

**Shock-expansion** (more involved):
- Lower: Oblique shock at $\beta \approx 47°$ → $C_{p,lower} \approx 0.42$
- Upper: Expansion $\Delta\theta = 10°$ → $C_{p,upper} \approx -0.20$
- $C_L = C_{p,lower} - C_{p,upper} = 0.42 - (-0.20) = 0.62$

**Nonlinear effects** increase lift ~50% over linear!

**Answer:** Shock-expansion: 0.62 vs. Ackeret: 0.40

## Area Rule

**Whitcomb's discovery** (1950s): Transonic wave drag depends on **axial distribution of total cross-sectional area** (including wings, fuselage, nacelles).

**Sears-Haack body**: Minimum drag for given length and volume → smooth area distribution.

**Application**: "Wasp waist" fuselages (indent where wing attaches) → reduce transonic drag.

## Conical Flow

**Geometry**: Bodies of revolution, delta wings with straight leading edges.

**Similarity**: Flow on any cone through apex has same properties at same cone angle.

**Reduces**: 3D → 2D problem in conical coordinates.

**Taylor-Maccoll equations**: Govern conical shock flows.

## Example 7: Cone Half-Angle Effect

Cone $\theta_c = 20°$ vs. $\theta_c = 10°$ at $\text{Ma}_\infty = 3$.

Which has higher drag?

**Solution:**

**Larger half-angle** → **stronger shock** → higher pressure on surface → **higher drag**.

$\theta_c = 20°$ has higher $C_D$ than $10°$.

**Slender cones** (small $\theta_c$) minimize wave drag.

**Answer:** 20° cone has higher drag

## Hypersonic Similarity

**Hypersonic limit**: $\text{Ma}_\infty \gg 1$

**Similarity parameter**: $K = \text{Ma}_\infty\theta$ (or $\text{Ma}_\infty\sin\theta$ for wedges)

**Flows with same $K$** have similar $C_p$ distributions.

**Example**: Ma = 10, $\theta = 5°$ similar to Ma = 20, $\theta = 2.5°$ (both $K \approx 0.87$).

## Example 8: Hypersonic Similarity Parameter

Wedge: $\theta = 10°$, $\text{Ma}_\infty = 8$.

Find $K$ and estimate similar flow.

**Solution:**

$$K = \text{Ma}_\infty\theta = 8(0.1745) = 1.396$$

**Similar flow**: Ma = 16, $\theta = 5°$ → $K = 16(0.0873) = 1.397$ ✓

**Same pressure coefficient** (approximately).

**Answer:** $K = 1.40$; similar at Ma=16, θ=5°

## High Temperature Effects

**Hypersonic** ($\text{Ma} > 5$): Severe aerodynamic heating.

**Stagnation temperature**: $T_0 = T_\infty(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}\text{Ma}^2)$

For Ma = 10: $T_0 = 21T_\infty$ (if $T_\infty = 250$ K → $T_0 = 5250$ K!)

**Real gas effects**:
- Vibrational excitation ($T > 800$ K)
- Dissociation ($T > 2000$ K, $O_2 \to 2O$)
- Ionization ($T > 9000$ K)

**Invalidates** $\gamma = \text{const}$ assumption.

## Example 9: Stagnation Temperature at Hypersonic Speed

$\text{Ma}_\infty = 20$, $T_\infty = 250$ K.

Find $T_0$ (assuming $\gamma = 1.4$ initially).

**Solution:**

$$T_0 = 250\left(1 + 0.2(400)\right) = 250(1 + 80) = 250(81) = 20,250 \text{ K}$$

**Extremely high!** Real gas effects dominant.

**Actual**: $\gamma$ drops, dissociation absorbs energy → lower $T_0$ (~10,000 K).

**Answer:** $T_0 \approx 20,000$ K (perfect gas); ~10,000 K (real gas)

## Pressure Coefficient Limits

**Newtonian theory** (hypersonic blunt body):

$$C_p = 2\sin^2\theta$$

**Maximum**: $C_{p,max} = 2$ (stagnation point, $\theta = 90°$).

**Simple estimate** for very high Ma.

## Example 10: Newtonian vs. Exact Pressure

Flat plate normal to flow ($\theta = 90°$), $\text{Ma}_\infty = 10$.

Compare Newtonian $C_p$ to normal shock value.

**Solution:**

**Newtonian**: $C_p = 2\sin^2(90°) = 2$

**Normal shock** at Ma = 10:
$$\frac{P_2}{P_1} = 1 + 1.167(100-1) = 116.5$$

$$C_p = \frac{P_2 - P_1}{\frac{1}{2}\rho_1 U_1^2} = \frac{115.5P_1}{\frac{1}{2}(\gamma P_1\text{Ma}^2)} = \frac{115.5}{0.5(1.4)(100)} = \frac{115.5}{70} = 1.65$$

**Newtonian overestimates** slightly (but close at high Ma).

**Answer:** Newtonian: 2.0; Exact: 1.65

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C** - Ackeret formula: **linearized supersonic** theory for **thin airfoils** at small $\alpha$, gives $C_L = 4\alpha/\sqrt{\text{Ma}^2-1}$, invalid subsonic/transonic/hypersonic.

**Problem 2: B** - Wave drag: $C_D = 4\alpha^2/\sqrt{\text{Ma}^2-1}$ scales as **$\alpha^2$** (induced drag from lift-producing shock/expansion system).

**Problem 3: C** - Linearized supersonic: $(\text{Ma}^2-1)\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2} - \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2} = 0$ is **hyperbolic** (wave equation type), characteristics = Mach lines.

**Problem 4: C** - Hypersonic similarity: flows with same $K = \text{Ma}_\infty\theta$ (or $\text{Ma}_\infty\sin\theta$) have similar $C_p$ distributions at high Ma.

**Problem 5: C** - Hypersonic: $T_0 \propto \text{Ma}^2$ → extreme **aerodynamic heating** (thousands of K), real gas effects, thermal protection critical for reentry vehicles.
