# Fluid Mechanics Topic 1.3: Fluid Statics and Pressure Distribution

Stand at the bottom of a swimming pool and you feel pressure pushing on your eardrums. Dive deeper and the pressure increases—your ears might pop. This hydrostatic pressure isn't random; it follows a precise mathematical relationship determined by gravity and the fluid's weight. At any depth $h$ below the surface, the pressure exceeds atmospheric pressure by $\rho gh$, the weight of the overlying fluid column per unit area. This simple principle—pressure increases with depth—governs everything from dam design (where water pressure creates enormous forces on structures) to atmospheric science (where pressure decreases exponentially with altitude).

Fluid statics deals with fluids at rest, where every element is in mechanical equilibrium. With no motion, there are no viscous stresses—only pressure forces and body forces (typically gravity) act on fluid elements. The governing equation, $\nabla P = \rho\mathbf{g}$, is elegant: pressure gradient balances gravitational body force. From this, we derive pressure distributions in oceans, atmospheres, and enclosed tanks; calculate forces on submerged surfaces like dam walls and submarine hulls; and explain buoyancy, the upward force that makes boats float and balloons rise. Understanding hydrostatics is essential for hydraulic engineering, ship design, and atmospheric physics.

This article develops the principles of fluid statics. We'll derive the hydrostatic equilibrium equation from force balance, solve for pressure distributions in constant and variable density fluids, distinguish between absolute, gauge, and atmospheric pressure, calculate hydrostatic forces on plane and curved surfaces including centers of pressure, explain Archimedes' principle and its applications to buoyancy and stability, and apply these principles to manometry and pressure measurement. You'll learn to compute pressure fields, predict forces on structures, and analyze floating and submerged body equilibrium.

## Hydrostatic Equilibrium

Consider a fluid element at rest. For equilibrium, net force must vanish.

**Forces on fluid element**:
- Pressure forces: $-\nabla P$ (gradient points from high to low pressure)
- Body force: $\rho\mathbf{g}$ (gravitational force per unit volume)

**Force balance**:
$\displaystyle \nabla P = \rho\mathbf{g}$

This is the **fundamental equation of fluid statics**.

For standard gravity $\mathbf{g} = -g\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ (where $z$ increases upward):
$\displaystyle \frac{dP}{dz} = -\rho g$

Pressure decreases with height (or increases with depth).

### Example 1: Deriving Hydrostatic Equation

Consider a cylindrical fluid element of height $\Delta z$ and cross-sectional area $A$. The bottom face is at height $z$ with pressure $P(z)$; the top face is at $z + \Delta z$ with pressure $P(z + \Delta z)$. Derive the pressure gradient.

**Solution:**

Forces in $z$-direction:
- Bottom face (upward): $P(z) \cdot A$
- Top face (downward): $-P(z + \Delta z) \cdot A$
- Weight (downward): $-\rho g \cdot A \Delta z$

Equilibrium:
$P(z) \cdot A - P(z + \Delta z) \cdot A - \rho g \cdot A \Delta z = 0$

Divide by $A \Delta z$:
$\displaystyle \frac{P(z) - P(z + \Delta z)}{\Delta z} = -\rho g$

Take limit $\Delta z \to 0$:
$\displaystyle -\frac{dP}{dz} = -\rho g$
$\displaystyle \frac{dP}{dz} = -\rho g$

**Answer:** $\displaystyle \frac{dP}{dz} = -\rho g$ (pressure decreases with height)

## Pressure in Constant Density Fluids

For incompressible fluid ($\rho = \text{constant}$), integrate hydrostatic equation:

$\displaystyle \int_{P_1}^{P_2} dP = -\rho g \int_{z_1}^{z_2} dz$

$\displaystyle P_2 - P_1 = -\rho g(z_2 - z_1)$

If $z_2 > z_1$ (higher elevation): $P_2 < P_1$ (lower pressure)

**Pressure at depth $h$ below surface**:
$\displaystyle P(h) = P_0 + \rho gh$

where $P_0$ is surface pressure.

### Example 2: Pressure at Ocean Depth

Calculate pressure at ocean depth $h = 1000$ m. Use $\rho = 1025$ kg/m³ for seawater, $g = 9.81$ m/s², and $P_0 = 1.01 \times 10^5$ Pa.

**Solution:**

$P(h) = P_0 + \rho gh$
$= 1.01 \times 10^5 + (1025)(9.81)(1000)$
$= 1.01 \times 10^5 + 1.005 \times 10^7$
$= 1.016 \times 10^7 \text{ Pa} = 10.16 \text{ MPa}$

This is about 100 atm—enormous pressure! Submarine hulls must withstand such forces.

**Answer:** $P = 10.16$ MPa $\approx$ 100 atm

### Example 3: Pressure Difference in Tank

A water tank has depth 5 m. Calculate pressure difference between bottom and surface.

**Solution:**

$\Delta P = \rho g h = (1000)(9.81)(5) = 49,050 \text{ Pa} \approx 49 \text{ kPa}$

Equivalently, $\Delta P \approx 0.49$ atm or about 7 psi.

**Answer:** $\Delta P = 49$ kPa

### Example 4: Multifluid System

A U-tube contains water (density $\rho_w = 1000$ kg/m³) and oil (density $\rho_o = 800$ kg/m³) that don't mix. The water column is 10 cm high, and the oil column is 15 cm high. If atmospheric pressure is $P_0 = 1.01 \times 10^5$ Pa, find pressure at the water-oil interface.

**Solution:**

Start from oil surface (at $P_0$) and descend through oil:
$P_{interface} = P_0 + \rho_o g h_o$
$= 1.01 \times 10^5 + (800)(9.81)(0.15)$
$= 1.01 \times 10^5 + 1177 = 1.022 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa}$

**Answer:** $P = 1.022 \times 10^5$ Pa at interface

## Barometric Formula for Compressible Fluids

For gases, density varies with pressure. Assuming ideal gas and isothermal atmosphere:

$P = \rho \frac{RT}{M}$

where $M$ is molar mass. Substituting into hydrostatic equation:
$\displaystyle \frac{dP}{dz} = -\rho g = -\frac{PM}{RT}g$

Separate variables:
$\displaystyle \frac{dP}{P} = -\frac{Mg}{RT}dz$

Integrate from $z = 0$ (where $P = P_0$) to height $z$:
$\displaystyle \ln\frac{P}{P_0} = -\frac{Mgz}{RT}$

$\displaystyle P(z) = P_0 e^{-Mgz/(RT)}$

This is the **barometric formula**. Define scale height:
$\displaystyle H = \frac{RT}{Mg}$

Then:
$\displaystyle P(z) = P_0 e^{-z/H}$

For Earth's atmosphere: $H \approx 8000$ m.

### Example 5: Atmospheric Pressure at Altitude

Using scale height $H = 8000$ m and $P_0 = 1.01 \times 10^5$ Pa, calculate pressure at: (a) $z = 2000$ m, (b) $z = 8000$ m.

**Solution:**

(a) At $z = 2000$ m:
$P = P_0 e^{-2000/8000} = 1.01 \times 10^5 \times e^{-0.25}$
$= 1.01 \times 10^5 \times 0.779 = 7.87 \times 10^4 \text{ Pa}$

About 78% of sea-level pressure.

(b) At $z = 8000$ m (one scale height):
$P = P_0 e^{-1} = 1.01 \times 10^5 \times 0.368 = 3.72 \times 10^4 \text{ Pa}$

About 37% of sea-level pressure—rarefied air!

**Answer:** (a) 78.7 kPa; (b) 37.2 kPa

### Example 6: Scale Height Derivation

For air at $T = 280$ K with $M = 29 \times 10^{-3}$ kg/mol, $R = 8.314$ J/(mol·K), and $g = 9.81$ m/s², calculate scale height.

**Solution:**

$\displaystyle H = \frac{RT}{Mg} = \frac{(8.314)(280)}{(29 \times 10^{-3})(9.81)}$
$\displaystyle = \frac{2328}{0.284} = 8190 \text{ m} \approx 8.2 \text{ km}$

**Answer:** $H \approx 8.2$ km

## Absolute, Gauge, and Atmospheric Pressure

**Absolute pressure** $P_{abs}$: Pressure relative to perfect vacuum (zero reference)

**Atmospheric pressure** $P_{atm}$: Local ambient air pressure (varies with weather/altitude)
- Standard: $P_{atm} = 1.01325 \times 10^5$ Pa = 1 atm = 760 mmHg

**Gauge pressure** $P_{gauge}$: Pressure relative to local atmospheric pressure
$P_{gauge} = P_{abs} - P_{atm}$

Many gauges read gauge pressure (tire pressure gauge shows pressure above atmospheric).

### Example 7: Gauge vs. Absolute Pressure

A tire gauge reads 32 psi. If atmospheric pressure is 14.7 psi, what is the absolute pressure inside the tire?

**Solution:**

$P_{abs} = P_{gauge} + P_{atm} = 32 + 14.7 = 46.7 \text{ psi}$

Convert to SI: $1 \text{ psi} = 6895$ Pa
$P_{abs} = 46.7 \times 6895 = 3.22 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa} = 322 \text{ kPa}$

**Answer:** $P_{abs} = 46.7$ psi = 322 kPa

## Hydrostatic Force on Surfaces

**Force on submerged surface**:
$\displaystyle F = \int_A P \, dA$

For plane surface at constant depth, pressure is uniform: $F = PA$

For varying depth, pressure varies: must integrate.

**Center of pressure**: Point where resultant force acts
$\displaystyle \mathbf{r}_{cp} = \frac{\int_A \mathbf{r} P \, dA}{\int_A P \, dA}$

For vertical plane surface submerged in liquid, center of pressure is below centroid because pressure increases with depth.

### Example 8: Force on Rectangular Plate

A rectangular plate (width $w = 2$ m, height $h = 3$ m) is submerged vertically in water with top edge at depth $d = 1$ m. Calculate total hydrostatic force.

**Solution:**

Pressure at depth $y$ below surface: $P(y) = P_0 + \rho g y$

For submerged plate, only gauge pressure contributes to net force:
$P_{gauge}(y) = \rho g y$

Consider horizontal strip at depth $y$ with thickness $dy$:
$dF = P_{gauge} \cdot dA = \rho g y \cdot (w \, dy)$

Integrate from $y = d$ to $y = d + h$:
$\displaystyle F = \int_d^{d+h} \rho g y w \, dy = \rho g w \int_d^{d+h} y \, dy$

$\displaystyle = \rho g w \left[\frac{y^2}{2}\right]_d^{d+h} = \rho g w \frac{(d+h)^2 - d^2}{2}$

$= \rho g w \frac{2dh + h^2}{2} = \rho g w h\left(d + \frac{h}{2}\right)$

This is pressure at centroid times area: $P_{centroid} \cdot A$ where centroid is at $y_c = d + h/2$:
$F = \rho g(d + h/2) \cdot (wh) = (1000)(9.81)(1 + 1.5)(2 \times 3)$
$= (1000)(9.81)(2.5)(6) = 147,150 \text{ N} \approx 147 \text{ kN}$

**Answer:** $F = 147$ kN

### Example 9: Center of Pressure

For the plate in Example 8, find the vertical location of the center of pressure below the surface.

**Solution:**

Moment about surface:
$\displaystyle M = \int_d^{d+h} y \cdot P_{gauge}(y) \, dA = \int_d^{d+h} y \cdot \rho g y \cdot w \, dy$

$\displaystyle = \rho g w \int_d^{d+h} y^2 \, dy = \rho g w \left[\frac{y^3}{3}\right]_d^{d+h}$

$\displaystyle = \rho g w \frac{(d+h)^3 - d^3}{3}$

Center of pressure depth:
$\displaystyle y_{cp} = \frac{M}{F} = \frac{\rho g w [(d+h)^3 - d^3]/3}{\rho g w h(d + h/2)}$

$\displaystyle = \frac{(d+h)^3 - d^3}{3h(d + h/2)}$

For $d = 1$, $h = 3$:
$\displaystyle y_{cp} = \frac{4^3 - 1^3}{3(3)(1 + 1.5)} = \frac{64 - 1}{9 \times 2.5} = \frac{63}{22.5} = 2.8 \text{ m}$

Center of pressure is at 2.8 m below surface, which is 0.3 m below the geometric centroid (at 2.5 m). Pressure increases with depth, so resultant acts deeper than centroid.

**Answer:** $y_{cp} = 2.8$ m below surface

### Example 10: Force on Curved Surface

A cylindrical gate (radius $R$, length $L$) holds water on one side. Calculate horizontal and vertical components of hydrostatic force.

**Solution:**

**Horizontal force**: Project curved surface onto vertical plane.
$F_h = \text{Force on vertical projection} = \rho g h_c A_{proj}$

where $h_c$ is centroid depth and $A_{proj} = 2RL$ (projection area).

**Vertical force**: Weight of water above curved surface.
$F_v = \rho g V$

where $V$ is volume of water above the gate.

For quarter-circle: $V = \frac{\pi R^2}{4} \times L$
$F_v = \rho g \frac{\pi R^2 L}{4}$

**Answer:** $F_h$ acts at center of pressure of projection; $F_v$ equals weight of fluid above surface

## Archimedes' Principle and Buoyancy

**Archimedes' Principle**: A body immersed in a fluid experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of displaced fluid.

$\displaystyle F_B = \rho_f g V_{disp}$

where $\rho_f$ is fluid density and $V_{disp}$ is displaced volume.

**Derivation**: Consider pressure forces on body surface. Horizontal components cancel by symmetry. Vertical components:
- Bottom surface (upward): $P_{bottom} A = (\rho g h_{bottom}) A$
- Top surface (downward): $-P_{top} A = -(\rho g h_{top}) A$

Net upward force:
$F_B = \rho g A(h_{bottom} - h_{top}) = \rho g V$

where $V = A(h_{bottom} - h_{top})$ is displaced volume.

### Example 11: Floating Body

A wooden block (density $\rho_w = 600$ kg/m³, volume $V = 0.1$ m³) floats in water ($\rho_f = 1000$ kg/m³). What fraction of volume is submerged?

**Solution:**

At equilibrium: Weight = Buoyant force
$\rho_w g V = \rho_f g V_{sub}$

$\displaystyle \frac{V_{sub}}{V} = \frac{\rho_w}{\rho_f} = \frac{600}{1000} = 0.60$

60% of volume is submerged; 40% above water.

**Answer:** 60% submerged

### Example 12: Submerged Body

A solid sphere (radius $R = 0.5$ m, density $\rho_s = 2500$ kg/m³) is fully submerged in water. Calculate the net force (weight minus buoyancy).

**Solution:**

Volume: $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 = \frac{4}{3}\pi (0.5)^3 = 0.524$ m³

Weight: $W = \rho_s g V = (2500)(9.81)(0.524) = 12,850$ N (downward)

Buoyancy: $F_B = \rho_f g V = (1000)(9.81)(0.524) = 5,140$ N (upward)

Net force: $F_{net} = W - F_B = 12,850 - 5,140 = 7,710$ N (downward)

Sphere sinks because $\rho_s > \rho_f$.

**Answer:** Net downward force = 7,710 N

### Example 13: Balloon Buoyancy

A helium balloon has volume $V = 100$ m³. Air density is $\rho_{air} = 1.2$ kg/m³; helium density is $\rho_{He} = 0.18$ kg/m³. Calculate net lift force (neglect balloon mass).

**Solution:**

Buoyant force: $F_B = \rho_{air} g V = (1.2)(9.81)(100) = 1,177$ N (upward)

Weight of helium: $W_{He} = \rho_{He} g V = (0.18)(9.81)(100) = 177$ N (downward)

Net lift: $F_{lift} = F_B - W_{He} = 1,177 - 177 = 1,000$ N

Can lift approximately 100 kg of payload (plus balloon mass).

**Answer:** Net lift = 1,000 N $\approx$ 100 kg

## Stability of Floating Bodies

**Metacentric height** $GM$ determines stability:
- $GM > 0$: Stable (righting moment restores equilibrium)
- $GM < 0$: Unstable (overturning moment)

**Metacenter** $M$: Point where buoyant force line intersects body centerline when tilted.

**Center of buoyancy** $B$: Centroid of displaced volume.

**Center of gravity** $G$: Centroid of body mass.

$GM = BM - BG$ where $BM$ is distance from $B$ to $M$.

### Example 14: Stability Analysis

A rectangular barge (width $w = 10$ m, height $h = 3$ m, draft $d = 1$ m) has center of gravity at $h_G = 1.5$ m above bottom. Determine if it's stable.

**Solution:**

Center of buoyancy: $h_B = d/2 = 0.5$ m (centroid of submerged rectangle)

For small tilt $\theta$, metacentric radius:
$\displaystyle BM = \frac{I}{V_{sub}}$

where $I$ is second moment of waterplane area about tilt axis.

For rectangle: $I = \frac{1}{12}Lw^3$ (per unit length $L$)

Submerged volume per length: $V_{sub}/L = wd$

$\displaystyle BM = \frac{w^3/12}{wd} = \frac{w^2}{12d} = \frac{10^2}{12(1)} = 8.33 \text{ m}$

$GM = BM - BG = BM - (h_G - h_B) = 8.33 - (1.5 - 0.5) = 7.33 \text{ m}$

Since $GM > 0$, barge is stable.

**Answer:** $GM = 7.33$ m (stable)

## Manometry

**Manometer**: Device for measuring pressure using fluid columns.

**U-tube manometer**: Connects to system; height difference $\Delta h$ in manometer fluid indicates pressure difference:
$\Delta P = \rho_{manometer} g \Delta h$

### Example 15: U-Tube Manometer

A U-tube contains mercury ($\rho = 13,600$ kg/m³). One end is open to atmosphere; the other connects to a gas tank. Mercury height difference is $\Delta h = 15$ cm with mercury higher on atmospheric side. Calculate gauge pressure in tank.

**Solution:**

$P_{gauge} = \rho g \Delta h = (13,600)(9.81)(0.15)$
$= 20,008 \text{ Pa} \approx 20 \text{ kPa}$

Tank pressure is 20 kPa above atmospheric (positive gauge pressure).

**Answer:** $P_{gauge} = 20$ kPa

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
Pascal's law: Pressure at a point in static fluid is same in all directions
Fluid cannot support shear stress at rest
Pressure is scalar (not directional)
Acts perpendicular to any surface orientation

**Problem 2: B**
$P = P_0 + \rho gh = 101 + (1000)(9.81)(20)$
$P = 101 + 196.2 = 297$ kPa $\approx 200$ kPa (gauge)
About 3 atm total pressure
Deep enough for significant pressure effects

**Problem 3: A**
$\displaystyle P(z) = P_0 e^{-z/H}$
$P(16) = P_0 e^{-16/8} = P_0 e^{-2}$
$e^{-2} = 0.135 \approx 0.14$
Pressure drops to ~14% at two scale heights
Corresponds to ~50,000 ft altitude

**Problem 4: D**
Floating equilibrium: $\rho_c V = \rho_w V_{sub}$
$\displaystyle \frac{V_{sub}}{V} = \frac{\rho_c}{\rho_w} = \frac{800}{1000} = 0.80$
80% submerged (20% above water)
Less dense than water, so floats

**Problem 5: C**
Pressure increases with depth
Lower portions experience greater pressure
Resultant force acts at higher-weighted location
Center of pressure below centroid for vertical surfaces
Distance increases with increasing depth range
