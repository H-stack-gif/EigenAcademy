# Article 6.1: Quantum Statistics and Indistinguishability

Classical statistical mechanics treats particles as distinguishable—we can, in principle, track individual particles and label them. But quantum mechanics reveals a profound truth: **identical particles are fundamentally indistinguishable**. Two electrons are not just "very similar"—they are absolutely identical, and quantum mechanics forbids any measurement that could distinguish them. This indistinguishability has dramatic consequences for statistical mechanics, leading to two completely different statistics depending on particle spin.

Particles with **half-integer spin** (electrons, protons, neutrons) are **fermions** and obey the **Pauli exclusion principle**—no two fermions can occupy the same quantum state. Their wavefunctions must be antisymmetric under particle exchange. Particles with **integer spin** (photons, helium-4 atoms, phonons) are **bosons** and can occupy the same state in unlimited numbers. Their wavefunctions must be symmetric under exchange. These symmetry requirements fundamentally alter the statistics, replacing the Boltzmann distribution with Fermi-Dirac (fermions) or Bose-Einstein (bosons) distributions.

This article develops quantum indistinguishability from first principles, introduces symmetrization and antisymmetrization of wavefunctions, establishes the connection between spin and statistics, explores occupation number representation and Fock space, introduces Slater determinants for fermions, and previews second quantization formalism. The mathematics involves permutation symmetry, determinants, combinatorics for indistinguishable particles, and the occupation number representation fundamental to quantum field theory.

## Identical Particles in Quantum Mechanics

In classical mechanics, particles have trajectories—we can track each particle's path and maintain labels "particle 1," "particle 2," etc.

**Quantum mechanics**: Particles described by wavefunctions $\psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2, ..., \mathbf{r}_N)$. For identical particles, **no measurement can distinguish which particle is which**.

**Physical consequence**: If we exchange two identical particles, the system must be physically indistinguishable. This means:

$$|\psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2, ...)|^2 = |\psi(\mathbf{r}_2, \mathbf{r}_1, ...)|^2$$

Therefore:

$$\psi(\mathbf{r}_2, \mathbf{r}_1, ...) = e^{i\phi} \psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2, ...)$$

for some phase $\phi$.

**Exchange twice** returns to original configuration:

$$e^{i\phi} \cdot e^{i\phi} = e^{2i\phi} = 1$$

Therefore: $e^{i\phi} = \pm 1$

**Two possibilities**:
1. $e^{i\phi} = +1$: **Symmetric** (bosons)
2. $e^{i\phi} = -1$: **Antisymmetric** (fermions)

## Symmetrization and Antisymmetrization

For two particles in single-particle states $\phi_a$ and $\phi_b$:

**Bosons** (symmetric):

$$\psi_+(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_1)\phi_b(\mathbf{r}_2) + \phi_a(\mathbf{r}_2)\phi_b(\mathbf{r}_1)]$$

If $a = b$ (same state):

$$\psi_+(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2) = \phi_a(\mathbf{r}_1)\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_2)$$

This is allowed—bosons can occupy same state.

**Fermions** (antisymmetric):

$$\psi_-(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_1)\phi_b(\mathbf{r}_2) - \phi_a(\mathbf{r}_2)\phi_b(\mathbf{r}_1)]$$

If $a = b$ (same state):

$$\psi_-(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_1)\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_2) - \phi_a(\mathbf{r}_2)\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_1)] = 0$$

**Pauli exclusion principle**: Two identical fermions cannot occupy the same quantum state!

## Fermions: Half-Integer Spin

**Definition**: Particles with spin $s = 1/2, 3/2, 5/2, ...$ (half-integer)

**Examples**:
- Electrons: $s = 1/2$
- Protons: $s = 1/2$
- Neutrons: $s = 1/2$
- Quarks: $s = 1/2$
- Helium-3 nuclei: $s = 1/2$ (net)

**Properties**:
- Antisymmetric wavefunctions under exchange
- Obey Pauli exclusion: maximum one fermion per quantum state
- Fermi-Dirac distribution
- Occupation numbers: $n_i = 0$ or $1$

**Physical consequences**:
- Atomic shell structure (exclusion builds periodic table)
- Metallic properties (Fermi seas in conductors)
- White dwarf stability (electron degeneracy pressure)
- Neutron star stability (neutron degeneracy pressure)

## Bosons: Integer Spin

**Definition**: Particles with spin $s = 0, 1, 2, ...$ (integer)

**Examples**:
- Photons: $s = 1$
- Helium-4 nuclei: $s = 0$ (net)
- Phonons: $s = 0$ (quasiparticles)
- W and Z bosons: $s = 1$
- Higgs boson: $s = 0$

**Properties**:
- Symmetric wavefunctions under exchange
- Can occupy same state in unlimited numbers
- Bose-Einstein distribution
- Occupation numbers: $n_i = 0, 1, 2, 3, ...$

**Physical consequences**:
- Lasers (stimulated emission, macroscopic photon occupation)
- Bose-Einstein condensation
- Superfluidity (helium-4)
- Enhanced low-energy states

## Occupation Numbers

**Occupation number representation**: Specify system by listing how many particles in each single-particle state.

State of system: $|n_1, n_2, n_3, ...\rangle$ where $n_i$ is number of particles in state $i$.

**Constraints**:
- Total particle number: $\sum_i n_i = N$
- Fermions: $n_i = 0$ or $1$ (exclusion)
- Bosons: $n_i = 0, 1, 2, 3, ...$ (no limit)

**Energy**: If single-particle states have energies $\epsilon_i$:

$$E = \sum_i n_i \epsilon_i$$

**Example**: Three particles, three states:

Fermions: $|1, 1, 1\rangle$, $|2, 1, 0\rangle$ (using labels for different states)
- Can't have $|2, 0, 0\rangle$ (violates exclusion)

Bosons: $|3, 0, 0\rangle$, $|2, 1, 0\rangle$, $|1, 1, 1\rangle$, etc.
- All configurations allowed

## Fock Space

**Fock space**: Hilbert space built from occupation number states.

For single-particle Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}_1$, the many-particle Fock space:

$$\mathcal{F} = \mathbb{C} \oplus \mathcal{H}_1 \oplus \mathcal{H}_2 \oplus \mathcal{H}_3 \oplus ...$$

where:
- $\mathbb{C}$: vacuum state (no particles)
- $\mathcal{H}_n$: $n$-particle sector (properly symmetrized/antisymmetrized)

**Basis states**: $|n_1, n_2, n_3, ...\rangle$ with varying total $N = \sum n_i$

**Operators** in Fock space:
- **Creation operator** $\hat{a}_i^\dagger$: adds particle to state $i$
- **Annihilation operator** $\hat{a}_i$: removes particle from state $i$

**Commutation relations**:

Bosons: $[\hat{a}_i, \hat{a}_j^\dagger] = \delta_{ij}$ (commutators)

Fermions: $\{\hat{a}_i, \hat{a}_j^\dagger\} = \delta_{ij}$ (anticommutators)

## Slater Determinants for Fermions

For $N$ fermions in single-particle states $\phi_1, \phi_2, ..., \phi_N$, the properly antisymmetrized wavefunction is a **Slater determinant**:

$$\psi(\mathbf{r}_1, ..., \mathbf{r}_N) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}} \begin{vmatrix}
\phi_1(\mathbf{r}_1) & \phi_2(\mathbf{r}_1) & \cdots & \phi_N(\mathbf{r}_1) \\
\phi_1(\mathbf{r}_2) & \phi_2(\mathbf{r}_2) & \cdots & \phi_N(\mathbf{r}_2) \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
\phi_1(\mathbf{r}_N) & \phi_2(\mathbf{r}_N) & \cdots & \phi_N(\mathbf{r}_N)
\end{vmatrix}$$

**Properties**:
- Automatically antisymmetric (determinant sign changes under row exchange)
- Vanishes if two columns identical (two particles in same state)
- Normalized if single-particle states orthonormal

**Example**: Two electrons in states $\phi_a$ and $\phi_b$:

$$\psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{vmatrix}
\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_1) & \phi_b(\mathbf{r}_1) \\
\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_2) & \phi_b(\mathbf{r}_2)
\end{vmatrix}$$

$$= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_1)\phi_b(\mathbf{r}_2) - \phi_b(\mathbf{r}_1)\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_2)]$$

This is exactly the antisymmetrized wavefunction.

## Second Quantization (Brief Introduction)

**First quantization**: Wavefunctions $\psi(\mathbf{r}_1, ..., \mathbf{r}_N)$ for fixed $N$

**Second quantization**: States in Fock space, operators create/annihilate particles

**Advantages**:
- Handles variable particle number naturally
- Symmetry built into formalism
- Compact notation
- Foundation for quantum field theory

**Number operator**: $\hat{n}_i = \hat{a}_i^\dagger \hat{a}_i$ (counts particles in state $i$)

**Hamiltonian** (non-interacting):

$$\hat{H} = \sum_i \epsilon_i \hat{a}_i^\dagger \hat{a}_i = \sum_i \epsilon_i \hat{n}_i$$

**Interaction terms**: Two-body interactions involve products like $\hat{a}_i^\dagger \hat{a}_j^\dagger \hat{a}_k \hat{a}_l$

## Example 1: Two-Fermion Wavefunction

Two electrons in states $|1s\rangle$ and $|2s\rangle$. Write antisymmetric spatial wavefunction.

**Solution:**

Using Slater determinant:

$$\psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{vmatrix}
\phi_{1s}(\mathbf{r}_1) & \phi_{2s}(\mathbf{r}_1) \\
\phi_{1s}(\mathbf{r}_2) & \phi_{2s}(\mathbf{r}_2)
\end{vmatrix}$$

Expanding:

$$= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\phi_{1s}(\mathbf{r}_1)\phi_{2s}(\mathbf{r}_2) - \phi_{2s}(\mathbf{r}_1)\phi_{1s}(\mathbf{r}_2)]$$

**Verification**: Exchange $\mathbf{r}_1 \leftrightarrow \mathbf{r}_2$:

$$\psi(\mathbf{r}_2, \mathbf{r}_1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\phi_{1s}(\mathbf{r}_2)\phi_{2s}(\mathbf{r}_1) - \phi_{2s}(\mathbf{r}_2)\phi_{1s}(\mathbf{r}_1)] = -\psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2)$$ ✓

Antisymmetric as required for fermions.

**Answer:** $\psi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\phi_{1s}(\mathbf{r}_1)\phi_{2s}(\mathbf{r}_2) - \phi_{2s}(\mathbf{r}_1)\phi_{1s}(\mathbf{r}_2)]$

## Example 2: Occupation Numbers for Three Particles

Three identical particles in three single-particle states. How many configurations for (a) fermions, (b) bosons?

**Solution:**

**(a) Fermions**: $n_i \in \{0, 1\}$, total $\sum n_i = 3$

Only one configuration: $|1, 1, 1\rangle$

Each particle must be in different state (exclusion).

**Count**: $\binom{3}{3} = 1$

**(b) Bosons**: $n_i \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, ...\}$, total $\sum n_i = 3$

Configurations:
1. $|3, 0, 0\rangle$
2. $|0, 3, 0\rangle$
3. $|0, 0, 3\rangle$
4. $|2, 1, 0\rangle$ (and permutations: 6 total)
5. $|1, 1, 1\rangle$

**Count**: Using stars and bars formula: $\binom{3 + 3 - 1}{3 - 1} = \binom{5}{2} = 10$

**Answer:** (a) 1 configuration (fermions), (b) 10 configurations (bosons)

## Example 3: Pauli Exclusion Verification

Show that two electrons cannot be in same state by proving antisymmetric wavefunction vanishes.

**Solution:**

Attempt to put both in state $\phi_a$:

$$\psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_1)\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_2) - \phi_a(\mathbf{r}_2)\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_1)]$$

$$= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_1)\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_2) - \phi_a(\mathbf{r}_1)\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_2)]$$

$$= 0$$

**Physical interpretation**: Antisymmetry requirement forces wavefunction to vanish identically—state is forbidden!

**Alternative**: Slater determinant with two identical columns:

$$\begin{vmatrix}
\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_1) & \phi_a(\mathbf{r}_1) \\
\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_2) & \phi_a(\mathbf{r}_2)
\end{vmatrix} = \phi_a(\mathbf{r}_1)\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_2) - \phi_a(\mathbf{r}_1)\phi_a(\mathbf{r}_2) = 0$$

Determinant with identical columns always vanishes.

**Answer:** $\psi = 0$ (two fermions cannot occupy same state)

## Example 4: Boson Enhancement

Three bosons distributed among three states with energies $0, \epsilon, 2\epsilon$. If ground state has 2 particles, list possible configurations.

**Solution:**

Constraint: $n_0 = 2$ (two in ground state), total $N = 3$

Remaining particle can be in state 1 or state 2:

**Configurations**:
1. $|2, 1, 0\rangle$: Energy $E = 2(0) + 1(\epsilon) + 0(2\epsilon) = \epsilon$
2. $|2, 0, 1\rangle$: Energy $E = 2(0) + 0(\epsilon) + 1(2\epsilon) = 2\epsilon$

For bosons, multiple particles in ground state is **favored** (bosonic enhancement)—this leads to Bose-Einstein condensation at low temperature.

**Answer:** Two configurations: $|2, 1, 0\rangle$ or $|2, 0, 1\rangle$

## Example 5: Spin-Statistics Connection

An unknown particle has spin $s = 3/2$. Is it a fermion or boson? Can two occupy same state?

**Solution:**

**Spin**: $s = 3/2$ is half-integer

**Spin-statistics theorem**: Half-integer spin → fermion

**Properties**:
- Antisymmetric wavefunction
- Obeys Pauli exclusion
- Cannot have two in same quantum state

**Physical examples**: Some baryons (e.g., $\Delta^+$ baryon)

**Answer**: Two particles cannot be in same state at most one per state

**Answer:** Fermion; two cannot occupy same state (Pauli exclusion)
