# Article 6.4: Blackbody Radiation and Photon Gas

At the turn of the 20th century, classical physics faced a crisis: the **ultraviolet catastrophe**. Classical theory predicted infinite energy for electromagnetic radiation in thermal equilibrium—absurd! **Max Planck** resolved this in 1900 by introducing energy quantization: $E = n\hbar\omega$. This revolutionary idea, combined with Bose-Einstein statistics for photons, yields the **Planck distribution** describing blackbody radiation perfectly.

A **photon gas**—electromagnetic radiation in thermal equilibrium—consists of massless bosons with two polarization states and chemical potential $\mu = 0$ (photon number not conserved). The resulting energy density has spectacular properties: the **Stefan-Boltzmann law** $u = \sigma T^4$ (energy density proportional to fourth power of temperature), **Wien's displacement law** $\lambda_{\max} T = $ constant (peak wavelength inversely proportional to temperature), and perfect agreement with experiments from radio waves to gamma rays.

This article derives the Planck distribution from photon statistics, calculates mode density in a cavity, integrates to obtain total energy density and Stefan-Boltzmann law, derives Wien's displacement law, discusses historical context of the ultraviolet catastrophe, and applies the formalism to stellar temperatures and cosmic microwave background radiation. The mathematics involves special integrals (particularly $\int_0^\infty x^3/(e^x - 1) dx = \pi^4/15$), mode counting in electromagnetic cavities, frequency-wavelength conversions, and evaluation of special functions.

## Photon Statistics

**Photons**: Massless bosons with spin $s = 1$, but only **two polarization states** (transverse to momentum).

**Key property**: Photon number **not conserved** (photons created/destroyed in atomic transitions).

**Chemical potential**: $\boxed{\mu = 0}$ (always, not just at critical temperature)

**Bose-Einstein distribution** for photons:

$$\boxed{\langle n_\omega \rangle = \frac{1}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1}}$$

where $\omega$ is angular frequency.

This is the **Planck distribution** (sometimes written with frequency $\nu = \omega/(2\pi)$).

**Properties**:
- $\langle n_\omega \rangle \to 1/(e^{\beta\hbar\omega}) \to 0$ as $\omega \to \infty$ (high frequency → few photons)
- $\langle n_\omega \rangle \to k_B T/(\hbar\omega)$ as $\omega \to 0$ (Rayleigh-Jeans classical limit)
- Average energy per mode: $\langle E_\omega \rangle = \hbar\omega \langle n_\omega \rangle$

## Mode Counting in a Cavity

Consider **cubic cavity** with side length $L$ (volume $V = L^3$).

**Electromagnetic modes**: Standing waves satisfying boundary conditions.

**Wavenumber** quantization:

$$\mathbf{k} = \frac{\pi}{L}(n_x, n_y, n_z), \quad n_i = 1, 2, 3, ...$$

**Dispersion relation** (photons): $\omega = ck$ where $c$ is speed of light.

**Density of states** in $k$-space:

$$\rho(\mathbf{k}) = \frac{V}{(2\pi)^3} \times 2$$

Factor of 2: two polarization states.

**Converting to frequency**:

Number of modes between $\omega$ and $\omega + d\omega$:

$$g(\omega) d\omega = \frac{V}{\pi^2 c^3} \omega^2 d\omega$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{g(\omega) = \frac{V\omega^2}{\pi^2 c^3}}$$

This is **mode density** (number of photon modes per unit frequency).

## Energy Density

**Energy per mode**: $\langle E_\omega \rangle = \hbar\omega \langle n_\omega \rangle$

**Total energy density** (energy per unit volume):

$$u(\omega, T) = \frac{\langle E_\omega \rangle}{V} \cdot g(\omega) = \frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2 c^3} \cdot \frac{1}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1}$$

$$\boxed{u(\omega, T) = \frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2 c^3(e^{\hbar\omega/(k_B T)} - 1)}}$$

This is **Planck's law** for energy density per unit frequency.

**Alternative** (per unit wavelength using $\omega = 2\pi c/\lambda$):

$$u(\lambda, T) = \frac{8\pi hc}{\lambda^5(e^{hc/(\lambda k_B T)} - 1)}$$

## Stefan-Boltzmann Law

**Total energy density** (integrate over all frequencies):

$$u = \int_0^\infty u(\omega, T) d\omega = \frac{\hbar}{\pi^2 c^3} \int_0^\infty \frac{\omega^3}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1} d\omega$$

**Dimensionless variable**: $x = \beta\hbar\omega$

$$u = \frac{(k_B T)^4}{\pi^2 \hbar^3 c^3} \int_0^\infty \frac{x^3}{e^x - 1} dx$$

**Special integral**:

$$\int_0^\infty \frac{x^3}{e^x - 1} dx = \frac{\pi^4}{15}$$

(Related to $\zeta(4) = \pi^4/90$)

Therefore:

$$\boxed{u = \frac{\pi^2 k_B^4}{15\hbar^3 c^3} T^4 = \sigma T^4}$$

**Stefan-Boltzmann constant**:

$$\sigma = \frac{\pi^2 k_B^4}{15\hbar^3 c^3} = 7.566 \times 10^{-16} \text{ J/(m}^3\text{·K}^4\text{)}$$

**Stefan-Boltzmann law**: Energy density $\propto T^4$

**Radiated power** per unit area (from cavity): $P = \frac{c}{4}u = \frac{\sigma c}{4}T^4 = \sigma_{SB} T^4$

where $\sigma_{SB} = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}$ W/(m²·K⁴) is Stefan-Boltzmann constant for radiance.

## Wien's Displacement Law

**Peak of $u(\lambda, T)$**: Find $\lambda_{\max}$ where $\frac{\partial u}{\partial \lambda} = 0$.

Setting up equation (using $u(\lambda, T) \propto \lambda^{-5}/(e^{hc/(\lambda k_B T)} - 1)$):

$$\frac{d}{d\lambda}\left[\frac{1}{\lambda^5(e^{hc/(\lambda k_B T)} - 1)}\right] = 0$$

**Result** (after differentiation and simplification):

$$\lambda_{\max} = \frac{hc}{4.965 k_B T} = \frac{b}{T}$$

where Wien's displacement constant:

$$\boxed{b = 2.898 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m·K}}$$

**Wien's displacement law**:

$$\boxed{\lambda_{\max} T = b}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Hotter objects radiate at shorter wavelengths (blue-shift with increasing temperature).

**Examples**:
- Sun surface ($T \approx 5800$ K): $\lambda_{\max} = 500$ nm (green, visible)
- Human body ($T = 310$ K): $\lambda_{\max} = 9.3$ μm (infrared)
- CMB ($T = 2.7$ K): $\lambda_{\max} = 1.1$ mm (microwave)

## Planck's Solution to Ultraviolet Catastrophe

**Classical prediction** (Rayleigh-Jeans law):

$$u_{\text{classical}}(\omega, T) = \frac{k_B T \omega^2}{\pi^2 c^3}$$

**Problem**: As $\omega \to \infty$, $u \to \infty$ → **infinite total energy**!

$$\int_0^\infty u_{\text{classical}} d\omega = \int_0^\infty \frac{k_B T}{\pi^2 c^3} \omega^2 d\omega = \infty$$

This is the **ultraviolet catastrophe**.

**Planck's solution** (1900): Quantize energy in multiples of $\hbar\omega$.

Quantum distribution:

$$u_{\text{quantum}}(\omega, T) = \frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2 c^3(e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1)}$$

**High frequency** ($\hbar\omega \gg k_B T$): Exponential suppression!

$$u \approx \frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2 c^3} e^{-\hbar\omega/(k_B T)} \to 0$$

Total energy **finite**:

$$\int_0^\infty u_{\text{quantum}} d\omega = \sigma T^4 < \infty$$ ✓

**Low frequency** ($\hbar\omega \ll k_B T$): Recovers classical limit:

$$u \approx \frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2 c^3} \cdot \frac{1}{\beta\hbar\omega} = \frac{k_B T \omega^2}{\pi^2 c^3}$$

(Rayleigh-Jeans law, valid at low frequencies)

## Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB)

**CMB**: Relic radiation from Big Bang, fills universe.

**Temperature**: $T_{CMB} = 2.725$ K (nearly perfect blackbody)

**Peak wavelength**:

$$\lambda_{\max} = \frac{b}{T} = \frac{2.898 \times 10^{-3}}{2.725} = 1.06 \text{ mm}$$

Microwave range (hence "cosmic microwave background").

**Energy density**:

$$u = \sigma T^4 = (7.566 \times 10^{-16})(2.725)^4 = 4.17 \times 10^{-14} \text{ J/m}^3$$

**Photon number density**:

$$n_\gamma \approx 411 \text{ photons/cm}^3$$

CMB photons vastly outnumber baryons ($\sim 10^9$ photons per baryon)!

**Anisotropies**: Tiny temperature fluctuations ($\Delta T/T \sim 10^{-5}$) reveal structure of early universe.

## Example 1: Sun Surface Temperature

Sun emits peak intensity at $\lambda_{\max} = 502$ nm (green). Estimate surface temperature.

**Solution:**

Using Wien's displacement law:

$$T = \frac{b}{\lambda_{\max}} = \frac{2.898 \times 10^{-3}}{502 \times 10^{-9}}$$

$$= 5.77 \times 10^3 \text{ K} = 5770 \text{ K}$$

**Actual value**: $T_{\odot} \approx 5778$ K (excellent agreement!)

**Physical interpretation**: Peak in green (our eyes evolved to detect this range—not coincidence!).

**Answer:** $T \approx 5770$ K

## Example 2: Energy Density Calculation

Calculate total blackbody energy density at $T = 3000$ K.

**Solution:**

$$u = \sigma T^4 = (7.566 \times 10^{-16})(3000)^4$$

$$= 7.566 \times 10^{-16} \times 8.1 \times 10^{13}$$

$$= 6.13 \times 10^{-2} \text{ J/m}^3 = 61.3 \text{ mJ/m}^3$$

**Comparison**: Room temperature ($T = 300$ K):

$$u = 7.566 \times 10^{-16} \times (300)^4 = 6.13 \times 10^{-6} \text{ J/m}^3$$

Energy density increases by factor $(3000/300)^4 = 10^4$!

**Answer:** $u = 61.3$ mJ/m³ at 3000 K

## Example 3: Human Body Radiation

Human body at $T = 310$ K. Find peak wavelength and total radiated power (assume area $A = 1.8$ m², emissivity $\epsilon = 0.98$).

**Solution:**

**Peak wavelength**:

$$\lambda_{\max} = \frac{b}{T} = \frac{2.898 \times 10^{-3}}{310} = 9.35 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m} = 9.35 \text{ μm}$$

Infrared (invisible to naked eye—need IR cameras).

**Radiated power**:

$$P = \epsilon \sigma_{SB} A T^4 = 0.98 \times (5.67 \times 10^{-8})(1.8)(310)^4$$

$$= 0.98 \times 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 1.8 \times 9.23 \times 10^9$$

$$= 923 \text{ W}$$

**Typical metabolic rate**: ~100 W at rest. Difference absorbed from environment or lost through evaporation/convection.

**Answer:** $\lambda_{\max} = 9.35$ μm, $P = 923$ W

## Example 4: Rayleigh-Jeans vs Planck

At $\omega = 10^{11}$ rad/s and $T = 300$ K, compare Rayleigh-Jeans and Planck predictions.

**Solution:**

**Check regime**: $\beta\hbar\omega = \frac{\hbar\omega}{k_B T} = \frac{(1.055 \times 10^{-34})(10^{11})}{(1.381 \times 10^{-23})(300)}$$

$$= \frac{1.055 \times 10^{-23}}{4.143 \times 10^{-21}} = 0.00255 \ll 1$$

Low frequency regime (microwave)—classical approximation valid.

**Rayleigh-Jeans**:

$$u_{RJ} = \frac{k_B T \omega^2}{\pi^2 c^3} = \frac{(1.381 \times 10^{-23})(300)(10^{11})^2}{\pi^2(3 \times 10^8)^3}$$

$$= 1.56 \times 10^{-11} \text{ J·s/m}^3$$

**Planck**:

$$u_P = \frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2 c^3(e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1)} \approx \frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2 c^3 \beta\hbar\omega} = \frac{k_B T \omega^2}{\pi^2 c^3}$$

Same as Rayleigh-Jeans in low-frequency limit! ✓

**Answer:** Both give $u \approx 1.56 \times 10^{-11}$ J·s/m³ (agreement in classical regime)

## Example 5: Photon Number Density

At $T = 2.7$ K (CMB), estimate photon number density.

**Solution:**

Average photon energy: $\langle E \rangle \approx 2.7 k_B T$ (numerical factor from integration)

$$\langle E \rangle \approx 2.7(1.381 \times 10^{-23})(2.7) = 1.00 \times 10^{-22} \text{ J}$$

Energy density: $u = \sigma T^4 = 7.566 \times 10^{-16} \times (2.7)^4 = 4.04 \times 10^{-14}$ J/m³

Photon density:

$$n_\gamma = \frac{u}{\langle E \rangle} = \frac{4.04 \times 10^{-14}}{1.00 \times 10^{-22}} = 4.04 \times 10^8 \text{ m}^{-3}$$

$$= 404 \text{ photons/cm}^3$$

**Literature value**: $\sim 411$ photons/cm³ (close!)

**Answer:** $n_\gamma \approx 410$ photons/cm³
