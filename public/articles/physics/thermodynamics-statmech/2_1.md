# Article 2.1: The First Law and Internal Energy

Energy cannot be created or destroyed—only transformed from one form to another. This is the **first law of thermodynamics**, the energy conservation principle applied to thermodynamic systems. When a gas expands, pushing a piston outward, it does work—mechanical energy flows from system to surroundings. When we heat the gas, thermal energy flows inward. The gas's internal energy—the sum of all molecular kinetic and potential energies—changes by the net energy transfer.

The first law connects three fundamental quantities: internal energy $U$ (a state function), heat $Q$ (energy transfer due to temperature difference), and work $W$ (energy transfer due to macroscopic forces). While internal energy depends only on the system's state, heat and work are path-dependent—they describe how energy crosses the boundary during a process. Understanding this distinction is essential: you can't ask "how much heat does this system contain?" because heat is not contained—it's transferred.

This article establishes the first law mathematically, explores internal energy as a state function, distinguishes heat and work as path functions, and develops the energy balance equations that govern closed thermodynamic systems. The mathematical framework centers on exact versus inexact differentials, cyclic integrals, and the fundamental property that distinguishes state functions from process variables.

## The First Law: Energy Conservation

The **first law of thermodynamics** states:

$$dU = \delta Q - \delta W$$

In words: the change in internal energy equals heat added minus work done by the system.

For finite processes:

$$\Delta U = Q - W$$

**Sign conventions**:
- $Q > 0$: heat flows into system (absorbed)
- $Q < 0$: heat flows out of system (rejected)
- $W > 0$: system does work on surroundings (expansion)
- $W < 0$: surroundings do work on system (compression)

Alternative form (with $W$ as work done on system):

$$\Delta U = Q + W$$

This form is common in chemistry. We'll use $\Delta U = Q - W$ (physics convention).

## Internal Energy as State Function

**Internal energy** $U$ is the total energy contained in the system: kinetic energy of molecular motion, potential energy of molecular interactions, energy in molecular vibrations and rotations, and electronic energy states.

Crucially, $U$ is a **state function**—it depends only on the system's current thermodynamic state, not the history. For any cyclic process returning to the initial state:

$$\oint dU = 0$$

This means:

$$\Delta U = U_f - U_i$$

is independent of path. Whether we go from state A to state B isothermally, adiabatically, or along any other path, $\Delta U$ is the same.

**Mathematical consequence**: Since $U$ is a state function, $dU$ is an **exact differential**. We can write:

$$dU = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_V dT + \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T dV$$

for a simple system with natural variables $(T, V)$.

## Heat and Work as Path Functions

In contrast to internal energy, **heat** $Q$ and **work** $W$ are **path functions**—they depend on the process connecting two states, not just the endpoints.

We denote them with inexact differentials:

$$\delta Q, \quad \delta W$$

(not $dQ$ or $dW$)

For cyclic processes:

$$\oint \delta Q \neq 0, \quad \oint \delta W \neq 0$$

However, their combination appears in the first law:

$$\oint dU = \oint (\delta Q - \delta W) = 0$$

which implies:

$$\oint \delta Q = \oint \delta W$$

For a cycle, total heat absorbed equals total work done.

**Physical interpretation**: Heat and work are not "in" the system—they describe energy crossing the boundary during processes. You can't say "the system contains 100 J of heat"—you can only say "100 J of heat was transferred to the system during this process."

## Energy Conservation in Closed Systems

For a **closed system** (no mass transfer), the first law is:

$$dU = \delta Q - P dV$$

where we've specified expansion work $\delta W = P dV$.

For processes with no volume change ($dV = 0$):

$$dU = \delta Q \quad \text{(constant volume)}$$

All energy transfer is heat. This defines heat capacity at constant volume:

$$C_V = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_V$$

For processes with no heat transfer ($\delta Q = 0$, adiabatic):

$$dU = -P dV \quad \text{(adiabatic)}$$

Work changes internal energy directly.

## Mechanical Equivalent of Heat

Historically, heat and work were thought to be fundamentally different phenomena. **Joule's experiments** (1840s) demonstrated they're equivalent—both are energy transfer mechanisms.

Joule showed that a given amount of mechanical work always produces the same temperature rise in a given mass of water, establishing:

$$1 \text{ cal} = 4.184 \text{ J}$$

This **mechanical equivalent of heat** unified the concepts. In SI units, we measure both heat and work in joules, eliminating any distinction in units.

## Energy Transfer Mechanisms

Energy crosses system boundaries through three mechanisms:

**1. Heat transfer**: Energy flow due to temperature difference
   - Conduction: molecular collisions
   - Convection: fluid motion
   - Radiation: electromagnetic waves

**2. Work transfer**: Energy flow due to organized forces
   - Expansion work: $P dV$
   - Shaft work: rotating machinery
   - Electrical work: $\mathcal{E} I dt$

**3. Mass transfer**: Energy carried by flowing matter (open systems)

For closed systems, only heat and work are relevant.

## Energy Balance Equations

For a closed system undergoing a process:

$$U_f - U_i = Q - W$$

Rate form:

$$\frac{dU}{dt} = \dot{Q} - \dot{W}$$

where $\dot{Q}$ is heat transfer rate (watts) and $\dot{W}$ is power (watts).

For **isolated systems** (no heat or work transfer):

$$\Delta U = 0$$

Internal energy is constant.

## Example 1: First Law for Ideal Gas Isothermal Expansion

An ideal gas undergoes isothermal expansion at $T = 400$ K from $V_i = 10$ L to $V_f = 25$ L. The gas does $W = 3.74$ kJ of work. Find:
(a) Change in internal energy
(b) Heat transferred

**Solution:**

(a) For **ideal gas**, internal energy depends only on temperature: $U = U(T)$ only (Joule's law).

Since temperature is constant:

$$\Delta U = 0$$

(b) From first law:

$$\Delta U = Q - W$$
$$0 = Q - 3.74 \text{ kJ}$$
$$Q = 3.74 \text{ kJ}$$

Heat absorbed equals work done—all the heat energy converts directly to mechanical work.

**Answer:** (a) $\Delta U = 0$, (b) $Q = 3.74$ kJ

## Example 2: Cyclic Process Energy Balance

A system undergoes a cycle: first absorbing $Q_1 = 500$ J and doing $W_1 = 300$ J, then rejecting $Q_2$ and having $W_2 = 100$ J of work done on it. Find $Q_2$.

**Solution:**

For a **cyclic process**, the system returns to its initial state, so:

$$\oint dU = \Delta U_{cycle} = 0$$

From first law:

$$\Delta U_{cycle} = (Q_1 + Q_2) - (W_1 + W_2) = 0$$

Note: $W_2 < 0$ since work is done on the system (compression).

$$(500 + Q_2) - (300 - 100) = 0$$
$$500 + Q_2 - 200 = 0$$
$$Q_2 = -300 \text{ J}$$

The negative sign means heat is rejected in the second process.

**Check**: Heat absorbed = $500 - 300 = 200$ J
Work done = $300 - 100 = 200$ J ✓

**Answer:** $Q_2 = -300$ J (heat rejected)

---

## Example 3: Adiabatic Compression Work

A gas is compressed adiabatically. Its internal energy increases by $\Delta U = 850$ J. Find the work done on the gas.

**Solution:**

**Adiabatic** means no heat transfer: $Q = 0$.

First law:

$$\Delta U = Q - W = 0 - W = -W$$

$$850 = -W$$
$$W = -850 \text{ J}$$

Negative work by the gas means positive work done on the gas: $+850$ J.

**Physical interpretation**: In adiabatic compression, all the work done on the gas increases its internal energy (temperature rises). No energy escapes as heat.

**Answer:** Work done on gas = $850$ J

---

## Example 4: State Function Path Independence

A gas goes from state A ($U_A = 200$ J) to state B ($U_B = 450$ J) along two paths:
- Path 1: $Q_1 = 300$ J absorbed
- Path 2: $Q_2 = 400$ J absorbed

Find work done along each path.

**Solution:**

Since internal energy is a **state function**:

$$\Delta U = U_B - U_A = 450 - 200 = 250 \text{ J}$$

This is the same for both paths.

**Path 1:**

$$\Delta U = Q_1 - W_1$$
$$250 = 300 - W_1$$
$$W_1 = 50 \text{ J}$$

**Path 2:**

$$\Delta U = Q_2 - W_2$$
$$250 = 400 - W_2$$
$$W_2 = 150 \text{ J}$$

Different paths have different $Q$ and $W$, but same $\Delta U$. Path 2 absorbs more heat and does more work, but the net change in internal energy is identical.

**Answer:** $W_1 = 50$ J, $W_2 = 150$ J

## Example 5: Calorimetry Application

A $50$ g aluminum block (specific heat $c = 0.900$ J/(g} \cdot text {K)) at $T_i = 100°\text{C}$ is dropped into $200$ g water (specific heat $c = 4.184$ J/(g} \cdot text {K)) at $T_i = 20°\text{C}$. Assuming no heat loss to surroundings, find equilibrium temperature.

**Solution:**

**Isolated system**: No heat exchange with surroundings, so total internal energy is conserved.

Heat lost by aluminum = Heat gained by water:

$$-Q_{Al} = Q_{water}$$

$$-m_{Al} c_{Al}(T_f - T_{Al,i}) = m_{water} c_{water}(T_f - T_{water,i})$$

$$-(50)(0.900)(T_f - 100) = (200)(4.184)(T_f - 20)$$

$$-45(T_f - 100) = 836.8(T_f - 20)$$

$$-45T_f + 4500 = 836.8T_f - 16736$$

$$4500 + 16736 = 836.8T_f + 45T_f$$

$$21236 = 881.8T_f$$

$$T_f = 24.1°\text{C}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Water has much higher heat capacity, so temperature changes little. Aluminum cools from 100°C to 24.1°C (loses 75.9 K), while water warms from 20°C to 24.1°C (gains only 4.1 K).

**Answer:** $T_f = 24.1°\text{C}$

The first law of thermodynamics states:
A) $\Delta U = Q + W$
B) $\Delta U = Q - W$ (with $W$ = work by system)
C) $\Delta S = Q/T$
D) $W = \int P dV$ always
E) Heat and work are state functions

### Problem 2
Which is a state function?
A) Heat $Q$
B) Work $W$
C) Internal energy $U$
D) Heat and work combined
E) None of these

### Problem 3
For a cyclic process, which is true?
A) $\Delta U = 0$ only
B) $Q = W$ only
C) Both $\Delta U = 0$ and $Q = W$
D) $Q = 0$
E) $W = 0$

### Problem 4
In an adiabatic process:
A) $\Delta U = 0$
B) $\Delta U = Q$
C) $\Delta U = -W$
D) $Q = W$
E) $T$ constant

### Problem 5
An ideal gas undergoes isothermal expansion doing $W = 500$ J. Heat transferred is:
A) $0$ J
B) $250$ J
C) $500$ J
D) $1000$ J
E) Cannot determine

---

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B** - First law: $\Delta U = Q - W$ where $W$ is work done by the system.

**Problem 2: C** - **Internal energy** $U$ is a state function. Heat and work are path functions.

**Problem 3: C** - For cycles: $\Delta U = 0$ (returns to initial state) and $Q = W$ (from first law).

**Problem 4: C** - Adiabatic: $Q = 0$, so $\Delta U = -W$.

**Problem 5: C** - Ideal gas isothermal: $\Delta U = 0$, so $Q = W = 500$ J.
