# Article 6.5: Debye and Einstein Models of Solids

How do solids store thermal energy? Classical physics predicts each atom contributes $3k_B$ to the heat capacity (Dulong-Petit law)—one $k_B$ for each spatial degree of freedom from equipartition. This works well at high temperature, but fails dramatically at low temperature where experimental measurements show $C_V \to 0$ as $T \to 0$. Quantum mechanics resolves this discrepancy through two complementary models treating atomic vibrations (phonons) as quantum harmonic oscillators.

**Einstein's model** (1907) treats all atoms as independent oscillators at a single frequency $\omega_E$. While capturing qualitative behavior—exponential decrease of $C_V$ at low temperature—it predicts too rapid a decrease. **Debye's model** (1912) accounts for the continuous spectrum of vibrational modes in a solid, treating low-frequency modes as sound waves with linear dispersion $\omega = v_s k$. This yields the famous **Debye $T^3$ law**: $C_V \propto T^3$ at low temperature, in excellent agreement with experiments.

This article develops Einstein's model with its characteristic temperature $\Theta_E$, calculates Einstein heat capacity, introduces Debye's continuous density of states with cutoff frequency $\omega_D$, derives Debye heat capacity and the $T^3$ law, explores high and low temperature limits, introduces Debye temperature $\Theta_D$, and compares both models to experimental data. The mathematics involves harmonic oscillator partition functions, phonon dispersion relations, Debye integrals requiring numerical evaluation, low and high temperature asymptotic expansions, and dimensional analysis.

## Einstein Model

**Assumption**: All $N$ atoms vibrate independently as **3D quantum harmonic oscillators** with same frequency $\omega_E$.

**Single oscillator**: Energy levels $E_n = \hbar\omega_E(n + 1/2)$

From Article 5.4, partition function:

$$z = \frac{e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_E/2}}{1 - e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_E}}$$

Average energy:

$$\langle E \rangle = \frac{\hbar\omega_E}{2} + \frac{\hbar\omega_E}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_E} - 1}$$

**Total energy** for $3N$ independent oscillators (3 dimensions × $N$ atoms):

$$U = 3N\left[\frac{\hbar\omega_E}{2} + \frac{\hbar\omega_E}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_E} - 1}\right]$$

## Einstein Temperature

Define **Einstein temperature**:

$$\boxed{\Theta_E = \frac{\hbar\omega_E}{k_B}}$$

Then:

$$U = 3N\hbar\omega_E\left[\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{e^{\Theta_E/T} - 1}\right]$$

**Physical meaning**: $\Theta_E$ is characteristic temperature for exciting vibrational modes.

**Typical values**: $\Theta_E \sim 100-300$ K for most solids.

## Einstein Heat Capacity

**Heat capacity**:

$$C_V = \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} = 3N\hbar\omega_E \frac{\partial}{\partial T}\left[\frac{1}{e^{\Theta_E/T} - 1}\right]$$

After differentiation:

$$\boxed{C_V = 3Nk_B\left(\frac{\Theta_E}{T}\right)^2 \frac{e^{\Theta_E/T}}{(e^{\Theta_E/T} - 1)^2}}$$

**Alternative form**: Define $x = \Theta_E/T$:

$$C_V = 3Nk_B f_E(x), \quad f_E(x) = x^2\frac{e^x}{(e^x - 1)^2}$$

## Einstein Model Limits

**High temperature** ($T \gg \Theta_E$, or $x \ll 1$):

$$e^x \approx 1 + x, \quad e^x - 1 \approx x$$

$$f_E(x) \approx x^2 \cdot \frac{1 + x}{x^2} = 1$$

$$\boxed{C_V \to 3Nk_B}$$ (Dulong-Petit law)

Classical limit: each atom contributes $3k_B$ ($k_B$ per degree of freedom × 3 dimensions).

**Low temperature** ($T \ll \Theta_E$, or $x \gg 1$):

$$f_E(x) \approx x^2 e^{-x}$$

$$\boxed{C_V \approx 3Nk_B\left(\frac{\Theta_E}{T}\right)^2 e^{-\Theta_E/T}}$$

Exponential suppression! At very low $T$, modes "frozen out."

**Problem with Einstein model**: Experimental $C_V \propto T^3$ at low $T$, not exponential.

## Debye Model

**Improvement**: Account for **continuous spectrum** of vibrational frequencies.

**Assumption**: Low-frequency modes are **sound waves** (phonons) with linear dispersion:

$$\omega = v_s k$$

where $v_s$ is sound speed.

**Density of states** (similar to photons, but with cutoff):

$$g(\omega) = \frac{3V\omega^2}{2\pi^2 v_s^3}, \quad 0 \leq \omega \leq \omega_D$$

Factor of 3: one longitudinal + two transverse polarizations.

**Debye cutoff frequency** $\omega_D$ determined by total mode count:

$$\int_0^{\omega_D} g(\omega) d\omega = 3N$$

$$\frac{3V}{2\pi^2 v_s^3} \cdot \frac{\omega_D^3}{3} = 3N$$

$$\boxed{\omega_D = v_s\left(\frac{6\pi^2 N}{V}\right)^{1/3}}$$

## Debye Temperature

Define **Debye temperature**:

$$\boxed{\Theta_D = \frac{\hbar\omega_D}{k_B}}$$

**Typical values**: $\Theta_D \sim 100-400$ K

Examples:
- Lead: $\Theta_D = 105$ K
- Aluminum: $\Theta_D = 428$ K
- Diamond: $\Theta_D = 2230$ K

## Debye Heat Capacity

**Total energy**:

$$U = \int_0^{\omega_D} g(\omega)\left[\frac{\hbar\omega}{2} + \frac{\hbar\omega}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1}\right] d\omega$$

Ignoring zero-point energy (doesn't contribute to $C_V$):

$$U = \frac{9N\hbar}{(\omega_D)^3} \int_0^{\omega_D} \frac{\omega^4}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1} d\omega$$

**Dimensionless variable**: $x = \beta\hbar\omega = \hbar\omega/(k_B T)$

$$U = 9Nk_B T\left(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\right)^3 \int_0^{\Theta_D/T} \frac{x^4}{e^x - 1} dx$$

**Debye heat capacity**:

$$\boxed{C_V = 9Nk_B\left(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\right)^3 \int_0^{\Theta_D/T} \frac{x^4 e^x}{(e^x - 1)^2} dx}$$

This integral requires **numerical evaluation** in general (no closed form).

Define **Debye function**:

$$D(y) = \frac{3}{y^3}\int_0^y \frac{x^4 e^x}{(e^x - 1)^2} dx$$

Then: $C_V = 3Nk_B D(\Theta_D/T)$

## Low Temperature: Debye $T^3$ Law

**Low temperature** ($T \ll \Theta_D$): Upper limit $\Theta_D/T \to \infty$

$$\int_0^\infty \frac{x^4 e^x}{(e^x - 1)^2} dx = \frac{4\pi^4}{15}$$

$$C_V \approx 9Nk_B\left(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\right)^3 \cdot \frac{4\pi^4}{15}$$

$$\boxed{C_V = \frac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk_B\left(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\right)^3}$$

**Debye $T^3$ law**: $C_V \propto T^3$ at low temperature!

**Physical interpretation**: At low $T$, only long-wavelength (low-frequency) phonons excited. These have continuous spectrum (unlike Einstein's single frequency), leading to $T^3$ rather than exponential behavior.

**Experimental verification**: Excellent agreement across many materials at low temperature.

## High Temperature Limit

**High temperature** ($T \gg \Theta_D$): Integral approaches constant.

Using approximation for large $T$:

$$\int_0^{\Theta_D/T} \frac{x^4 e^x}{(e^x - 1)^2} dx \approx \frac{(\Theta_D/T)^3}{3}$$

$$C_V \approx 9Nk_B \cdot \frac{1}{3} = 3Nk_B$$

**Dulong-Petit law** recovered! Both Einstein and Debye models give classical limit at high $T$.

## Comparison: Einstein vs Debye

**Einstein model**:
- Simple (single frequency)
- Qualitatively correct
- Predicts exponential decrease at low $T$ (too rapid)
- Good for optical phonons (high frequency)

**Debye model**:
- More realistic (continuous spectrum)
- Correct low-temperature behavior ($T^3$ law)
- Excellent agreement with experiments
- Good for acoustic phonons (low frequency)

**Reality**: Actual phonon spectrum is complex—neither pure Einstein nor pure Debye. But Debye model captures essential physics at low $T$.

## Example 1: Einstein Heat Capacity

Aluminum: $\Theta_E = 240$ K. Calculate $C_V$ at $T = 100$ K for 1 mole.

**Solution:**

$$x = \frac{\Theta_E}{T} = \frac{240}{100} = 2.4$$

$$f_E(x) = x^2\frac{e^x}{(e^x - 1)^2} = (2.4)^2\frac{e^{2.4}}{(e^{2.4} - 1)^2}$$

$$= 5.76 \cdot \frac{11.02}{(11.02 - 1)^2} = 5.76 \cdot \frac{11.02}{100.4} = 0.632$$

$$C_V = 3Nk_B f_E(x) = 3(6.02 \times 10^{23})(1.381 \times 10^{-23})(0.632)$$

$$= 3(8.314)(0.632) = 15.8 \text{ J/(mol·K)}$$

**Classical value**: $3R = 24.9$ J/(mol·K)

Quantum value is $\sim 63\%$ of classical.

**Answer:** $C_V = 15.8$ J/(mol·K)

## Example 2: Debye Temperature from Cutoff

Solid with sound speed $v_s = 5000$ m/s, atomic density $n = 5 \times 10^{28}$ m⁻³. Find $\Theta_D$.

**Solution:**

$$\omega_D = v_s(6\pi^2 n)^{1/3}$$

$$= 5000(6\pi^2 \times 5 \times 10^{28})^{1/3}$$

$$= 5000(2.96 \times 10^{30})^{1/3} = 5000 \times 1.44 \times 10^{10}$$

$$= 7.2 \times 10^{13} \text{ rad/s}$$

$$\Theta_D = \frac{\hbar\omega_D}{k_B} = \frac{(1.055 \times 10^{-34})(7.2 \times 10^{13})}{1.381 \times 10^{-23}}$$

$$= 550 \text{ K}$$

**Answer:** $\Theta_D = 550$ K

## Example 3: Low-Temperature Heat Capacity

Diamond: $\Theta_D = 2230$ K. Calculate $C_V$ at $T = 10$ K for 1 mole.

**Solution:**

Low temperature: $T \ll \Theta_D$ (10 K $\ll$ 2230 K)

Use Debye $T^3$ law:

$$C_V = \frac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk_B\left(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\right)^3$$

$$= \frac{12\pi^4}{5}(6.02 \times 10^{23})(1.381 \times 10^{-23})\left(\frac{10}{2230}\right)^3$$

$$= \frac{12\pi^4}{5}(8.314)\left(\frac{1}{223}\right)^3$$

$$= 233.76 \times 8.314 \times 9.02 \times 10^{-8}$$

$$= 0.175 \text{ J/(mol·K)}$$

Very small! Diamond has high Debye temperature (stiff lattice), so modes frozen out at 10 K.

**Answer:** $C_V = 0.175$ J/(mol·K)

## Example 4: Comparing Models

At $T = \Theta/2$ (where $\Theta$ is characteristic temperature), estimate ratio $C_V/(3Nk_B)$ for Einstein model.

**Solution:**

$$x = \frac{\Theta_E}{T} = 2$$

$$f_E(2) = (2)^2\frac{e^2}{(e^2 - 1)^2} = 4 \cdot \frac{7.389}{(7.389 - 1)^2}$$

$$= 4 \cdot \frac{7.389}{40.82} = 0.724$$

$$\frac{C_V}{3Nk_B} = 0.724 = 72.4\%$$

At half the Einstein temperature, heat capacity is about 72% of classical value.

**Physical interpretation**: Significant quantum suppression even at $T = \Theta_E/2$.

**Answer:** $C_V/(3Nk_B) = 0.724$ (72.4% of classical)

## Example 5: Dulong-Petit Verification

Show that Debye model gives Dulong-Petit law at high temperature.

**Solution:**

High temperature: $T \gg \Theta_D$, so $\Theta_D/T \to 0$

For small $y$:

$$\int_0^y \frac{x^4 e^x}{(e^x - 1)^2} dx \approx \int_0^y x^2 dx = \frac{y^3}{3}$$

(using $e^x \approx 1 + x$ for small $x$)

$$C_V \approx 9Nk_B\left(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\right)^3 \cdot \frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{\Theta_D}{T}\right)^3 = 3Nk_B$$

**Dulong-Petit law** confirmed! ✓

**Physical interpretation**: At high temperature, all modes fully excited classically—quantum effects negligible.

**Answer:** $C_V \to 3Nk_B$ at high $T$ (Dulong-Petit law)
