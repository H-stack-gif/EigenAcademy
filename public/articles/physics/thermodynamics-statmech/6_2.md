# Article 6.2: Fermi-Dirac Statistics

Fermions—electrons in metals, quarks in nuclei, helium-3 atoms at low temperature—obey fundamentally different statistics than classical particles. The **Pauli exclusion principle** prevents multiple fermions from occupying the same quantum state, leading to the **Fermi-Dirac distribution**. At zero temperature, fermions fill states from lowest energy upward until all $N$ particles are accommodated—the highest occupied state defines the **Fermi energy** $E_F$. This creates a sharp boundary in momentum space called the **Fermi surface**.

At finite temperature, thermal excitations blur the Fermi surface slightly—particles near $E_F$ can be thermally promoted to higher states. The characteristic temperature scale is the **Fermi temperature** $T_F = E_F/k_B$, often enormous for electrons in metals ($T_F \sim 10^4$ K). Below $T_F$, the system is **degenerate**—quantum statistics dominates over thermal effects. The electronic heat capacity becomes linear in temperature, $C_V \propto T$, rather than constant as classical theory predicts.

This article derives the Fermi-Dirac distribution from grand canonical ensemble, explores its properties at zero and finite temperature, introduces Fermi energy and Fermi temperature, calculates density of states for free particles, examines Fermi sphere geometry, derives electronic heat capacity using Sommerfeld expansion, and applies the formalism to metals and degenerate matter. The mathematics involves integrals with Fermi functions, density of states calculations, low-temperature expansions, and asymptotic analysis.

## Fermi-Dirac Distribution

In **grand canonical ensemble** for fermions, average occupation of single-particle state with energy $\epsilon_i$:

$$\boxed{\langle n_i \rangle = f_{FD}(\epsilon_i) = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)} + 1}}$$

where $\beta = 1/(k_B T)$ and $\mu$ is chemical potential.

This is the **Fermi-Dirac distribution** (or Fermi function).

**Properties**:
- $0 \leq f_{FD} \leq 1$ always (consistent with exclusion: $n_i \in \{0,1\}$)
- At $\epsilon = \mu$: $f_{FD} = 1/2$ (half-occupied)
- Low energy ($\epsilon \ll \mu$): $f_{FD} \to 1$ (fully occupied)
- High energy ($\epsilon \gg \mu$): $f_{FD} \to e^{-\beta(\epsilon - \mu)}$ (classical limit)

**Derivation**: Grand canonical for single state:

$$\mathcal{Z}_i = \sum_{n=0}^1 e^{-\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)n} = 1 + e^{-\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)}$$

$$\langle n_i \rangle = \frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\partial \ln \mathcal{Z}_i}{\partial \mu} = \frac{e^{-\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)}}{1 + e^{-\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)}} = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)} + 1}$$

## Chemical Potential and Fermi Energy

At **zero temperature** ($T = 0$), Fermi function becomes step function:

$$f_{FD}(\epsilon, T=0) = \begin{cases}
1 & \epsilon < \mu(0) \\
0 & \epsilon > \mu(0)
\end{cases}$$

The chemical potential at $T = 0$ is called **Fermi energy**:

$$\boxed{E_F = \mu(T = 0)}$$

**Physical meaning**: Highest occupied state at $T = 0$. All states below $E_F$ filled, all above empty.

**Total particle number**:

$$N = \int_0^\infty g(\epsilon) f_{FD}(\epsilon) \, d\epsilon$$

where $g(\epsilon)$ is **density of states** (number of states per unit energy).

At $T = 0$:

$$N = \int_0^{E_F} g(\epsilon) \, d\epsilon$$

This determines $E_F$ for given $N$.

## Fermi Temperature

Define **Fermi temperature**:

$$\boxed{T_F = \frac{E_F}{k_B}}$$

This is the characteristic temperature scale for quantum degeneracy.

**Regimes**:
- $T \ll T_F$: **Degenerate** (quantum statistics dominates)
- $T \gg T_F$: **Non-degenerate** (classical statistics adequate)

**Examples**:

**Electrons in copper**: $E_F \approx 7$ eV $\implies T_F \approx 80,000$ K

Room temperature $T = 300$ K $\ll T_F$ → highly degenerate!

**Helium-3** (liquid): $T_F \approx 5$ K

Below $T_F$, helium-3 exhibits quantum behavior (superfluidity).

## Density of States

For **free particles** in 3D box with volume $V$:

**Energy**: $\epsilon = \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2m}$ where $k = |\mathbf{k}|$

**Density of states** (including spin degeneracy $g_s = 2s + 1$):

$$\boxed{g(\epsilon) = \frac{g_s V}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2} \epsilon^{1/2}}$$

For spin-1/2 fermions ($g_s = 2$):

$$g(\epsilon) = \frac{V}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2} \sqrt{\epsilon}$$

**Derivation**: Number of states in momentum space shell $k$ to $k + dk$:

$$dN = \frac{V}{(2\pi)^3} \cdot 4\pi k^2 dk \cdot g_s$$

Convert to energy using $\epsilon = \hbar^2 k^2/(2m)$:

$$k = \sqrt{\frac{2m\epsilon}{\hbar^2}}, \quad dk = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\hbar^2\epsilon}} d\epsilon$$

$$g(\epsilon) d\epsilon = \frac{V g_s}{2\pi^2} k^2 dk = \frac{V g_s}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2} \epsilon^{1/2} d\epsilon$$

## Fermi Sphere and Fermi Surface

In momentum space, at $T = 0$, all states with $k < k_F$ are occupied, where:

$$E_F = \frac{\hbar^2 k_F^2}{2m}$$

$$\boxed{k_F = \sqrt{\frac{2mE_F}{\hbar^2}}}$$

This defines the **Fermi sphere**—sphere of radius $k_F$ in $k$-space.

**Fermi surface**: Boundary of Fermi sphere ($|\mathbf{k}| = k_F$)

**Total particle number**:

$$N = \int_0^{E_F} g(\epsilon) d\epsilon = \frac{V g_s}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2} \int_0^{E_F} \epsilon^{1/2} d\epsilon$$

$$= \frac{V g_s}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2} \cdot \frac{2}{3}E_F^{3/2}$$

Solving for $E_F$:

$$\boxed{E_F = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\frac{3\pi^2 N}{g_s V}\right)^{2/3}}$$

For electrons ($g_s = 2$):

$$E_F = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(3\pi^2 n)^{2/3}$$

where $n = N/V$ is particle density.

## Electronic Heat Capacity

Total energy at temperature $T$:

$$U = \int_0^\infty \epsilon \cdot g(\epsilon) f_{FD}(\epsilon) \, d\epsilon$$

Heat capacity:

$$C_V = \frac{\partial U}{\partial T}$$

**Sommerfeld expansion** (for $T \ll T_F$):

$$\int_0^\infty h(\epsilon) f_{FD}(\epsilon) d\epsilon \approx \int_0^{E_F} h(\epsilon) d\epsilon + \frac{\pi^2}{6}(k_B T)^2 h'(E_F) + ...$$

Applying to energy:

$$U \approx U_0 + \frac{\pi^2}{6}(k_B T)^2 g(E_F)$$

where $U_0 = \int_0^{E_F} \epsilon g(\epsilon) d\epsilon$ is zero-temperature energy.

**Heat capacity**:

$$\boxed{C_V = \frac{\pi^2}{3} k_B^2 T \cdot g(E_F)}$$

For free fermions with $g(E_F) = \frac{3N}{2E_F}$:

$$C_V = \frac{\pi^2}{2} Nk_B \frac{T}{T_F}$$

**Key result**: $C_V \propto T$ (linear in temperature)!

**Classical prediction**: $C_V = \frac{3}{2}Nk_B$ (constant, independent of $T$)

**Ratio**:

$$\frac{C_V^{\text{quantum}}}{C_V^{\text{classical}}} = \frac{\pi^2}{3}\frac{T}{T_F} \ll 1$$ for $T \ll T_F$

At room temperature for copper:

$$\frac{C_V}{C_V^{\text{classical}}} \approx \frac{\pi^2}{3} \cdot \frac{300}{80000} \approx 0.012 = 1.2\%$$

Only electrons near Fermi surface contribute thermally!

## Degenerate Fermi Gas

**Degenerate gas**: System with $T \ll T_F$

**Characteristics**:
- Most states below $E_F$ remain filled
- Only small fraction $\sim T/T_F$ near Fermi surface thermally excited
- Pressure largely independent of temperature (degeneracy pressure)
- Quantum effects dominate

**Pressure** at $T = 0$:

$$P = \frac{2}{3}\frac{U_0}{V} = \frac{2}{5}nE_F$$

For non-relativistic fermions:

$$P = \frac{\hbar^2}{5m}(3\pi^2)^{2/3} n^{5/3}$$

**Applications**:
- White dwarf stars (electron degeneracy)
- Neutron stars (neutron degeneracy)
- Metals at room temperature

## Example 1: Fermi Energy of Copper

Copper: one conduction electron per atom, density $8.96$ g/cm³, atomic mass $63.5$ g/mol. Find $E_F$.

**Solution:**

**Electron density**:

$$n = \frac{\rho N_A}{M} = \frac{(8.96 \times 10^3)(6.02 \times 10^{23})}{63.5 \times 10^{-3}}$$

$$= 8.49 \times 10^{28} \text{ m}^{-3}$$

**Fermi energy** (with $g_s = 2$):

$$E_F = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(3\pi^2 n)^{2/3}$$

$$= \frac{(1.055 \times 10^{-34})^2}{2(9.109 \times 10^{-31})}(3\pi^2 \times 8.49 \times 10^{28})^{2/3}$$

$$= 1.13 \times 10^{-18} \text{ J} = 7.05 \text{ eV}$$

**Fermi temperature**:

$$T_F = \frac{E_F}{k_B} = \frac{7.05 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19}}{1.381 \times 10^{-23}} = 81,800 \text{ K}$$

**Answer:** $E_F = 7.05$ eV, $T_F = 81,800$ K

## Example 2: Fermi Function at Different Temperatures

Calculate $f_{FD}(\epsilon)$ at $\epsilon = E_F + k_B T$ for temperatures $T = 0.1T_F$ and $T = T_F$.

**Solution:**

At $\epsilon = E_F + k_B T$ and $\mu \approx E_F$:

$$f_{FD} = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(E_F + k_B T - E_F)} + 1} = \frac{1}{e^1 + 1} = \frac{1}{e + 1} = 0.269$$

This is **independent** of whether $T = 0.1T_F$ or $T = T_F$ (as long as $\epsilon - \mu = k_B T$).

**At exactly** $\epsilon = E_F$ (assuming $\mu = E_F$):

$$f_{FD}(E_F) = \frac{1}{e^0 + 1} = \frac{1}{2}$$

Always half-occupied at Fermi level!

**Physical interpretation**: States above $E_F$ by $\sim k_B T$ have $\sim 27\%$ probability of occupation.

**Answer:** $f_{FD} = 0.269$ (independent of specific $T$ value when $\epsilon - \mu = k_B T$)

## Example 3: Electronic Heat Capacity of Silver

Silver at $T = 300$ K: $E_F = 5.5$ eV, $N = 1$ mole electrons. Calculate $C_V$.

**Solution:**

$$T_F = \frac{E_F}{k_B} = \frac{5.5 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19}}{1.381 \times 10^{-23}} = 63,800 \text{ K}$$

**Heat capacity**:

$$C_V = \frac{\pi^2}{2} Nk_B \frac{T}{T_F}$$

$$= \frac{\pi^2}{2}(6.02 \times 10^{23})(1.381 \times 10^{-23})\frac{300}{63800}$$

$$= 4.935(8.314)\frac{300}{63800} = 0.193 \text{ J/K}$$

**Classical prediction**:

$$C_V^{\text{classical}} = \frac{3}{2}Nk_B = \frac{3}{2}(8.314) = 12.5 \text{ J/K}$$

**Ratio**:

$$\frac{C_V^{\text{quantum}}}{C_V^{\text{classical}}} = \frac{0.193}{12.5} = 0.0154 = 1.54\%$$

Only $\sim 1.5\%$ of classical value!

**Answer:** $C_V = 0.193$ J/K (much less than classical)

## Example 4: Density of States at Fermi Energy

For $N = 10^{23}$ electrons in $V = 10^{-5}$ m³ with $E_F = 5$ eV, find $g(E_F)$.

**Solution:**

**Relation**:

$$g(E_F) = \frac{3N}{2E_F}$$

$$= \frac{3(10^{23})}{2(5 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19})} = \frac{3 \times 10^{23}}{1.602 \times 10^{-18}}$$

$$= 1.87 \times 10^{41} \text{ states/J}$$

Or per eV:

$$g(E_F) = 1.87 \times 10^{41} \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19} = 3.0 \times 10^{22} \text{ states/eV}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Enormous density of states near Fermi level—many available states for thermal excitations.

**Answer:** $g(E_F) = 3.0 \times 10^{22}$ states/eV

## Example 5: Fermi Momentum

Electron gas with density $n = 10^{29}$ m⁻³. Find Fermi momentum $p_F = \hbar k_F$.

**Solution:**

$$k_F = (3\pi^2 n)^{1/3} = (3\pi^2 \times 10^{29})^{1/3}$$

$$= (9.42 \times 10^{29})^{1/3} = 9.80 \times 10^9 \text{ m}^{-1}$$

**Fermi momentum**:

$$p_F = \hbar k_F = (1.055 \times 10^{-34})(9.80 \times 10^9)$$

$$= 1.03 \times 10^{-24} \text{ kg·m/s}$$

**Fermi velocity**:

$$v_F = \frac{p_F}{m} = \frac{1.03 \times 10^{-24}}{9.109 \times 10^{-31}} = 1.13 \times 10^6 \text{ m/s}$$

About $0.4\%$ speed of light—non-relativistic.

**Answer:** $p_F = 1.03 \times 10^{-24}$ kg·m/s, $v_F = 1.13 \times 10^6$ m/s

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C** - Fermi-Dirac: $0 \leq f_{FD} \leq 1$, maximum is **1** (fully occupied).

**Problem 2: B** - At $T = 0$: Fermi function is **step function** at $E_F$.

**Problem 3: B** - Degenerate Fermi gas: $C_V \propto T$ (linear, Sommerfeld result).

**Problem 4: A** - Free particles: $E_F \propto n^{2/3}$ (from density of states).

**Problem 5: C** - Copper: $T_F \sim 80,000$ K $\gg$ 300 K, so **$T \ll T_F$ (degenerate)**.
