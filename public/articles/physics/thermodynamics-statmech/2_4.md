# Article 2.4: Real Gases and Joule-Thomson Effect

Ideal gases provide elegant theoretical results, but real gases deviate from ideality. These deviations become pronounced at high pressures (molecules close together) and low temperatures (intermolecular forces significant). For real gases, internal energy depends on both temperature and volume: $U = U(T, V)$, not just temperature. This volume-dependence reflects energy stored in intermolecular interactions—attractive forces at moderate distances, repulsive forces at short distances.

Two famous experiments explore real gas behavior: **Joule's free expansion** measuring how temperature changes with volume at constant internal energy, and the **Joule-Thomson expansion** measuring temperature change during throttling at constant enthalpy. The Joule-Thomson effect is particularly important—it's the basis for gas liquefaction technology, enabling production of liquid nitrogen, oxygen, and helium used in medicine, research, and industry.

This article develops the mathematical framework for real gas internal energy, defines the Joule and Joule-Thomson coefficients, explores inversion temperatures where gases switch between cooling and heating, examines van der Waals internal energy, and discusses practical applications in cryogenics and gas processing.

## Internal Energy for Real Gases: $U = U(T, V)$

For **ideal gas**, internal energy depends only on temperature:

$$\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T = 0$$

For **real gases**, $U$ depends on both $T$ and $V$:

$$dU = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_V dT + \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T dV = C_V dT + \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T dV$$

The term $(\partial U/\partial V)_T$ represents energy change due to changing molecular spacing—work against intermolecular forces.

**Physical interpretation**:
- If $(\partial U/\partial V)_T > 0$: expansion increases $U$ (molecules attracting—energy increases as they separate)
- If $(\partial U/\partial V)_T < 0$: expansion decreases $U$ (molecules repelling—energy decreases as they separate)

For most real gases at moderate conditions, attractions dominate: $(\partial U/\partial V)_T > 0$.

## Joule Coefficient

The **Joule coefficient** measures temperature change during free expansion at constant internal energy:

$$\mu_J = \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\right)_U$$

From thermodynamic relations (using chain rule):

$$\mu_J = -\frac{1}{C_V}\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T$$

**For ideal gas**: $(\partial U/\partial V)_T = 0 \Rightarrow \mu_J = 0$ (temperature doesn't change during free expansion).

**For real gas with attractions**: $(\partial U/\partial V)_T > 0 \Rightarrow \mu_J < 0$ (temperature drops during free expansion as molecules separate against attractive forces).

**Joule's experiment** (1845): measured $\mu_J$ by free expansion. Results showed $\mu_J \approx 0$ for most gases at room temperature, suggesting near-ideal behavior. More sensitive experiments revealed small but non-zero effects.

## Joule-Thomson Coefficient

The **Joule-Thomson coefficient** measures temperature change during throttling at constant enthalpy:

$$\mu_{JT} = \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_H$$

**Throttling process**: Gas flows through porous plug or valve from high to low pressure. Process is **isenthalpic** ($\Delta H = 0$) because it's adiabatic and does no external work.

Using implicit differentiation and Maxwell relations:

$$\mu_{JT} = -\frac{1}{C_P}\left[\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial P}\right)_T\right] = \frac{1}{C_P}\left[T\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P - V\right]$$

For **ideal gas**: $V = nRT/P$, so:

$$\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P = \frac{nR}{P} = \frac{V}{T}$$

Thus: $\mu_{JT} = \frac{1}{C_P}[T \cdot V/T - V] = 0$.

For **real gases**, $\mu_{JT} \neq 0$:
- $\mu_{JT} > 0$: cooling upon expansion (most gases below inversion temperature)
- $\mu_{JT} < 0$: heating upon expansion (most gases above inversion temperature)

## Inversion Temperature

The **inversion temperature** $T_i$ is where $\mu_{JT}$ changes sign:

$$\mu_{JT}(T_i) = 0$$

At this temperature:

$$T_i\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P = V$$

**For van der Waals gas**, the inversion temperature is approximately:

$$T_i = \frac{2a}{Rb}$$

where $a$ and $b$ are van der Waals parameters.

**Physical significance**:
- Below $T_i$: gas cools during throttling ($\mu_{JT} > 0$)
- Above $T_i$: gas heats during throttling ($\mu_{JT} < 0$)

Common inversion temperatures:
- Nitrogen: $T_i \approx 621$ K ($348°\text{C}$)
- Oxygen: $T_i \approx 764$ K ($491°\text{C}$)
- Hydrogen: $T_i \approx 202$ K ($-71°\text{C}$)
- Helium: $T_i \approx 40$ K ($-233°\text{C}$)

Room temperature ($300$ K) is below $T_i$ for most gases except He and H₂.

## Van der Waals Internal Energy

For **van der Waals gas**:

$$\left(P + \frac{an^2}{V^2}\right)(V - nb) = nRT$$

The internal energy includes correction for intermolecular attractions:

$$U = nC_V T - \frac{an^2}{V}$$

The term $-an^2/V$ is potential energy of attraction (negative because attractive forces lower energy).

Partial derivative:

$$\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T = \frac{an^2}{V^2}$$

Always positive—expansion increases energy by separating attracting molecules.

## Gas Liquefaction

The **Linde process** uses Joule-Thomson cooling for gas liquefaction:

1. Compress gas (increases temperature)
2. Cool compressed gas to room temperature
3. Expand through throttling valve (Joule-Thomson cooling)
4. Use cooled gas to pre-cool incoming compressed gas
5. Repeat until gas liquefies

This cascade cooling is essential for producing liquid nitrogen ($77$ K), liquid oxygen ($90$ K), and liquid air used in medicine, research, and industry.

For gases with low $T_i$ (H₂, He), must first cool below inversion temperature using liquid nitrogen before Joule-Thomson effect works.

## Example 1: Joule Coefficient for Van der Waals Gas

For van der Waals gas with $U = nC_V T - an^2/V$, calculate $\mu_J$.

**Solution:**

Joule coefficient:

$$\mu_J = -\frac{1}{C_V}\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T$$

From van der Waals internal energy:

$$\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T = \frac{\partial}{\partial V}\left(nC_V T - \frac{an^2}{V}\right) = \frac{an^2}{V^2}$$

Therefore:

$$\mu_J = -\frac{1}{C_V} \cdot \frac{an^2}{V^2} = -\frac{an^2}{C_V V^2}$$

Since $a > 0$ (attractive forces), $\mu_J < 0$: temperature **drops** during free expansion.

**Physical interpretation**: Molecules do work against attractive forces during expansion, drawing energy from kinetic energy (temperature).

**Answer:** $\mu_J = -\frac{an^2}{C_V V^2} < 0$

## Example 2: Inversion Temperature for Nitrogen

For nitrogen with $a = 1.370$ atm} \cdot text {L²/mol², $b = 0.0387$ L/mol, estimate inversion temperature.

**Solution:**

For van der Waals gas:

$$T_i \approx \frac{2a}{Rb}$$

Using $R = 0.08206$ atm} \cdot text {L/(mol} \cdot text {K):

$$T_i = \frac{2(1.370)}{(0.08206)(0.0387)} = \frac{2.740}{0.003176} = 863 \text{ K}$$

$$= 863 - 273 = 590°\text{C}$$

Experimental value is approximately $621$ K ($348°\text{C}$)—reasonable agreement.

**Physical meaning**: At room temperature ($300$ K $< T_i$), nitrogen cools during throttling. Above $863$ K, it would heat during throttling.

**Answer:** $T_i \approx 863$ K

## Example 3: Internal Energy Change for Real Gas

One mole of van der Waals gas with $C_V = 12.5$ J/(mol} \cdot text {K) and $a = 0.50$ J} \cdot text {m³/mol² expands from $V_1 = 0.020$ m³ to $V_2 = 0.040$ m³ at constant temperature $T = 300$ K. Find $\Delta U$.

**Solution:**

For van der Waals gas:

$$\Delta U = nC_V\Delta T + \int_{V_1}^{V_2} \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T dV$$

At constant temperature, $\Delta T = 0$:

$$\Delta U = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} \frac{an^2}{V^2} dV = an^2 \int_{V_1}^{V_2} \frac{dV}{V^2}$$

$$= an^2\left[-\frac{1}{V}\right]_{V_1}^{V_2} = an^2\left(\frac{1}{V_1} - \frac{1}{V_2}\right)$$

$$= (0.50)(1)^2\left(\frac{1}{0.020} - \frac{1}{0.040}\right) = 0.50(50 - 25)$$

$$= 0.50 \times 25 = 12.5 \text{ J}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Despite constant temperature, internal energy increases by $12.5$ J because molecules separate against attractive forces. For ideal gas, this would be zero.

**Answer:** $\Delta U = 12.5$ J

## Example 4: Joule-Thomson Cooling

Nitrogen at $P_1 = 150$ atm and $T_1 = 300$ K undergoes Joule-Thomson expansion to $P_2 = 1$ atm. Given $\mu_{JT} = 0.27$ K/atm at these conditions, estimate final temperature.

**Solution:**

For small pressure changes, approximate:

$$\Delta T \approx \mu_{JT} \Delta P$$

Here:

$$\Delta P = P_2 - P_1 = 1 - 150 = -149 \text{ atm}$$

$$\Delta T = (0.27)(-149) = -40.2 \text{ K}$$

$$T_2 = T_1 + \Delta T = 300 - 40.2 = 259.8 \text{ K} = -13.2°\text{C}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Throttling from high pressure (150 atm) to atmospheric pressure cools nitrogen by about $40$ K. Multiple stages can achieve even lower temperatures for liquefaction.

**Answer:** $T_2 \approx 260$ K ($-13°\text{C}$)

## Example 5: Comparing Ideal and Real Gas

Compare internal energy change for one mole of gas expanding isothermally from $V_1 = 0.010$ m³ to $V_2 = 0.030$ m³ at $T = 350$ K:
(a) Ideal gas
(b) Van der Waals gas with $a = 0.40$ J} \cdot text {m³/mol²

**Solution:**

(a) **Ideal gas**:

$$\Delta U = 0$$

(temperature constant, $U = U(T)$ only)

(b) **Van der Waals gas**:

$$\Delta U = an^2\left(\frac{1}{V_1} - \frac{1}{V_2}\right) = (0.40)(1)^2\left(\frac{1}{0.010} - \frac{1}{0.030}\right)$$

$$= 0.40(100 - 33.3) = 0.40 \times 66.7 = 26.7 \text{ J}$$

**Comparison**: Real gas requires $26.7$ J more energy input to maintain constant temperature during expansion—energy overcomes intermolecular attractions.

**Answer:** (a) 0, (b) 26.7 J
