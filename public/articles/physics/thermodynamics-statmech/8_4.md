# Article 8.4: Mean-Field Theory

Exact solutions of interacting systems are rare treasures in statistical mechanics. For most models—3D Ising, real magnets, liquids near critical points—we must approximate. **Mean-field theory** provides a tractable framework: replace fluctuating microscopic fields with their average values. While this approximation sacrifices accuracy (predicting incorrect critical exponents), it captures essential physics: spontaneous symmetry breaking, order parameter evolution, phase boundaries. Mean-field theory succeeds because fluctuations become less important in high dimensions—it's exact for $d \geq 4$ (upper critical dimension).

The **Curie-Weiss mean-field theory** for the Ising model exemplifies the approach. Each spin experiences an effective field from neighbors' average magnetization, leading to a **self-consistency equation** $m = \tanh(\beta Jzm + \beta h)$. Below critical temperature $T_c = Jz/k_B$, nontrivial solutions $m \neq 0$ emerge spontaneously—the system "chooses" a magnetization direction despite Hamiltonian symmetry. Near $T_c$, we can expand and extract critical exponents: $\beta = 1/2$ (magnetization), $\gamma = 1$ (susceptibility), different from exact 2D and 3D values but providing qualitative understanding.

This article develops mean-field approximation systematically, derives the self-consistency equation for Ising ferromagnets, analyzes graphical and analytical solutions, calculates critical temperature from stability analysis, derives spontaneous magnetization using Taylor expansion, extracts mean-field critical exponents, calculates magnetic susceptibility and its divergence, explores limitations and breakdown of mean-field theory, and discusses applications beyond magnetism including BCS superconductivity and Landau-Ginzburg theory. The mathematics involves self-consistency equations, bifurcation analysis, Taylor expansions, stability testing, and graphical methods.

## Mean-Field Approximation

**Basic idea**: Replace fluctuating quantity with its average.

**Ising model**: Hamiltonian has $H = -J\sum_{\langle i,j \rangle} s_i s_j$

**Exact**: Spin $s_i$ interacts with actual neighbors $s_j$

**Mean-field**: Replace neighbors with average magnetization:

$$s_j \to \langle s \rangle = m$$

**Effective Hamiltonian** for single spin:

$$H_i = -s_i \sum_{j \in \text{neighbors}} J m - hs_i$$

$$= -s_i(Jzm + h)$$

where $z$ is coordination number (number of nearest neighbors).

**Physical interpretation**: Each spin sees **effective field** $h_{\text{eff}} = Jzm + h$ created by neighbors' average magnetization.

## Curie-Weiss Mean-Field Theory

**Single spin in effective field**:

$$\langle s_i \rangle = \tanh[\beta(Jzm + h)]$$

This follows from canonical ensemble for spin-1/2 in field.

**Self-consistency**: Average magnetization equals assumed $m$:

$$\boxed{m = \tanh(\beta Jzm + \beta h)}$$

This is **Curie-Weiss equation** (also called mean-field equation).

**No external field** ($h = 0$):

$$\boxed{m = \tanh(\beta Jzm)}$$

## Self-Consistency Equation

**Graphical solution**: Plot $y = m$ and $y = \tanh(\beta Jzm)$; intersections are solutions.

**High temperature** ($T \gg Jz/k_B$): $\beta Jz \ll 1$
- $\tanh(\beta Jzm) \approx \beta Jzm$ (linear for small argument)
- Only intersection: $m = 0$

**Low temperature** ($T \ll Jz/k_B$): $\beta Jz \gg 1$
- $\tanh$ saturates quickly
- **Three** intersections: $m = 0$ (unstable) and $m = \pm m_0$ (stable)

**Critical temperature**: Where nontrivial solution bifurcates from $m = 0$.

At $m = 0$:

$$\frac{d}{dm}\tanh(\beta Jzm)\bigg|_{m=0} = \beta Jz$$

Nontrivial solution appears when slope equals 1:

$$\beta_c Jz = 1$$

$$\boxed{k_B T_c = Jz}$$

**Coordination numbers**:
- 1D ($z = 2$): $T_c = 2J$ (wrong—actually $T_c = 0$)
- 2D square ($z = 4$): $T_c = 4J$ (overestimate of exact $2.27J$)
- 3D simple cubic ($z = 6$): $T_c = 6J$
- 3D BCC ($z = 8$): $T_c = 8J$
- 3D FCC ($z = 12$): $T_c = 12J$

Higher $z$ → higher $T_c$ (more neighbors stabilize order).

## Order Parameter Below $T_c$

**Near $T_c$**: Magnetization small, use Taylor expansion.

$$\tanh(x) = x - \frac{x^3}{3} + \frac{2x^5}{15} - ...$$

Keeping terms to $m^3$:

$$m = \beta Jzm - \frac{(\beta Jzm)^3}{3}$$

$$m\left[1 - \beta Jz + \frac{(\beta Jz)^3m^2}{3}\right] = 0$$

**Nontrivial solution** ($m \neq 0$):

$$1 - \beta Jz + \frac{(\beta Jz)^3m^2}{3} = 0$$

$$m^2 = \frac{3(1 - \beta Jz)}{(\beta Jz)^3} = \frac{3(\beta_c - \beta)}{(\beta Jz)^3}$$

Using $\beta = 1/(k_B T)$ and $\beta_c = 1/(k_B T_c)$:

$$\frac{1}{T_c} - \frac{1}{T} = \frac{T - T_c}{TT_c} \approx \frac{T - T_c}{T_c^2}$$ (for $T \approx T_c$)

$$m^2 \approx \frac{3(T_c - T)}{T_c}$$

$$\boxed{m \sim (T_c - T)^{1/2}}$$

**Critical exponent**: $\beta = 1/2$ (mean-field prediction)

**Comparison**:
- 2D Ising exact: $\beta = 1/8$
- 3D Ising: $\beta \approx 0.33$
- Mean-field: $\beta = 1/2$

Mean-field overestimates how rapidly magnetization vanishes near $T_c$.

## Magnetic Susceptibility

**Susceptibility**: Response to small applied field:

$$\chi = \frac{\partial m}{\partial h}\bigg|_{h=0}$$

From self-consistency equation:

$$m = \tanh(\beta Jzm + \beta h)$$

Differentiating with respect to $h$:

$$\frac{\partial m}{\partial h} = \text{sech}^2(\beta Jzm + \beta h)\left[\beta Jz\frac{\partial m}{\partial h} + \beta\right]$$

At $h = 0$:

$$\chi = \text{sech}^2(\beta Jzm)[\beta Jz \chi + \beta]$$

$$\chi[1 - \beta Jz \cdot \text{sech}^2(\beta Jzm)] = \beta \cdot \text{sech}^2(\beta Jzm)$$

**Above $T_c$** ($m = 0$):

$$\chi = \frac{\beta}{1 - \beta Jz} = \frac{1/T}{(1/T_c) - (1/T)} = \frac{T_c}{T - T_c}$$

$$\boxed{\chi \sim (T - T_c)^{-1}}$$

**Critical exponent**: $\gamma = 1$ (mean-field)

**Comparison**: 
- 2D Ising: $\gamma = 7/4 = 1.75$
- 3D Ising: $\gamma \approx 1.24$
- Mean-field: $\gamma = 1$

**Below $T_c$**: Susceptibility also diverges approaching from below.

## Bifurcation Analysis

**Stability** of solutions: Check second derivative of free energy.

Near $T_c$, effective free energy (Landau form):

$$F(m) = -\frac{a}{2}m^2 + \frac{b}{4}m^4$$

where $a = \beta Jz - 1 = (T_c - T)/T_c$.

**Minimization**: $\frac{\partial F}{\partial m} = -am + bm^3 = 0$

**Solutions**:
1. $m = 0$
2. $m = \pm\sqrt{a/b}$

**Second derivative**:

$$\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial m^2} = -a + 3bm^2$$

**At $m = 0$**:
- $T > T_c$ ($a < 0$): $\partial^2F/\partial m^2 < 0$ → **maximum** (unstable)
- $T < T_c$ ($a > 0$): $\partial^2F/\partial m^2 > 0$ → **minimum** (stable)

**Bifurcation**: At $T = T_c$, $m = 0$ loses stability; system spontaneously magnetizes to $m = \pm\sqrt{a/b}$.

This is **spontaneous symmetry breaking**—Hamiltonian symmetric under $m \to -m$, but ground state chooses one direction.

## Limitations of Mean-Field Theory

**Breakdown**: Mean-field assumes fluctuations negligible—valid only when:

1. **High dimension**: $d \geq 4$ (upper critical dimension)
2. **Long-range interactions**: Interactions decay slowly with distance
3. **Far from $T_c$**: Fluctuations small away from critical point

**Near $T_c$ in low dimensions**: Fluctuations diverge, invalidate mean-field.

**Ginzburg criterion**: Estimates where mean-field breaks down:

$$\frac{\text{Fluctuation amplitude}^2}{\text{Mean-field value}^2} \ll 1$$

Fails near $T_c$ for $d < 4$.

**Incorrect predictions**:
- Critical exponents wrong for $d < 4$
- Overestimates $T_c$ (except upper critical dimension)
- Misses logarithmic corrections at $d = 4$

**Success**: Despite limitations, captures qualitative physics—phase diagrams, symmetry breaking, order parameter behavior.

## Example 1: Critical Temperature Calculation

Simple cubic lattice ($z = 6$) with $J = 8$ meV. Find mean-field $T_c$.

**Solution:**

$$k_B T_c = Jz = (8 \text{ meV})(6) = 48 \text{ meV}$$

Converting:

$$T_c = \frac{48 \times 10^{-3}}{8.617 \times 10^{-5}} = 557 \text{ K}$$

**Typical real magnet**: Iron has $T_c = 1043$ K, higher due to stronger coupling and quantum effects beyond classical Ising model.

**Answer:** $T_c = 557$ K

## Example 2: Magnetization at $T = 0.5T_c$

Mean-field magnet with $Jz/k_B = 100$ K at $T = 50$ K. Find $m$.

**Solution:**

$$T_c = 100 \text{ K}, \quad T = 0.5T_c$$

Using $(T_c - T)/T_c = 0.5$:

$$m = \sqrt{\frac{3(T_c - T)}{T_c}} = \sqrt{\frac{3(0.5)}{1}} = \sqrt{1.5} = 1.22$$

But $m \leq 1$ (magnetization cannot exceed saturation)! Approximation breaks down far from $T_c$.

**Better approach**: Solve $m = \tanh(2m)$ numerically at $\beta Jz = 2$.

Result: $m \approx 0.96$ (96% saturated)

**Answer:** $m \approx 0.96$ (numerical solution)

## Example 3: Susceptibility Above $T_c$

At $T = 1.1T_c$, find susceptibility in units of $1/(k_B T_c)$.

**Solution:**

$$\chi = \frac{1}{k_B(T - T_c)} = \frac{1}{k_B T_c(1.1 - 1)} = \frac{1}{0.1 k_B T_c}$$

$$= \frac{10}{k_B T_c}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Susceptibility diverges as $T \to T_c^+$—small field induces large magnetization near critical point.

**Answer:** $\chi = 10/(k_B T_c)$

## Example 4: Spontaneous Magnetization vs Temperature

For mean-field magnet, calculate $m(T)/m(0)$ at $T/T_c = 0.2, 0.5, 0.8$.

**Solution:**

Using $m = \sqrt{3(T_c - T)/T_c}$ near $T_c$ (approximate):

At $T = 0$: $m(0) \approx 1$ (fully magnetized)

**$T/T_c = 0.2$**:
$$m/m(0) = \sqrt{3(1 - 0.2)} = \sqrt{2.4} = 1.55$$

Too large—approximation invalid far from $T_c$. Need numerical solution of $m = \tanh(\beta Jzm)$.

**Numerical results** (for $T_c = Jz$):
- $T/T_c = 0.2$: $m \approx 0.996$
- $T/T_c = 0.5$: $m \approx 0.963$
- $T/T_c = 0.8$: $m \approx 0.723$

**Answer:** $m(0.2T_c)/m(0) = 0.996$, $m(0.5T_c)/m(0) = 0.963$, $m(0.8T_c)/m(0) = 0.723$

## Example 5: Heat Capacity Jump

Mean-field predicts heat capacity discontinuity at $T_c$. From $F = -\frac{a}{2}m^2 + \frac{b}{4}m^4$, find $\Delta C$.

**Solution:**

Above $T_c$: $m = 0$, $F = F_0$

$$C_{T > T_c} = -T\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial T^2} = 0$$ (constant $F_0$)

Below $T_c$: $m^2 = a/b = (T_c - T)/(bT_c)$

$$F = F_0 - \frac{a^2}{4b} = F_0 - \frac{(T_c - T)^2}{4bT_c^2}$$

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial T} = \frac{T_c - T}{2bT_c^2}$$

$$C_{T < T_c} = -T\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial T^2} = \frac{T}{2bT_c^2}$$

At $T_c$:

$$\Delta C = C(T_c^-) - C(T_c^+) = \frac{T_c}{2bT_c^2} = \frac{1}{2bT_c}$$

**Jump**: Proportional to $1/T_c$. Real systems show divergence (power law), not jump—mean-field underestimates critical singularity.

**Answer:** $\Delta C = 1/(2bT_c)$ (discontinuous jump)
