# Article 3.5: Statistical Interpretation of Entropy

Classical thermodynamics defines entropy through heat and temperature: $dS = \delta Q_{rev}/T$. But what is entropy physically? Why does it increase in isolated systems? The answer lies in statistics. Ludwig Boltzmann showed that entropy measures the number of microscopic arrangements (microstates) consistent with a given macroscopic state. His famous formula $S = k_B\ln\Omega$ connects thermodynamic entropy to statistical multiplicity.

The statistical view reveals entropy as a measure of disorder, uncertainty, or missing information. A gas confined to half a container has fewer available microstates than when it fills the entire container—hence lower entropy. The second law emerges naturally: systems evolve toward states with more microstates because they're overwhelmingly more probable. Irreversibility is statistical—reversible processes are possible but fantastically improbable.

This article develops Boltzmann's statistical interpretation, explores multiplicity calculations, connects to information theory through Gibbs entropy, and introduces the third law. The mathematics involves combinatorics, Stirling's approximation, probability theory, and logarithmic functions.

## Microstates and Macrostates

A **microstate** specifies the complete microscopic configuration: position and momentum of every particle.

A **macrostate** specifies only macroscopic variables: pressure, temperature, volume, energy.

**Key insight**: Many different microstates correspond to the same macrostate.

**Example**: Four molecules, two energy units to distribute:

Possible microstates:
- Molecule 1 has 2 units, others have 0: (2,0,0,0)
- Molecule 2 has 2 units, others have 0: (0,2,0,0)
- Molecule 3 has 2 units, others have 0: (0,0,2,0)
- Molecule 4 has 2 units, others have 0: (0,0,0,2)
- Molecules 1 and 2 each have 1 unit: (1,1,0,0)
- And many more...

The macrostate "2 energy units total" corresponds to **many** microstates. The number of microstates is the **multiplicity** $\Omega$.

## Boltzmann's Entropy Formula

Boltzmann's fundamental insight:

$$\boxed{S = k_B \ln \Omega}$$

where:
- $S$ = entropy
- $k_B = 1.381 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K = Boltzmann constant
- $\Omega$ = number of microstates (multiplicity)

**Physical interpretation**: Entropy measures the number of ways to arrange microscopic constituents while maintaining the same macroscopic state. More microstates = higher entropy = more disorder.

**Why logarithm?** 
1. Entropy is extensive: $S \propto N$ (number of particles)
2. Multiplicity is multiplicative: $\Omega \propto e^N$
3. Logarithm converts multiplication to addition: $\ln(e^N) \propto N$

## Multiplicity and Probability

For system with $N$ distinguishable particles distributed among $k$ energy levels with $n_i$ particles in level $i$:

$$\Omega = \frac{N!}{n_1! n_2! \cdot text{s} n_k!}$$

This is **multinomial coefficient**—number of ways to arrange $N$ objects into $k$ groups.

**Probability** of macrostate is proportional to multiplicity:

$$P \propto \Omega$$

States with more microstates are more probable. System naturally evolves toward highest-probability (maximum $\Omega$) states.

## Stirling's Approximation

For large $N$ (typical: $N \sim 10^{23}$), factorials are enormous. **Stirling's approximation**:

$$\ln N! \approx N\ln N - N$$

More accurate form:

$$N! \approx \sqrt{2\pi N}\left(\frac{N}{e}\right)^N$$

This simplifies entropy calculations:

$$S = k_B\ln\Omega = k_B\ln\frac{N!}{\prod n_i!} \approx k_B\left[N\ln N - \sum n_i\ln n_i\right]$$

## Gibbs Entropy and Information Theory

For system in state $i$ with probability $p_i$, **Gibbs entropy** is:

$$S = -k_B \sum_i p_i \ln p_i$$

This generalizes Boltzmann's formula:
- If all $\Omega$ microstates equally probable: $p_i = 1/\Omega$

$$S = -k_B \sum_{i=1}^\Omega \frac{1}{\Omega}\ln\frac{1}{\Omega} = -k_B \cdot \Omega \cdot \frac{1}{\Omega} \cdot (-\ln\Omega) = k_B\ln\Omega$$ ✓

**Connection to information theory**: Shannon entropy (information missing) has same form:

$$H = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i \quad \text{(bits)}$$

With $k_B = 1$ and $\ln \to \log_2$, thermodynamic entropy = information entropy.

**Physical interpretation**: Entropy measures our ignorance about microscopic state. Maximum ignorance (all microstates equally likely) = maximum entropy.

## Third Law of Thermodynamics

**Third law** (Nernst): As temperature approaches absolute zero, entropy approaches a constant (usually zero):

$$\lim_{T \to 0} S = 0$$

**Statistical interpretation**: At $T = 0$, system is in ground state (lowest energy). If ground state is non-degenerate: $\Omega = 1$.

$$S = k_B\ln(1) = 0$$

**Consequences**:
- Cannot reach absolute zero in finite steps
- Heat capacities $C_V, C_P \to 0$ as $T \to 0$
- Thermal expansion coefficient $\alpha \to 0$ as $T \to 0$

Quantum mechanics: some systems have degenerate ground states ($\Omega > 1$), giving residual zero-point entropy.

## Example 1: Boltzmann Entropy for Two-State System

A system has two equally probable microstates ($\Omega = 2$). Calculate entropy.

**Solution:**

Boltzmann's formula:

$$S = k_B\ln\Omega = k_B\ln(2) = (1.381 \times 10^{-23}) \times 0.693$$

$$= 9.57 \times 10^{-24} \text{ J/K}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Even simple two-state system has non-zero entropy (one bit of information). This is minimum entropy for system with any uncertainty.

**Answer:** $S = 9.57 \times 10^{-24}$ J/K

## Example 2: Multiplicity Calculation

Four distinguishable particles, 6 energy units total. How many ways to distribute energy if:
(a) Each particle can have any number of units
(b) Calculate entropy for $\Omega$ from (a)

**Solution:**

(a) This is "stars and bars" problem. Number of ways to put $n$ indistinguishable items into $k$ distinguishable bins:

$$\Omega = \binom{n + k - 1}{k - 1}$$

Here: $n = 6$ units, $k = 4$ particles:

$$\Omega = \binom{6 + 4 - 1}{4 - 1} = \binom{9}{3} = \frac{9!}{3! \cdot 6!} = \frac{9 \times 8 \times 7}{3 \times 2 \times 1} = 84$$

(b) Entropy:

$$S = k_B\ln\Omega = k_B\ln(84) = (1.381 \times 10^{-23}) \times 4.431$$

$$= 6.12 \times 10^{-23} \text{ J/K}$$

**Answer:** (a) $\Omega = 84$, (b) $S = 6.12 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K

## Example 3: Gibbs Entropy

A system has three microstates with probabilities $p_1 = 0.5$, $p_2 = 0.3$, $p_3 = 0.2$. Calculate entropy.

**Solution:**

Gibbs entropy:

$$S = -k_B \sum_i p_i\ln p_i$$

$$= -k_B[p_1\ln p_1 + p_2\ln p_2 + p_3\ln p_3]$$

$$= -k_B[0.5\ln(0.5) + 0.3\ln(0.3) + 0.2\ln(0.2)]$$

$$= -k_B[0.5(-0.693) + 0.3(-1.204) + 0.2(-1.609)]$$

$$= -k_B[-0.347 - 0.361 - 0.322] = -k_B(-1.030)$$

$$= 1.030 k_B = 1.030 \times 1.381 \times 10^{-23} = 1.42 \times 10^{-23} \text{ J/K}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Non-uniform probabilities give lower entropy than uniform ($p_i = 1/3$ would give $S = k_B\ln 3 = 1.52 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K). Less uncertainty = lower entropy.

**Answer:** $S = 1.42 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K

## Example 4: Using Stirling's Approximation

Calculate $\ln(100!)$ using:
(a) Stirling's approximation
(b) Compare to exact value (using calculator: $\ln(100!) = 363.74$)

**Solution:**

(a) Stirling's approximation:

$$\ln N! \approx N\ln N - N$$

$$\ln(100!) \approx 100\ln(100) - 100 = 100(4.605) - 100$$

$$= 460.5 - 100 = 360.5$$

(b) Exact: $\ln(100!) = 363.74$

Error:

$$\frac{|363.74 - 360.5|}{363.74} = \frac{3.24}{363.74} = 0.0089 = 0.89\%$$

**Physical interpretation**: Stirling's approximation is excellent for large $N$. For thermodynamic systems ($N \sim 10^{23}$), error is negligible.

**Answer:** (a) 360.5, (b) 0.89% error

## Example 5: Second Law from Statistics

Gas initially confined to left half of container (volume $V/2$) expands to fill entire container (volume $V$). Calculate entropy change for $N$ molecules.

**Solution:**

**Initial state**: Each molecule confined to volume $V/2$
Microstates available per molecule: $\Omega_i \propto V/2$

Total initial multiplicity: $\Omega_i \propto (V/2)^N$

**Final state**: Each molecule can be anywhere in volume $V$
Total final multiplicity: $\Omega_f \propto V^N$

**Entropy change**:

$$\Delta S = k_B\ln\Omega_f - k_B\ln\Omega_i = k_B\ln\frac{\Omega_f}{\Omega_i}$$

$$= k_B\ln\frac{V^N}{(V/2)^N} = k_B\ln(2^N) = Nk_B\ln 2$$

For $N = N_A$ (one mole):

$$\Delta S = N_A k_B \ln 2 = R\ln 2 = 8.314 \times 0.693 = 5.76 \text{ J/K}$$

This matches thermodynamic calculation: $\Delta S = nR\ln(V_f/V_i) = R\ln 2$ ✓

**Physical interpretation**: Expansion increases available microstates by factor $2^N$ (each molecule has twice as many positions). Entropy increases accordingly.

**Answer:** $\Delta S = Nk_B\ln 2$ per mole

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C** - Boltzmann: $S = k_B\ln\Omega$ (entropy = constant × natural log of multiplicity).

**Problem 4: D** - Third law: entropy approaches **zero** at absolute zero.

**Problem 5: B** - More microstates → **higher entropy** ($S = k_B\ln\Omega$ increases with $\Omega$).
