# Article 5.1: Foundations of Statistical Mechanics

Thermodynamics describes macroscopic systems through variables like pressure, temperature, and entropy without reference to microscopic constituents. But these macroscopic properties emerge from the collective behavior of vast numbers of molecules—typically $10^{23}$ particles obeying Newton's laws. **Statistical mechanics** bridges this gap, deriving thermodynamic laws from microscopic dynamics through probability theory. The central insight: we can't track every molecule, so we use statistical ensembles representing all possible microscopic states consistent with macroscopic constraints.

The foundations rest on several profound concepts. **Phase space** provides a geometric arena where each point represents a complete microscopic state. The **fundamental postulate** of equal a priori probabilities states that isolated systems access all available microstates with equal probability. **Liouville's theorem** ensures phase space volume conservation during evolution. The **ergodic hypothesis** connects time averages (what we measure) to ensemble averages (what we calculate). Together, these principles transform mechanics into statistical mechanics.

This article develops phase space formalism, establishes the fundamental postulate, introduces ensemble concepts, explores time versus ensemble averaging, states the ergodic hypothesis, proves Liouville's theorem, and discusses phase space density evolution. The mathematics involves high-dimensional geometry, probability theory, Hamiltonian mechanics, Poisson brackets, partial differential equations, and volume integrals in phase space.

## Phase Space and Microstates

For system of $N$ particles in three dimensions, each particle has position $\mathbf{r}_i = (x_i, y_i, z_i)$ and momentum $\mathbf{p}_i = (p_{x,i}, p_{y,i}, p_{z,i})$.

**Phase space** is the $6N$-dimensional space with coordinates:

$$\Gamma = (\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{p}) = (q_1, ..., q_{3N}, p_1, ..., p_{3N})$$

where $\mathbf{q} = (\mathbf{r}_1, ..., \mathbf{r}_N)$ and $\mathbf{p} = (\mathbf{p}_1, ..., \mathbf{p}_N)$.

Each **point** in phase space represents a complete **microstate**—a specification of all positions and momenta.

**Phase space volume element**:

$$d\Gamma = d^{3N}q \, d^{3N}p = dq_1 \cdots dq_{3N} \, dp_1 \cdots dp_{3N}$$

**Quantum correction**: Classical phase space must be divided into cells of volume $h^{3N}$ where $h$ is Planck's constant, ensuring correct quantum counting.

**Accessible phase space volume** for system with energy $E$:

$$\Omega(E) = \frac{1}{h^{3N}} \int_{H(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p}) = E} d\Gamma$$

where $H(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{p})$ is the Hamiltonian (total energy).

## The Fundamental Postulate

**Fundamental postulate of statistical mechanics**: For an isolated system in equilibrium, all accessible microstates are equally probable.

If system has energy $E$, all microstates with $H(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{p}) = E$ occur with equal probability:

$$P_i = \frac{1}{\Omega(E)}$$

where $\Omega(E)$ is the number of accessible microstates.

**Consequences**:
1. Entropy $S = k_B \ln \Omega$ (Boltzmann formula)
2. Second law emerges—systems evolve toward maximum $\Omega$
3. Thermodynamics follows from statistics

**Justification**: No reason to prefer one microstate over another—all are consistent with macroscopic constraints. This is a maximally unbiased assumption (maximum entropy principle).

## Ensemble Concept

An **ensemble** is a large collection of mental copies of the system, each in a different microstate, but all consistent with the same macroscopic constraints.

**Types of ensembles**:

1. **Microcanonical**: Isolated system, fixed $(E, V, N)$
   - All microstates with energy $E$ equally probable

2. **Canonical**: System in thermal contact with reservoir, fixed $(T, V, N)$
   - Energy can fluctuate, probability $P_i \propto e^{-\beta E_i}$

3. **Grand canonical**: System exchanges energy and particles, fixed $(T, V, \mu)$
   - Both energy and particle number fluctuate

**Ensemble average** of observable $A$:

$$\langle A \rangle = \sum_i P_i A_i$$

where $A_i$ is the value of $A$ in microstate $i$.

## Time Averages vs. Ensemble Averages

**Time average**: Follow single system over long time, measure $A(t)$, average:

$$\bar{A} = \lim_{\tau \to \infty} \frac{1}{\tau} \int_0^\tau A(t) \, dt$$

**Ensemble average**: Consider many copies at fixed time, average over ensemble:

$$\langle A \rangle = \int A(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{p}) \rho(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{p}) \, d\Gamma$$

where $\rho$ is probability density in phase space.

**Key question**: Are they equal? $\bar{A} \stackrel{?}{=} \langle A \rangle$

This is the content of the ergodic hypothesis.

## The Ergodic Hypothesis

**Ergodic hypothesis**: For ergodic systems, time averages equal ensemble averages:

$$\bar{A} = \langle A \rangle$$

**Physical meaning**: A single system, followed for long enough, explores all regions of accessible phase space. Time spent in each region is proportional to phase space volume of that region.

**Conditions for ergodicity**:
- System must be mixing (trajectories diverge)
- No conserved quantities beyond energy (and momentum, angular momentum for free systems)
- Typically requires chaotic dynamics

**Non-ergodic systems**: Integrable systems (harmonic oscillator, free particle) are not ergodic—trajectories lie on lower-dimensional manifolds.

**Practical importance**: We measure time averages (single system over time), but calculate ensemble averages (much easier mathematically). Ergodicity justifies this approach.

## Liouville's Theorem

**Liouville's theorem**: Phase space volume is conserved during Hamiltonian evolution.

If we follow a region $\Delta \Gamma$ in phase space as it evolves:

$$\frac{d(\Delta \Gamma)}{dt} = 0$$

**Mathematical formulation**: The phase space density $\rho(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{p}, t)$ satisfies:

$$\frac{d\rho}{dt} = \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \{\rho, H\} = 0$$

This is **Liouville's equation**.

**Proof sketch**: Hamilton's equations give:

$$\dot{q}_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \quad \dot{p}_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}$$

The flow in phase space is **incompressible** (divergence-free):

$$\sum_i \left(\frac{\partial \dot{q}_i}{\partial q_i} + \frac{\partial \dot{p}_i}{\partial p_i}\right) = \sum_i \left(\frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial q_i \partial p_i} - \frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial p_i \partial q_i}\right) = 0$$

This implies volume conservation.

**Physical interpretation**: Phase space "fluid" is incompressible—it may deform but never compresses or expands. This is fundamental to all of statistical mechanics.

## Density in Phase Space

The **phase space density** $\rho(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{p}, t)$ gives probability of finding system at point $(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{p})$ at time $t$.

**Normalization**:

$$\int \rho(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{p}, t) \, d\Gamma = 1$$

**Equation of motion** (Liouville's equation):

$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \sum_i \left(\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial q_i}\dot{q}_i + \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial p_i}\dot{p}_i\right) = 0$$

Or using Poisson bracket:

$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \{\rho, H\} = 0$$

where:

$$\{A, B\} = \sum_i \left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial B}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial A}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial B}{\partial q_i}\right)$$

**Equilibrium**: For equilibrium, $\partial \rho/\partial t = 0$, so:

$$\{\rho, H\} = 0$$

This means $\rho$ is constant along trajectories, or $\rho = \rho(H)$ (function of energy only).

## Poisson Brackets

The **Poisson bracket** of observables $A$ and $B$:

$$\{A, B\} = \sum_i \left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial B}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial A}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial B}{\partial q_i}\right)$$

**Properties**:
- Anti-symmetric: $\{A, B\} = -\{B, A\}$
- Bilinear
- Jacobi identity: $\{A, \{B, C\}\} + \{B, \{C, A\}\} + \{C, \{A, B\}\} = 0$

**Time evolution** of observable:

$$\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{\partial A}{\partial t} + \{A, H\}$$

For observable with no explicit time dependence:

$$\frac{dA}{dt} = \{A, H\}$$

If $\{A, H\} = 0$, then $A$ is **conserved**.

## Example 1: Phase Space for Single Particle

One particle in one dimension with Hamiltonian $H = p^2/(2m) + V(x)$. Describe phase space.

**Solution:**

**Phase space**: Two-dimensional with coordinates $(x, p)$.

**Dimensions**: $x$ has units of length, $p$ has units of momentum.

**Energy surface**: Curve in $(x, p)$ plane where $H(x, p) = E$:

$$\frac{p^2}{2m} + V(x) = E$$

$$p = \pm\sqrt{2m[E - V(x)]}$$

For **harmonic oscillator** $V = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$:

$$\frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = E$$

This is an **ellipse** in $(x, p)$ space.

**Phase space volume** (quantum):

$$\Omega = \frac{1}{h} \oint p \, dx$$

For harmonic oscillator with period $T$:

$$\Omega = \frac{E}{h\nu} = \frac{E}{\hbar\omega}$$

where $\nu = 1/T$ is frequency.

**Answer:** Two-dimensional $(x, p)$ space; energy surfaces are curves; harmonic oscillator gives ellipses

## Example 2: Equal Probability Verification

Gas has two accessible microstates with $E_1 = 1$ eV and $E_2 = 1$ eV. If both have same energy, show they're equally probable.

**Solution:**

Fundamental postulate: All microstates with same energy are equally probable.

Since $E_1 = E_2$:

$$P_1 = P_2 = \frac{1}{2}$$

**Justification**: No physical reason to prefer one over the other—they have identical energy. Maximum entropy principle: uniform distribution maximizes $S = -k_B \sum P_i \ln P_i$ subject to constraint $\sum P_i = 1$.

For two states:

$$S = -k_B[P_1 \ln P_1 + P_2 \ln P_2]$$

Maximize with $P_1 + P_2 = 1$:

Using Lagrange multipliers: $P_1 = P_2 = 1/2$ maximizes entropy.

**Answer:** Both equally probable: $P_1 = P_2 = 1/2$

## Example 3: Liouville's Theorem for Free Particle

Free particle in one dimension: $H = p^2/(2m)$. Verify Liouville's equation holds.

**Solution:**

For free particle, phase space density $\rho(x, p, t)$.

Hamilton's equations:

$$\dot{x} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p} = \frac{p}{m}$$

$$\dot{p} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial x} = 0$$

Liouville's equation:

$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial x}\dot{x} + \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial p}\dot{p} = 0$$

$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \frac{p}{m}\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial x} = 0$$

This is **continuity equation** in phase space.

**Verify volume conservation**: 

$$\text{div}(\dot{x}, \dot{p}) = \frac{\partial \dot{x}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial \dot{p}}{\partial p} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{p}{m}\right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial p}(0) = 0$$

Divergence-free flow confirms volume conservation ✓

**Answer:** Liouville's equation: $\partial \rho/\partial t + (p/m)(\partial \rho/\partial x) = 0$

## Example 4: Ensemble Average Calculation

System has three microstates with energies $E_1 = 0$, $E_2 = \epsilon$, $E_3 = 2\epsilon$, each equally probable. Find average energy.

**Solution:**

Equal probabilities: $P_1 = P_2 = P_3 = 1/3$

Ensemble average:

$$\langle E \rangle = \sum_i P_i E_i = \frac{1}{3}(0) + \frac{1}{3}(\epsilon) + \frac{1}{3}(2\epsilon)$$

$$= \frac{\epsilon + 2\epsilon}{3} = \frac{3\epsilon}{3} = \epsilon$$

**Variance**:

$$\langle E^2 \rangle = \frac{1}{3}(0^2 + \epsilon^2 + 4\epsilon^2) = \frac{5\epsilon^2}{3}$$

$$\sigma^2 = \langle E^2 \rangle - \langle E \rangle^2 = \frac{5\epsilon^2}{3} - \epsilon^2 = \frac{2\epsilon^2}{3}$$

**Standard deviation**: $\sigma = \sqrt{2/3}\, \epsilon \approx 0.816\epsilon$

**Answer:** $\langle E \rangle = \epsilon$, $\sigma = \sqrt{2/3}\, \epsilon$

## Example 5: Poisson Bracket Calculation

For $H = p^2/(2m) + V(x)$, calculate $\{x, H\}$ and $\{p, H\}$.

**Solution:**

**For $\{x, H\}$**:

$$\{x, H\} = \frac{\partial x}{\partial x}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p} - \frac{\partial x}{\partial p}\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}$$

$$= (1)\left(\frac{p}{m}\right) - (0)\left(\frac{dV}{dx}\right) = \frac{p}{m}$$

This equals $\dot{x}$ from Hamilton's equations ✓

**For $\{p, H\}$**:

$$\{p, H\} = \frac{\partial p}{\partial x}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p} - \frac{\partial p}{\partial p}\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}$$

$$= (0)\left(\frac{p}{m}\right) - (1)\left(\frac{dV}{dx}\right) = -\frac{dV}{dx}$$

This equals $\dot{p}$ from Hamilton's equations ✓

**General result**:

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = \{x, H\}, \quad \frac{dp}{dt} = \{p, H\}$$

**Answer:** $\{x, H\} = p/m$, $\{p, H\} = -dV/dx$
