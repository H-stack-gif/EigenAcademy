# Article 8.5: Landau Theory and Renormalization (Introduction)

Mean-field theory, while illuminating, leaves crucial questions unanswered: Why do different systems share critical exponents (universality)? Why does mean-field fail in low dimensions? What determines critical behavior? **Landau theory** provides a more general phenomenological framework, expressing free energy as polynomial in order parameter. Minimization yields phase boundaries and qualitative behavior. But Landau theory, like mean-field, assumes weak fluctuations—the **Ginzburg criterion** quantifies when this assumption breaks down.

The breakthrough came with **renormalization group (RG)** theory in the 1970s (Wilson, Fisher, Kadanoff). RG reveals that near critical points, systems become **scale-invariant**—physics looks the same at different length scales. Coarse-graining (averaging over short-distance fluctuations) generates transformations of coupling constants. **Fixed points** of these transformations determine critical behavior, and different systems "flow" to the same fixed point, explaining universality. RG is mathematically sophisticated, but we can grasp the essential ideas: coarse-graining, scaling, fixed points, and why microscopic details become "irrelevant" near criticality.

This article develops Landau free energy expansion and its minimization for first and second-order transitions, derives conditions for transition type from polynomial coefficients, introduces the Ginzburg criterion for mean-field validity, explores fluctuation effects qualitatively, presents conceptual RG ideas including coarse-graining and scale invariance, discusses fixed points and their stability, explains universality classes through RG flow, and motivates how critical exponents emerge from scaling near fixed points. The mathematics involves polynomial minimization, stability analysis, symmetry breaking, scaling transformations, fixed point analysis, and dimensional reasoning.

## Landau Free Energy

**Landau ansatz**: Near phase transition, expand free energy in **order parameter** $\psi$:

$$\boxed{F(\psi, T) = F_0 + a(T)\psi^2 + b\psi^4 + c\psi^6 + ...}$$

**Assumptions**:
- Expansion valid for small $\psi$ (near transition)
- **Symmetry**: If Hamiltonian symmetric under $\psi \to -\psi$, only even powers appear
- **Temperature dependence**: Leading coefficient $a(T)$ changes sign at $T_c$

**Standard form**: $a(T) = a_0(T - T_c)$ where $a_0 > 0$

**Above $T_c$**: $a > 0$ → minimum at $\psi = 0$ (disordered)

**Below $T_c$**: $a < 0$ → minimum at $\psi \neq 0$ (ordered)

## Minimization of Landau Free Energy

**Equilibrium**: Minimize $F$ with respect to $\psi$:

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial \psi} = 2a\psi + 4b\psi^3 + 6c\psi^5 + ... = 0$$

**Second-order transition** ($b > 0$, $c > 0$):

Keeping up to $\psi^4$:

$$2a\psi + 4b\psi^3 = 0$$

**Solutions**:
1. $\psi = 0$
2. $\psi = \pm\sqrt{-a/(2b)} = \pm\sqrt{a_0(T_c - T)/(2b)}$

For $T < T_c$:

$$\psi \sim (T_c - T)^{1/2}$$

**Critical exponent**: $\beta = 1/2$ (Landau/mean-field result)

**First-order transition** ($b < 0$, $c > 0$):

Must keep $\psi^6$ term to stabilize:

$$F = a\psi^2 + b\psi^4 + c\psi^6$$

**Minimization** gives discontinuous jump in $\psi$ at transition—characteristic of first-order.

## First vs. Second Order Transitions

**Landau criterion**:

**Second-order** ($b > 0$):
- Continuous $\psi$ at $T_c$
- No latent heat
- Free energy smooth

**First-order** ($b < 0$):
- Discontinuous $\psi$ at $T_c$
- Latent heat $L = T\Delta S \neq 0$
- Free energy has kink

**Tricritical point**: Where $b = 0$, system changes from second to first order.

**Physical examples**:
- Metamagnets: External field can change transition order
- Helium-3/Helium-4 mixtures: Pressure tunes transition
- Liquid crystals: Various phases with different transitions

## Stability Analysis

**Second derivative test**:

$$\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial \psi^2} = 2a + 12b\psi^2 + 30c\psi^4 + ...$$

**At $\psi = 0$**:

$$\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial \psi^2}\bigg|_{\psi=0} = 2a = 2a_0(T - T_c)$$

- $T > T_c$: positive → minimum (stable)
- $T < T_c$: negative → maximum (unstable)

**At $\psi = \pm\sqrt{-a/(2b)}$ (for $T < T_c$, $b > 0$)**:

$$\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial \psi^2} = 2a + 12b\left(\frac{-a}{2b}\right) = 2a - 6a = -4a > 0$$

Positive → minimum (stable) ✓

**Bifurcation**: At $T = T_c$, $\psi = 0$ loses stability; system spontaneously develops $\psi \neq 0$.

## Fluctuations and Ginzburg Criterion

**Mean-field assumes**: Order parameter nearly uniform, fluctuations small.

**Reality near $T_c$**: Fluctuations grow, correlation length diverges.

**Ginzburg criterion**: Mean-field valid when fluctuation correction $\ll$ mean-field value.

**Fluctuation amplitude**:

$$\delta\psi^2 \sim \frac{k_B T}{\xi^d}$$

where $\xi \sim t^{-\nu}$ is correlation length.

**Mean-field order parameter**: $\psi^2 \sim t$

**Ratio**:

$$\frac{\delta\psi^2}{\psi^2} \sim \frac{k_B T}{\xi^d \cdot t} \sim t^{d\nu - 1}$$

**Mean-field valid** when ratio $\ll 1$, requiring:

$$d\nu > 1$$

For mean-field $\nu = 1/2$:

$$d > 2$$

But exact results give $\nu \approx 1$ (2D), $\nu \approx 0.63$ (3D), so:

$$d \geq 4$$ (upper critical dimension)

Below $d = 4$, fluctuations invalidate mean-field near $T_c$.

## Introduction to Renormalization Group

**Key insight**: Near critical point, system has **no characteristic length scale**—looks same at different magnifications (scale invariance).

**Coarse-graining**: Average spins over blocks, reducing to smaller system with **renormalized** couplings.

**Example** (1D Ising):
- Original: $N$ spins with coupling $J$
- Block: Average pairs → $N/2$ "block spins" with coupling $J'(J, T)$
- Repeat: Generate sequence $J, J', J'', ...$

**Fixed points**: Couplings where $J^* = J'(J^*)$—invariant under coarse-graining.

**Types**:
1. **Trivial fixed point** ($J^* = 0$): High temperature (disordered)
2. **Ordered fixed point** ($J^* = \infty$): Low temperature (ordered)
3. **Critical fixed point** ($J^* = J_c$): Describes critical behavior

## Fixed Point Analysis

**Linearization** near fixed point:

$$\delta J' = \lambda \delta J$$

where $\lambda$ is **scaling exponent** (eigenvalue of linearized RG transformation).

**Relevant** ($\lambda > 1$): Perturbation grows under RG → flows away from fixed point

**Irrelevant** ($\lambda < 1$): Perturbation shrinks → flows toward fixed point

**Marginal** ($\lambda = 1$): Needs nonlinear analysis

**Critical exponents** from scaling exponents:

$$\nu = \frac{1}{\ln \lambda_T}$$

where $\lambda_T$ is eigenvalue for temperature perturbation.

**Physical meaning**: "Relevant" operators determine critical behavior; "irrelevant" operators don't affect critical exponents (microscopic details).

## Universality Classes

**RG flow**: Different microscopic Hamiltonians flow to **same fixed point** if they share:
- **Dimension** $d$
- **Symmetry** (Ising, XY, Heisenberg, ...)
- **Range of interactions**

**Universality class**: Systems flowing to same fixed point have **same critical exponents**.

**Example**: 3D Ising class includes:
- Uniaxial ferromagnets
- Liquid-gas critical point  
- Binary fluid mixtures
- Lattice gas

All have $\beta \approx 0.33$, $\gamma \approx 1.24$, $\nu \approx 0.63$—microscopic details irrelevant!

**Why universality?**: Long-wavelength fluctuations dominate near $T_c$. Microscopic details affect short scales, which RG "integrates out"—only symmetry and dimension survive.

## Scale Invariance

**Scaling hypothesis**: Near critical point, correlation function:

$$G(r, t) = r^{-(d-2+\eta)} g(r/\xi)$$

where $\xi \sim t^{-\nu}$ and $g$ is scaling function.

**Homogeneity**: Under $r \to br$:

$$G(br, t) = b^{-(d-2+\eta)} G(r, t')$$

where $t' = b^{1/\nu}t$ (rescaled temperature).

**Critical exponent relations** follow from scaling—e.g., Fisher relation:

$$\gamma = \nu(2 - \eta)$$

**Physical content**: At $T_c$, system has fluctuations at **all length scales** up to $\xi = \infty$—no characteristic scale, hence power laws.

## Example 1: Landau Energy Minimization

$F = a_0(T - T_c)\psi^2 + b\psi^4$ with $a_0 = 1$, $b = 2$, $T_c = 100$ K. Find $\psi$ at $T = 90$ K.

**Solution:**

$$a = a_0(T - T_c) = 1(90 - 100) = -10$$

$$\psi^2 = -\frac{a}{2b} = -\frac{-10}{2(2)} = \frac{10}{4} = 2.5$$

$$\psi = \pm 1.58$$

**Stability check**:

$$\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial \psi^2}\bigg|_{\psi = 1.58} = 2(-10) + 12(2)(2.5) = -20 + 60 = 40 > 0$$ ✓

**Answer:** $\psi = \pm 1.58$ (stable minima)

## Example 2: Ginzburg Criterion

Estimate Ginzburg temperature for 3D Ising with $\xi_0 \sim 1$ Å, $T_c = 500$ K.

**Solution:**

Mean-field breaks down when:

$$t_G \sim \left(\frac{k_B T_c}{\xi_0^d \cdot J}\right)^{1/(4-d)}$$

For $d = 3$:

$$t_G \sim \left(\frac{k_B T_c}{\xi_0^3 \cdot Jz}\right)$$

With $Jz = k_B T_c$ (mean-field):

$$t_G \sim \frac{1}{(T_c/\xi_0)^3}$$

Rough estimate assuming $k_B T_c/(\xi_0^3) \sim 0.1$:

$$t_G \sim 0.1$$

**Temperature range**: $|T - T_c|/T_c < 0.1$ → mean-field invalid within $\pm 50$ K of $T_c$.

**Answer:** Mean-field breaks down for $|T - T_c| < 50$ K

## Example 3: Scaling Exponent Relation

Given $\nu = 0.63$, $\eta = 0.04$ for 3D Ising. Calculate $\gamma$ using Fisher relation.

**Solution:**

$$\gamma = \nu(2 - \eta) = 0.63(2 - 0.04)$$

$$= 0.63(1.96) = 1.23$$

**Experimental value**: $\gamma \approx 1.24$

Excellent agreement! (Scaling relations are exact.)

**Answer:** $\gamma = 1.23$

## Example 4: Fixed Point Classification

RG transformation near fixed point: $\delta T' = 2\delta T$, $\delta h' = 0.5\delta h$. Classify temperature and field perturbations.

**Solution:**

**Temperature**: $\lambda_T = 2 > 1$ → **relevant**

Grows under RG; determines critical behavior. Related to correlation length exponent:

$$\nu = \frac{1}{\ln \lambda_T} = \frac{1}{\ln 2} = 1.44$$

**Magnetic field**: $\lambda_h = 0.5 < 1$ → **irrelevant**

Shrinks under RG; doesn't affect critical exponents at $h = 0$.

**Physical interpretation**: Temperature drives transition (relevant); weak fields unimportant at $T_c$ (irrelevant).

**Answer:** Temperature relevant, field irrelevant

## Example 5: Universality Class Identification

System in $d = 3$ with $O(3)$ symmetry (three-component order parameter), short-range interactions. Identify universality class and predict $\nu$.

**Solution:**

**Classification**:
- $d = 3$ (dimension)
- $O(3)$ symmetry (Heisenberg)
- Short-range

**Universality class**: 3D Heisenberg

**Critical exponents** (known from experiments/theory):
- $\beta \approx 0.365$
- $\gamma \approx 1.39$
- $\nu \approx 0.71$

**Examples**: Isotropic ferromagnets (e.g., EuS), superfluid helium-4 near lambda point.

**Answer:** 3D Heisenberg class, $\nu \approx 0.71$
