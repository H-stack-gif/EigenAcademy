# Article 1.2: The Zeroth Law and Temperature

Temperature is intuitive—we know hot and cold—yet subtle to define rigorously. What is temperature? How do we know two objects have the same temperature without contact? Why do thermometers work? The **zeroth law of thermodynamics** provides the answer, establishing temperature as a fundamental intensive parameter characterizing thermal equilibrium.

The zeroth law was recognized as foundational after the first, second, and third laws were established, but it logically precedes them. Without it establishing consistent temperature, we couldn't discuss heat engines (first law), entropy (second law), or absolute zero (third law). The zeroth law transforms our intuitive "hotness" into precise mathematics based on equivalence relations and integrating factors.

This article develops temperature from foundations: thermal contact and equilibrium, the zeroth law and empirical scales, absolute temperature via ideal gas thermometry, and the connection between temperature and entropy through integrating factors.

## Thermal Contact and Thermal Equilibrium

Two systems are in **thermal contact** when energy can flow between them (but not matter). A diathermal wall allows thermal contact; an adiabatic wall prevents it.

When systems at different temperatures contact, energy flows until **thermal equilibrium**—no further net transfer. This equilibration is universal.

Key insight: thermal equilibrium is characterized by equality of temperature:
$$T_A = T_B \iff \text{systems A and B in thermal equilibrium}$$

## The Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics

**Zeroth Law:** If system A is in thermal equilibrium with B, and B with C, then A is in thermal equilibrium with C.

This is **transitivity** of an equivalence relation. Combined with symmetry and reflexivity, thermal equilibrium is an equivalence relation.

Consequence: we can define temperature $T$ assigning numbers to equivalence classes:
$$T_A = T_B \iff A \text{ and } B \text{ in equilibrium}$$

Without the zeroth law, temperature as a single-valued function couldn't exist.

## Empirical Temperature Scales

An **empirical scale** requires:
1. Thermometric substance (mercury, gas, etc.)
2. Thermometric property that varies with temperature
3. Two fixed points with assigned values

**Celsius scale** originally defined:
- Ice point: $0°\text{C}$
- Steam point: $100°\text{C}$

Linear interpolation:
$$T(°\text{C}) = 100 \times \frac{X - X_0}{X_{100} - X_0}$$

Problem: different substances give different readings!

## Absolute Temperature and Ideal Gas Temperature

The **ideal gas thermometer** provides a universal scale. For ideal gas: $PV = nRT$.

Constant-volume gas thermometer:
$$T = 273.16 \text{ K} \times \lim_{P_{tr} \to 0} \frac{P}{P_{tr}}$$

where $P_{tr}$ is pressure at water's triple point ($273.16$ K by definition).

This **Kelvin scale** has:
1. Universality (all gases agree in ideal limit)
2. Absolute zero at $T = 0$ K
3. Direct thermodynamic significance

Relation to Celsius:
$$T(\text{K}) = T(°\text{C}) + 273.15$$

## Temperature as Integrating Factor

Heat $\delta Q$ is inexact:
$$\oint \delta Q \neq 0$$

But temperature $T$ makes:
$$\frac{\delta Q_{rev}}{T} = dS$$

exact (entropy $S$).

Temperature is an **integrating factor** converting inexact differential to exact. This provides deep theoretical foundation independent of any substance.

## Thermal Expansion

**Coefficient of thermal expansion**:
$$\alpha = \frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P$$

Most substances have $\alpha > 0$ (expand when heated). Water between $0-4°\text{C}$ has $\alpha < 0$ (contracts when heated).

## Example 1: Zeroth Law Application

Three beakers tested with mercury and alcohol thermometers give:
- Mercury: A reads 25°, B reads 50°, C reads 25°
- Alcohol: A reads 23°, B reads 55°, C reads 23°

Which are in thermal equilibrium?

**Solution:**

**A and C**: Both thermometers show equal readings (mercury: both 25°; alcohol: both 23°). Even though numerical values differ, both consistently show $T_A = T_C$. By zeroth law, **A and C are in equilibrium**.

**A and B**: Different readings on both thermometers. Not in equilibrium.

**B and C**: Different readings. Not in equilibrium.

Key: different thermometers may give different numbers, but must agree on which systems are in equilibrium.

**Answer:** Only A and C are in thermal equilibrium

## Example 2: Ideal Gas Thermometer

Constant-volume gas thermometer has $P_{tr} = 400$ torr at triple point ($273.16$ K). At what temperature is $P = 520$ torr?

**Solution:**

For constant-volume gas thermometer:
$$\frac{T}{T_{tr}} = \frac{P}{P_{tr}}$$

$$T = 273.16 \times \frac{520}{400} = 273.16 \times 1.3 = 355.1 \text{ K}$$

Convert to Celsius:
$$T(°\text{C}) = 355.1 - 273.15 = 82.0°\text{C}$$

**Answer:** $T = 355.1$ K or $82.0°\text{C}$

## Example 3: Thermal Expansion

Brass rod has $L_0 = 2.000$ m at $T_0 = 20°\text{C}$. Linear expansion coefficient $\alpha_L = 1.9 \times 10^{-5}$ K$^{-1}$. Find length at $T = 120°\text{C}$.

**Solution:**

Linear thermal expansion:
$$L(T) = L_0[1 + \alpha_L(T - T_0)]$$

$$L = 2.000[1 + (1.9 \times 10^{-5})(100)]$$
$$= 2.000[1 + 0.0019] = 2.0038 \text{ m}$$

Expansion:
$$\Delta L = 2.0038 - 2.000 = 0.0038 \text{ m} = 3.8 \text{ mm}$$

**Answer:** $L = 2.0038$ m (expansion of 3.8 mm)

## Example 4: Integrating Factor

Show $\mu = 1/(xy)$ is integrating factor for $\omega = y dx + x dy$.

**Solution:**

Check if $\omega$ is exact. With $M = y$ and $N = x$:
$$\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = 1 = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}$$

Already exact! $\omega = d(xy)$.

Multiplying by $\mu = 1/(xy)$:
$$\mu \omega = \frac{dx}{x} + \frac{dy}{y} = d(\ln x + \ln y) = d(\ln(xy))$$

Also exact! Both work.

**Answer:** $\mu = 1/(xy)$ is integrating factor giving $d(\ln(xy))$

## Example 5: Absolute Zero

Ideal gas has $P_1 = 3.0 \times 10^5$ Pa at $T_1 = 400$ K (constant volume). At what temperature would $P = 0$?

**Solution:**

For constant volume: $P \propto T$

$$\frac{P}{T} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^5}{400} = 750 \text{ Pa/K}$$

For $P = 0$: $T = 0$ K.

Physical interpretation: ideal gas temperature scale has natural zero—**absolute zero** at $T = 0$ K ($-273.15°\text{C}$). Real gases liquefy before reaching this limit.

**Answer:** $T = 0$ K (absolute zero)
