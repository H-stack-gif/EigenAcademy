# Article 8.2: Critical Phenomena and Scaling

Near critical points, physical systems exhibit remarkable universal behavior. Approaching the critical temperature $T_c$, response functions like heat capacity and susceptibility **diverge as power laws**. Correlation length—the distance over which fluctuations are correlated—grows without bound. These singularities are characterized by **critical exponents** ($\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta, \nu, \eta$), which surprisingly take the same values for vastly different systems. A ferromagnet and a liquid-gas system, despite having utterly different microscopic physics, share the same critical exponents—this is **universality**.

**Landau theory** provides a phenomenological framework for phase transitions through free energy expansion in the order parameter. While capturing basic features, mean-field Landau theory predicts incorrect critical exponents (except in high dimensions). **Scaling theory** reveals that near critical points, thermodynamic functions become homogeneous with specific scaling relations connecting different exponents. Only a few exponents are independent; the rest follow from **scaling relations** like $\alpha + 2\beta + \gamma = 2$. Understanding why different systems exhibit the same critical behavior requires renormalization group theory (Article 8.5).

This article develops the phenomenology of critical points, introduces critical exponents and their physical meaning, derives power law behavior near $T_c$, establishes scaling relations between exponents, introduces Landau theory and its free energy expansion, explores universality classes and their physical basis, demonstrates critical opalescence and diverging correlation length, and examines applications to magnetic and fluid systems. The mathematics involves power laws, logarithmic derivatives, scaling functions, homogeneous functions, and dimensional analysis.

## Critical Point Behavior

**Critical point**: Special point in phase diagram where second-order transition occurs.

**Characteristics**:
- Phases become indistinguishable
- Correlation length diverges: $\xi \to \infty$
- Response functions diverge
- Power law behavior (not exponential)
- Universal critical exponents

**Reduced temperature**:
$$\boxed{t = \frac{|T - T_c|}{T_c}}$$

Dimensionless distance from critical point.

## Divergence of Correlation Length

**Correlation function** $G(\mathbf{r})$: Measures how fluctuations at separation $\mathbf{r}$ are correlated.

Away from $T_c$:
$$G(r) \sim e^{-r/\xi}$$

where $\xi$ is **correlation length** (exponential decay).

**Near critical point**:
$$\boxed{\xi \sim t^{-\nu}}$$

As $t \to 0$ ($T \to T_c$): $\xi \to \infty$ (power law divergence).

**Physical interpretation**: Near $T_c$, fluctuations become correlated over arbitrarily large distances—system loses characteristic length scale.

**Critical exponent** $\nu$:
- Mean-field theory: $\nu = 1/2$
- 3D Ising: $\nu \approx 0.63$
- 2D Ising: $\nu = 1$

## Critical Exponents

Six primary critical exponents characterize singularities:

**Heat capacity**:
$$C \sim t^{-\alpha}$$

**Order parameter** (below $T_c$):
$$\psi \sim (-t)^\beta \quad (T < T_c)$$

**Susceptibility**:
$$\chi \sim t^{-\gamma}$$

**Critical isotherm** (at $T = T_c$):
$$\psi \sim h^{1/\delta}$$

where $h$ is conjugate field (e.g., magnetic field).

**Correlation length**:
$$\xi \sim t^{-\nu}$$

**Correlation function** (at $T_c$):
$$G(r) \sim r^{-(d-2+\eta)}$$

where $d$ is dimension.

**Summary table**:

| Exponent | Quantity | Mean-field | 3D Ising | 2D Ising |
|----------|----------|------------|----------|----------|
| $\alpha$ | Heat capacity | 0 | 0.11 | 0 (log) |
| $\beta$ | Order parameter | 1/2 | 0.33 | 1/8 |
| $\gamma$ | Susceptibility | 1 | 1.24 | 7/4 |
| $\delta$ | Critical isotherm | 3 | 4.8 | 15 |
| $\nu$ | Correlation length | 1/2 | 0.63 | 1 |
| $\eta$ | Correlation decay | 0 | 0.04 | 1/4 |

## Power Law Behavior Near $T_c$

**Why power laws?** Near critical point, system has no characteristic scale (scale invariance).

**Example**: Magnetization near Curie point:

$$M(T) \sim (T_c - T)^\beta$$

Taking logarithm:
$$\ln M \sim \beta \ln(T_c - T)$$

**Log-log plot**: Linear with slope $\beta$.

**Experimental determination**: Measure $M(T)$ near $T_c$, plot $\ln M$ vs $\ln|T - T_c|$, extract slope.

**Contrast with exponential**: $f(t) \sim e^{-t/t_0}$ has characteristic scale $t_0$. Power laws have no scale—self-similar at all scales.

## Scaling Relations

Not all exponents independent—connected by **scaling relations**:

**Rushbrooke relation**:
$$\boxed{\alpha + 2\beta + \gamma = 2}$$

**Widom relation**:
$$\boxed{\gamma = \beta(\delta - 1)}$$

**Josephson relation** (hyperscaling):
$$\boxed{\nu d = 2 - \alpha}$$

**Fisher relation**:
$$\boxed{\gamma = \nu(2 - \eta)}$$

Only **two independent exponents** in $d$ dimensions! Rest follow from scaling.

**Example verification** (mean-field):
- $\alpha = 0$, $\beta = 1/2$, $\gamma = 1$
- Check: $0 + 2(1/2) + 1 = 2$ ✓
- Check: $1 = (1/2)(3 - 1) = 1$ ✓

## Landau Theory of Phase Transitions

**Free energy expansion** in order parameter $\psi$:

$$\boxed{F(\psi, T) = F_0 + a(T)\psi^2 + b\psi^4 + c\psi^6 + ...}$$

**Assumptions**:
- Expansion valid for small $\psi$
- Symmetric: only even powers (e.g., ferromagnet with $\pm M$ symmetry)
- $a(T) = a_0(T - T_c)$ changes sign at $T_c$

**Minimization**: $\frac{\partial F}{\partial \psi} = 0$

$$2a\psi + 4b\psi^3 = 0$$

**Solutions**:

1. $\psi = 0$ (disordered phase)

2. $\psi = \pm\sqrt{-\frac{a}{2b}}$ (ordered phase, requires $a < 0$ and $b > 0$)

**Critical temperature**: Where $a(T_c) = 0$

**Below $T_c$ ($T < T_c$, so $a < 0$)**:

$$\psi \sim \sqrt{T_c - T} \implies \beta = 1/2$$

**Susceptibility**: $\chi = \partial^2 F/\partial h^2|_{h=0}$

Above $T_c$: $\chi \sim (T - T_c)^{-1} \implies \gamma = 1$

**Landau prediction**: $\alpha = 0$, $\beta = 1/2$, $\gamma = 1$, $\delta = 3$

These are **mean-field exponents**—correct only for $d \geq 4$ (upper critical dimension).

## Universality

**Universality**: Systems with different microscopic details can have **same critical exponents**.

**Universality class**: Set of systems sharing same exponents.

**Classification factors**:
- **Dimension** $d$ (1D, 2D, 3D)
- **Symmetry** of order parameter (Ising $\mathbb{Z}_2$, XY $O(2)$, Heisenberg $O(3)$)
- **Range of interactions** (short vs long range)

**Examples**:

**3D Ising universality class**:
- Uniaxial ferromagnets (iron)
- Liquid-gas critical point
- Binary fluid mixtures
- Lattice gas models

All share: $\beta \approx 0.33$, $\gamma \approx 1.24$, $\nu \approx 0.63$

**Physical basis**: Critical behavior determined by **symmetry** and **dimension**, not microscopic details. Long-wavelength fluctuations dominate—microscopic details "irrelevant" (renormalization group language).

## Critical Opalescence

**Phenomenon**: Near critical point, transparent fluids become opaque/milky.

**Mechanism**:
- Correlation length grows: $\xi \sim t^{-\nu}$
- Near $T_c$: $\xi \sim$ visible wavelengths ($\sim 500$ nm)
- Density fluctuations scatter light strongly
- Scattering intensity: $I \propto \xi^6$ (Rayleigh-like)

**Observation**: First clearly documented by Thomas Andrews (1869) studying CO₂ critical point.

**Modern application**: Used to study critical dynamics and measure critical exponents.

## Example 1: Calculating Critical Exponent

Ferromagnet data near $T_c = 1000$ K:

| $T$ (K) | $M$ (A/m) |
|---------|-----------|
| 950 | $5.0 \times 10^5$ |
| 980 | $2.5 \times 10^5$ |
| 995 | $1.25 \times 10^5$ |

Estimate $\beta$.

**Solution:**

Using $M \sim (T_c - T)^\beta$:

$$\beta = \frac{\ln(M_1/M_2)}{\ln[(T_c - T_1)/(T_c - T_2)]}$$

Using first two points:

$$\beta = \frac{\ln(5.0/2.5)}{\ln[(1000-950)/(1000-980)]} = \frac{\ln 2}{\ln 2.5}$$

$$= \frac{0.693}{0.916} = 0.76$$

This is **too high** (expect $\beta \approx 0.33$)—likely not sufficiently close to $T_c$ for asymptotic scaling.

Using last two points (closer to $T_c$):

$$\beta = \frac{\ln(2.5/1.25)}{\ln(20/5)} = \frac{0.693}{1.386} = 0.50$$

Closer to mean-field value (0.5) but still above true 3D Ising (0.33).

**Answer:** $\beta \approx 0.5$ (mean-field-like, need data closer to $T_c$)

## Example 2: Scaling Relation Verification

Given $\alpha = 0.11$, $\beta = 0.33$, $\gamma = 1.24$ for 3D Ising. Verify Rushbrooke relation.

**Solution:**

$$\alpha + 2\beta + \gamma = 2$$

$$0.11 + 2(0.33) + 1.24 = 0.11 + 0.66 + 1.24$$

$$= 2.01 \approx 2$$ ✓

Excellent agreement! (Within experimental/theoretical uncertainty.)

**Physical significance**: Scaling relations are **exact**—any deviation indicates inconsistency in measurements or theory.

**Answer:** Verified: $2.01 \approx 2$ ✓

## Example 3: Landau Free Energy Minimization

$F = F_0 + a(T - T_c)\psi^2 + b\psi^4$ with $a > 0$, $b > 0$, $T < T_c$. Find equilibrium $\psi$.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial \psi} = 2a(T - T_c)\psi + 4b\psi^3 = 0$$

$$2\psi[a(T - T_c) + 2b\psi^2] = 0$$

**Solutions**:

1. $\psi = 0$

2. $\psi^2 = -\frac{a(T - T_c)}{2b} = \frac{a(T_c - T)}{2b}$

$$\psi = \pm\sqrt{\frac{a(T_c - T)}{2b}}$$

**Stability**: Check second derivative:

$$\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial \psi^2}\bigg|_{\psi=0} = 2a(T - T_c)$$

At $T < T_c$: negative → **unstable**

At $\psi = \pm\sqrt{a(T_c-T)/(2b)}$:

$$\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial \psi^2} = 4a(T_c - T) > 0$$ → **stable**

**Answer:** $\psi = \pm\sqrt{a(T_c - T)/(2b)}$ (spontaneous symmetry breaking)

## Example 4: Correlation Length Divergence

Ising magnet: $\xi_0 = 5$ Å at room temperature far from $T_c$. If $T_c = 1000$ K and $\nu = 0.63$, find $\xi$ at $T = 999$ K.

**Solution:**

$$\xi \sim t^{-\nu} = \left(\frac{|T - T_c|}{T_c}\right)^{-\nu}$$

$$\frac{\xi(999)}{\xi_0} \sim \left(\frac{1/1000}{0.3}\right)^{-0.63}$$

(Assuming $\xi_0$ measured at $t \approx 0.3$)

$$= (0.00333)^{-0.63} = 60^{0.63} = 15.8$$

$$\xi(999) \approx 15.8 \times 5 \text{ Å} = 79 \text{ Å} = 7.9 \text{ nm}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Very close to $T_c$, correlation length grows dramatically—fluctuations become long-ranged.

**Answer:** $\xi \approx 79$ Å (16× increase)

## Example 5: Universality Class Identification

System has $\beta = 0.125$ ($=1/8$), 2D, single-component order parameter. Identify universality class.

**Solution:**

**Given**:
- $d = 2$ (two dimensions)
- $\beta = 1/8$ (exact value)
- Single component (Ising-like, $\mathbb{Z}_2$ symmetry)

**2D Ising model exact results** (Onsager):
- $\beta = 1/8$ ✓
- $\gamma = 7/4$
- $\nu = 1$
- $\alpha = 0$ (logarithmic divergence)

**Identification**: **2D Ising universality class**

**Examples in this class**:
- 2D ferromagnets
- 2D lattice gas
- 2D binary alloy order-disorder

**Answer:** 2D Ising universality class
