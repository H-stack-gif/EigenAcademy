# Article 8.3: Ising Model

The **Ising model**—spins on a lattice interacting with nearest neighbors—is the quintessential model in statistical mechanics. Despite its simplicity (spins are just $\pm 1$), it captures the essence of phase transitions and exhibits rich behavior: ferromagnetism in 2D and 3D, critical points, spontaneous symmetry breaking. In **1D**, the model has an exact solution via transfer matrix method but exhibits no phase transition at finite temperature. In **2D**, Onsager's exact solution (1944) proved that phase transition occurs at finite $T_c$, one of the great achievements of mathematical physics.

The **Hamiltonian** $H = -J\sum_{\langle i,j \rangle} s_i s_j - h\sum_i s_i$ describes nearest-neighbor ferromagnetic coupling ($J > 0$, favoring parallel spins) and external magnetic field $h$. The **transfer matrix method** elegantly solves the 1D case: partition function becomes trace of transfer matrix power, reducing to eigenvalue problem. For 2D and 3D, exact solutions (except special cases) remain unavailable, but **mean-field approximation** provides insights, predicting qualitative features though with incorrect critical exponents.

This article develops the Ising model Hamiltonian and its physical interpretation, presents the exact 1D solution using transfer matrix method with detailed eigenvalue calculation, proves absence of 1D phase transition at finite temperature, introduces Onsager's 2D exact result (without full derivation), explores mean-field approximation for arbitrary dimension, derives spontaneous magnetization below $T_c$, and discusses applications to ferromagnetism, binary alloys, and lattice gases. The mathematics involves linear algebra (eigenvalues, matrix diagonalization), combinatorics for spin configurations, hyperbolic functions, and self-consistency equations.

## Ising Model Hamiltonian

**System**: $N$ spins on lattice, each $s_i = \pm 1$ (up/down, or $\pm h\bar/2$ in quantum language).

**Hamiltonian**:

$$\boxed{H = -J\sum_{\langle i,j \rangle} s_i s_j - h\sum_i s_i}$$

**Terms**:

1. **Interaction**: $-J\sum_{\langle i,j \rangle} s_i s_j$
   - Sum over nearest-neighbor pairs $\langle i,j \rangle$
   - $J > 0$: **Ferromagnetic** (parallel spins favored: $s_i = s_j$ gives $-J$)
   - $J < 0$: **Antiferromagnetic** (antiparallel favored)

2. **External field**: $-h\sum_i s_i$
   - Magnetic field $h$ favors spins aligning with field

**Energy examples** (1D chain, 3 spins):
- All up ($\uparrow\uparrow\uparrow$): $E = -J(1 + 1) - h(3) = -2J - 3h$
- Alternating ($\uparrow\downarrow\uparrow$): $E = -J(-1 - 1) - h(1) = 2J - h$

Parallel alignment lowers energy (ferromagnetic $J > 0$).

## 1D Ising Model

**1D chain**: $N$ spins with periodic boundary conditions ($s_{N+1} = s_1$).

**Hamiltonian**:

$$H = -J\sum_{i=1}^N s_i s_{i+1} - h\sum_{i=1}^N s_i$$

**Partition function**:

$$Z = \sum_{\{s_i\}} e^{-\beta H} = \sum_{\{s_i\}} \exp\left[\beta J\sum_i s_i s_{i+1} + \beta h\sum_i s_i\right]$$

Sum over $2^N$ spin configurations.

**Direct calculation**: Prohibitive for large $N$. Need clever method → **transfer matrix**.

## Transfer Matrix Method

**Key idea**: Rewrite partition function as matrix product.

**Single-site factor**:

$$e^{\beta J s_i s_{i+1} + \frac{\beta h}{2}(s_i + s_{i+1})}$$

(Split field term evenly between adjacent sites.)

**Transfer matrix** $\mathbf{T}$: $2 \times 2$ matrix with elements:

$$T_{s_i, s_{i+1}} = e^{\beta J s_i s_{i+1} + \frac{\beta h}{2}(s_i + s_{i+1})}$$

**Explicit form**:

$$\boxed{\mathbf{T} = \begin{pmatrix} e^{\beta(J+h)} & e^{-\beta J} \\ e^{-\beta J} & e^{\beta(J-h)} \end{pmatrix}}$$

where rows/columns correspond to $s_i, s_{i+1} = \pm 1$.

**Partition function**:

$$Z = \text{Tr}(\mathbf{T}^N) = \lambda_+^N + \lambda_-^N$$

where $\lambda_\pm$ are eigenvalues of $\mathbf{T}$.

## Eigenvalues of Transfer Matrix

**Characteristic equation**: $\det(\mathbf{T} - \lambda \mathbf{I}) = 0$

$$\det\begin{pmatrix} e^{\beta(J+h)} - \lambda & e^{-\beta J} \\ e^{-\beta J} & e^{\beta(J-h)} - \lambda \end{pmatrix} = 0$$

$$[e^{\beta(J+h)} - \lambda][e^{\beta(J-h)} - \lambda] - e^{-2\beta J} = 0$$

$$\lambda^2 - \lambda[e^{\beta(J+h)} + e^{\beta(J-h)}] + e^{2\beta J} - e^{-2\beta J} = 0$$

**Simplification**:

$$\lambda^2 - 2\lambda e^{\beta J}\cosh(\beta h) + 2\sinh(2\beta J) = 0$$

**Solution**:

$$\boxed{\lambda_\pm = e^{\beta J}\cosh(\beta h) \pm \sqrt{e^{2\beta J}\sinh^2(\beta h) + e^{-2\beta J}}}$$

## Free Energy and Magnetization

**Thermodynamic limit** ($N \to \infty$): Larger eigenvalue dominates:

$$Z \approx \lambda_+^N$$

**Free energy per spin**:

$$f = -\frac{1}{\beta N}\ln Z = -\frac{1}{\beta}\ln \lambda_+$$

$$\boxed{f = -k_B T\ln\left[e^{\beta J}\cosh(\beta h) + \sqrt{e^{2\beta J}\sinh^2(\beta h) + e^{-2\beta J}}\right]}$$

**Magnetization**:

$$m = -\frac{\partial f}{\partial h} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \langle s_i \rangle$$

After differentiation:

$$m = \frac{\sinh(\beta h)}{\sqrt{\sinh^2(\beta h) + e^{-4\beta J}}}$$

## No Phase Transition in 1D

**Zero field** ($h = 0$):

$$m = 0$$ for all $T > 0$

**Physical interpretation**: No spontaneous magnetization at any finite temperature—thermal fluctuations always disorder spins in 1D.

**Proof**: At $h = 0$, $m = 0$ identically. No critical temperature $T_c$ exists.

**Intuition**: In 1D, flipping single spin creates only two domain walls (energy cost $4J$). Entropy gain $\sim \ln N$ always overwhelms energy cost $\sim J$ for large $N$.

**Conclusion**: **1D Ising model has no phase transition at $T > 0$**.

Only at $T = 0$ does ferromagnetic order persist.

## 2D Ising Model

**2D square lattice**: Each spin has $z = 4$ nearest neighbors.

**Exact solution**: Found by Lars Onsager (1944) using sophisticated mathematical techniques.

**Critical temperature**:

$$\boxed{k_B T_c = \frac{2J}{\ln(1 + \sqrt{2})} \approx 2.269 J}$$

**Below $T_c$**: Spontaneous magnetization appears:

$$m(T) \sim (T_c - T)^{1/8}$$ (critical exponent $\beta = 1/8$)

**At $T_c$**: Singularities with exact critical exponents:
- $\alpha = 0$ (logarithmic divergence)
- $\beta = 1/8$
- $\gamma = 7/4$
- $\nu = 1$

**Physical significance**: First rigorous proof that phase transition occurs in finite dimension at $T > 0$.

## Mean-Field Approximation

**Approximation**: Replace fluctuating neighbor spins with average:

$$s_j \to \langle s \rangle = m$$

**Effective field** at site $i$:

$$h_{\text{eff}} = h + \frac{Jz m}{1}$$

where $z$ is coordination number.

**Self-consistency**: Magnetization from effective field must equal assumed $m$:

$$\boxed{m = \tanh(\beta J z m + \beta h)}$$

This is **mean-field equation**.

## Self-Consistency Equation

**Zero field** ($h = 0$):

$$m = \tanh(\beta J z m)$$

**Solutions**:

1. **Trivial**: $m = 0$ (always a solution)

2. **Nontrivial**: $m \neq 0$ (requires $\beta J z > 1$)

**Critical temperature**: Where nontrivial solution appears:

$$\tanh'(0) = 1 \implies \beta_c J z = 1$$

$$\boxed{k_B T_c = Jz}$$

**Mean-field prediction**:
- 1D ($z = 2$): $T_c = 2J$ (Wrong! Actually $T_c = 0$)
- 2D square ($z = 4$): $T_c = 4J$ (Overestimates exact $T_c \approx 2.27J$)
- 3D cubic ($z = 6$): $T_c = 6J$ (Qualitatively correct)

## Spontaneous Magnetization

**Below $T_c$**: Solve $m = \tanh(\beta J z m)$ numerically or expand for small $m$:

$$\tanh(x) \approx x - \frac{x^3}{3} + ...$$

Near $T_c$ ($m$ small):

$$m \approx \beta J z m - \frac{(\beta J z m)^3}{3}$$

$$1 = \beta J z - \frac{(\beta J z)^3 m^2}{3}$$

$$m^2 = \frac{3(\beta J z - 1)}{(\beta J z)^3}$$

At $\beta_c J z = 1$:

$$m \sim (T_c - T)^{1/2}$$

**Mean-field critical exponent**: $\beta = 1/2$

(Compare: 2D Ising exact $\beta = 1/8$, 3D Ising $\beta \approx 0.33$)

## Example 1: 1D Ising Eigenvalues

For 1D Ising with $J = 1$, $h = 0.5$, $T = 2$ (units $k_B = 1$), find eigenvalues of transfer matrix.

**Solution:**

$$\beta = 1/T = 0.5$$

$$\beta J = 0.5, \quad \beta h = 0.25$$

$$\lambda_\pm = e^{0.5}\cosh(0.25) \pm \sqrt{e^1 \sinh^2(0.25) + e^{-1}}$$

$$= 1.649(1.031) \pm \sqrt{2.718(0.253)^2 + 0.368}$$

$$= 1.700 \pm \sqrt{0.174 + 0.368} = 1.700 \pm \sqrt{0.542}$$

$$= 1.700 \pm 0.736$$

$$\lambda_+ = 2.436, \quad \lambda_- = 0.964$$

**Free energy per spin**:

$$f/k_B T = -\ln \lambda_+ = -\ln(2.436) = -0.891$$

**Answer:** $\lambda_+ = 2.44$, $\lambda_- = 0.96$

## Example 2: Mean-Field Critical Temperature

2D square lattice ($z = 4$) with $J = 10$ meV. Predict $T_c$ using mean-field theory.

**Solution:**

$$k_B T_c = Jz = (10 \text{ meV})(4) = 40 \text{ meV}$$

Converting to Kelvin ($k_B = 8.617 \times 10^{-5}$ eV/K):

$$T_c = \frac{40 \times 10^{-3}}{8.617 \times 10^{-5}} = 464 \text{ K}$$

**Exact 2D result**: $k_B T_c = 2.269 J = 22.69$ meV → $T_c = 263$ K

Mean-field **overestimates** by factor $\sim 1.76$.

**Answer:** $T_c = 464$ K (mean-field); exact: 263 K

## Example 3: Spontaneous Magnetization

Mean-field magnet at $T = 0.8T_c$ with $Jz = 1$. Find $m$ from $m = \tanh(m/t)$ where $t = T/T_c$.

**Solution:**

At $T = 0.8T_c$: $t = 0.8$

$$m = \tanh\left(\frac{m}{0.8}\right) = \tanh(1.25m)$$

**Graphical/numerical solution**: Intersection of $y = x$ and $y = \tanh(1.25x)$

Trying $m = 0.8$:

$$\tanh(1.0) = 0.762 < 0.8$$

Trying $m = 0.75$:

$$\tanh(0.9375) = 0.737 < 0.75$$

Trying $m = 0.73$:

$$\tanh(0.9125) = 0.724 \approx 0.73$$ ✓

**Answer:** $m \approx 0.73$ (73% magnetization)

## Example 4: 1D No Transition Proof

Show that 1D Ising model correlation length $\xi$ finite for all $T > 0$.

**Solution:**

**Correlation function**: $\langle s_0 s_r \rangle - \langle s_0 \rangle^2$

For 1D Ising at $h = 0$:

$$\langle s_0 s_r \rangle = [\tanh(\beta J)]^r$$

**Correlation length**: From $e^{-r/\xi} = [\tanh(\beta J)]^r$

$$\frac{1}{\xi} = -\ln[\tanh(\beta J)]$$

For any $T > 0$: $\tanh(\beta J) < 1$ → $\xi$ finite.

Only at $T = 0$: $\tanh(\infty) = 1$ → $\xi \to \infty$

**Conclusion**: No phase transition at finite $T$ (finite $\xi$ means short-range order only).

**Answer:** $\xi = -1/\ln[\tanh(\beta J)]$ finite for $T > 0$ (no transition)

## Example 5: Onsager Critical Temperature

2D Ising exact: $k_B T_c = 2J/\ln(1 + \sqrt{2})$. For $J = 5$ meV, find $T_c$.

**Solution:**

$$k_B T_c = \frac{2(5 \text{ meV})}{\ln(1 + \sqrt{2})} = \frac{10}{\ln(2.414)}$$

$$= \frac{10}{0.8814} = 11.35 \text{ meV}$$

Converting to Kelvin:

$$T_c = \frac{11.35 \times 10^{-3}}{8.617 \times 10^{-5}} = 132 \text{ K}$$

**Physical example**: Actual 2D magnetic materials have $T_c$ ranging from tens to hundreds of Kelvin.

**Answer:** $T_c = 132$ K
