# Article 8.1: Classification of Phase Transitions

Matter exists in different phases—solid, liquid, gas—and transitions between these phases reveal deep principles of statistical mechanics. Water boils, ice melts, iron magnetizes—these dramatic changes reflect underlying reorganization at molecular scales. But not all phase transitions are alike. Some transitions release latent heat and exhibit discontinuous changes in properties (**first-order transitions**), while others involve diverging heat capacity and no latent heat (**second-order transitions**). Understanding this classification requires analyzing how thermodynamic potentials and their derivatives behave near transition points.

The **Ehrenfest classification** characterizes transitions by which derivative of the Gibbs free energy $G(T, P)$ first exhibits discontinuity. First-order transitions show discontinuous first derivatives—entropy $S = -(\partial G/\partial T)_P$ and volume $V = (\partial G/\partial P)_T$ jump at the transition, producing latent heat $L = T\Delta S$. Second-order transitions have continuous first derivatives but discontinuous second derivatives—heat capacity $C_P = -T(\partial^2 G/\partial T^2)_P$ diverges or jumps. The **order parameter**—a quantity that distinguishes phases—vanishes in the disordered phase and becomes nonzero in the ordered phase, providing a unified framework for diverse transitions.

This article develops the Ehrenfest classification scheme, analyzes first-order transitions with latent heat and coexistence curves, treats second-order transitions with diverging response functions, introduces the order parameter concept and its physical meaning, examines representative examples including liquid-gas, ferromagnetic, and superconducting transitions, and explores how thermodynamic potential analysis reveals transition character. The mathematics involves thermodynamic derivatives, continuity analysis, singularities in functions, order parameter definitions, and stability criteria.

## First-Order Phase Transitions

**Definition**: Transitions where **first derivatives** of Gibbs free energy $G(T, P)$ are **discontinuous**.

**Key signatures**:

1. **Entropy discontinuity**:
$$S = -\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial T}\right)_P$$

At transition: $\Delta S = S_2 - S_1 \neq 0$

2. **Volume discontinuity**:
$$V = \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial P}\right)_T$$

At transition: $\Delta V = V_2 - V_1 \neq 0$

3. **Latent heat**:
$$\boxed{L = T\Delta S}$$

Energy absorbed/released during transition at constant temperature.

**Examples**:
- **Melting**: solid → liquid (latent heat of fusion)
- **Boiling**: liquid → gas (latent heat of vaporization)
- **Sublimation**: solid → gas

**Gibbs free energy**: $G(T, P)$ is **continuous** at transition (coexistence), but has **kink** (non-differentiable corner).

## Latent Heat

**Physical mechanism**: Breaking/forming intermolecular bonds requires energy input/release.

**Melting**: Breaking crystal structure requires energy → $L > 0$ (endothermic)

**Freezing**: Forming crystal releases energy → $L < 0$ (exothermic)

**Typical values**:
- Water (ice → liquid): $L_f = 334$ kJ/kg at 0°C
- Water (liquid → vapor): $L_v = 2260$ kJ/kg at 100°C
- Lead (solid → liquid): $L_f = 23$ kJ/kg at 327°C

**Why $L_v \gg L_f$**: Vaporization separates molecules completely (breaks all bonds), while melting only partially disrupts structure.

**Clausius-Clapeyron equation** (from Unit 4):
$$\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{L}{T\Delta V}$$

Connects slope of coexistence curve to latent heat.

## Second-Order Phase Transitions

**Definition**: Transitions where first derivatives of $G$ are **continuous**, but **second derivatives** are **discontinuous** (or diverge).

**Key signatures**:

1. **No latent heat**: $\Delta S = 0$ (continuous entropy)

2. **No volume change**: $\Delta V = 0$ (continuous volume)

3. **Diverging heat capacity**:
$$C_P = -T\left(\frac{\partial^2 G}{\partial T^2}\right)_P$$

Near $T_c$: $C_P \sim |T - T_c|^{-\alpha}$ (power law divergence)

4. **Diverging susceptibility**: Response functions become infinite at transition

**Examples**:
- **Ferromagnetism**: paramagnet ↔ ferromagnet (Curie point)
- **Superconductivity**: normal metal ↔ superconductor (critical temperature)
- **Liquid helium**: normal → superfluid (lambda transition)
- **Binary fluids**: mixed ↔ separated (critical point)

**Gibbs free energy**: $G(T, P)$ is **continuous** and **smooth** (differentiable), but second derivative has **singularity**.

## Order Parameter Concept

**Order parameter** $\psi$: Quantity that characterizes the degree of order.

**Properties**:
- **Disordered phase**: $\psi = 0$ (high temperature, high symmetry)
- **Ordered phase**: $\psi \neq 0$ (low temperature, broken symmetry)
- **Symmetry breaking**: Transition breaks some symmetry of Hamiltonian

**Examples**:

| System | Order Parameter | Disordered | Ordered |
|--------|----------------|------------|---------|
| Ferromagnet | Magnetization $M$ | $M = 0$ (paramagnet) | $M \neq 0$ (ferromagnet) |
| Superconductor | Gap parameter $\Delta$ | $\Delta = 0$ (normal) | $\Delta \neq 0$ (SC) |
| Liquid-gas | Density difference $\rho_L - \rho_G$ | $\Delta\rho = 0$ (critical) | $\Delta\rho \neq 0$ (coexist) |
| Ising model | Magnetization $m$ | $m = 0$ ($T > T_c$) | $m \neq 0$ ($T < T_c$) |

**Physical interpretation**: Order parameter measures extent to which system has "chosen" particular broken symmetry state.

## Liquid-Gas Transition

**Phase diagram**: $(P, T)$ plane with coexistence curves.

**First-order line**: Liquid-gas coexistence (boiling curve)
- Ends at **critical point** $(T_c, P_c)$
- Below $T_c$: First-order transition with latent heat
- At $T_c$: Second-order critical point

**Critical point properties**:
- Phases become identical: $\rho_{\text{liquid}} = \rho_{\text{gas}}$
- Latent heat vanishes: $L \to 0$
- **Critical opalescence**: Large density fluctuations scatter light

**Order parameter**: $\psi = \rho_L - \rho_G$ (density difference)

**Above $T_c$**: No distinction between liquid and gas—single **supercritical fluid** phase.

**Van der Waals description**: Captures qualitative behavior, predicts critical point.

## Ferromagnetic Transition

**System**: Magnetic material (iron, nickel, cobalt) with interacting spins.

**Phases**:
- **Paramagnetic** ($T > T_c$): Spins randomly oriented, no net magnetization
- **Ferromagnetic** ($T < T_c$): Spins align spontaneously, macroscopic magnetization

**Curie temperature** $T_c$: Critical temperature for ferromagnetism
- Iron: $T_c = 1043$ K
- Nickel: $T_c = 627$ K
- Cobalt: $T_c = 1388$ K

**Order parameter**: Spontaneous magnetization $M$

**Second-order transition**:
- No latent heat
- $M$ continuous but not smooth at $T_c$
- Heat capacity diverges: $C \sim |T - T_c|^{-\alpha}$
- Magnetic susceptibility diverges: $\chi \sim |T - T_c|^{-\gamma}$

**Below $T_c$**: $M \sim (T_c - T)^\beta$ where $\beta \approx 0.33$ (critical exponent)

## Superconducting Transition

**System**: Metal cooled below critical temperature $T_c$ (in zero magnetic field).

**Phases**:
- **Normal**: Finite electrical resistance
- **Superconducting**: Zero resistance, Meissner effect (expels magnetic field)

**Critical temperatures** (examples):
- Mercury: $T_c = 4.2$ K
- Lead: $T_c = 7.2$ K
- Niobium: $T_c = 9.3$ K
- YBa₂Cu₃O₇ (high-$T_c$): $T_c = 92$ K

**Order parameter**: Cooper pair wavefunction $\psi$ (or energy gap $\Delta$)

**Second-order transition** (Type I superconductors in zero field):
- No latent heat
- Heat capacity jump (discontinuous derivative)
- Gap opens continuously: $\Delta(T) \sim (T_c - T)^{1/2}$

**BCS theory**: Explains superconductivity through electron pairing (Cooper pairs).

## Ehrenfest Classification

**Paul Ehrenfest** (1933): Classify transitions by lowest-order discontinuous derivative of $G$.

**$n$-th order transition**: First $n-1$ derivatives of $G$ continuous; $n$-th derivative discontinuous.

**Examples**:
- **First-order**: $\partial G/\partial T$ (entropy) discontinuous → latent heat
- **Second-order**: $\partial^2 G/\partial T^2$ (heat capacity) discontinuous or divergent
- **Higher-order**: Rarely observed; modern classification uses other criteria

**Modern view**: Ehrenfest classification less commonly used. Instead:
- **Discontinuous transitions**: First-order (latent heat, coexistence)
- **Continuous transitions**: Critical phenomena (power laws, universality)

## Example 1: Latent Heat of Vaporization

Water at 100°C: $\Delta S = 6.05$ kJ/(kg·K) for liquid → vapor. Calculate latent heat.

**Solution:**

$$L = T\Delta S = (373 \text{ K})(6.05 \text{ kJ/(kg·K)})$$

$$= 2260 \text{ kJ/kg}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Each kilogram of water requires 2260 kJ to vaporize at constant temperature—energy breaks hydrogen bonds.

**Comparison**: Much larger than melting ($L_f = 334$ kJ/kg)—vaporization completely separates molecules.

**Answer:** $L = 2260$ kJ/kg

## Example 2: Clausius-Clapeyron Application

Water-steam coexistence: $L = 2260$ kJ/kg, $\Delta V = 1.67$ m³/kg at 100°C. Find $dP/dT$.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{L}{T\Delta V} = \frac{2.26 \times 10^6}{(373)(1.67)}$$

$$= \frac{2.26 \times 10^6}{623} = 3.63 \times 10^3 \text{ Pa/K}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Pressure increases by ~3.6 kPa per degree—boiling curve slope in phase diagram.

**Verification**: At 101°C, steam pressure ~101.3 + 3.6 = 104.9 kPa (close to tabulated 105.0 kPa) ✓

**Answer:** $dP/dT = 3.6$ kPa/K

## Example 3: Order Parameter for Ferromagnet

Iron below Curie temperature has spontaneous magnetization. If $M(0) = 1.7 \times 10^6$ A/m and $T_c = 1043$ K, estimate $M$ at $T = 1000$ K using $M \sim (T_c - T)^{1/2}$.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{M(T)}{M(0)} \sim \left(\frac{T_c - T}{T_c}\right)^{1/2}$$

$$M(1000) \approx M(0)\left(\frac{1043 - 1000}{1043}\right)^{1/2}$$

$$= 1.7 \times 10^6 \left(\frac{43}{1043}\right)^{1/2}$$

$$= 1.7 \times 10^6 (0.0412)^{1/2} = 1.7 \times 10^6 (0.203)$$

$$= 3.5 \times 10^5 \text{ A/m}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Near $T_c$, magnetization decreases rapidly—thermal fluctuations increasingly disrupt spin alignment.

**Answer:** $M(1000 \text{ K}) \approx 3.5 \times 10^5$ A/m

## Example 4: Heat Capacity Jump

Superconductor has heat capacity $C_n = \gamma T$ (normal) and $C_s = aT^3$ (superconducting) at low $T$. Find jump at $T_c = 4$ K if $\gamma = 0.7$ mJ/(mol·K²).

**Solution:**

At $T_c$:

**Normal state**:
$$C_n(T_c) = \gamma T_c = (0.7 \times 10^{-3})(4) = 2.8 \text{ mJ/(mol·K)}$$

**Superconducting state** (just below $T_c$): Typically $C_s(T_c^-) \approx 2.43\gamma T_c$ (BCS theory)

$$C_s(T_c^-) = 2.43(0.7 \times 10^{-3})(4) = 6.8 \text{ mJ/(mol·K)}$$

**Jump**:

$$\Delta C = C_s - C_n = 6.8 - 2.8 = 4.0 \text{ mJ/(mol·K)}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Heat capacity **increases** at transition (anomalous)—opening of gap changes density of states.

**Answer:** $\Delta C = 4.0$ mJ/(mol·K) (discontinuous jump)

## Example 5: Critical Opalescence

Near water's critical point ($T_c = 647$ K, $P_c = 22.1$ MPa), density fluctuations have correlation length $\xi \sim 10^{-7}$ m. Why does system appear milky?

**Solution:**

**Light scattering**: Requires density fluctuations with size $\sim \lambda_{\text{light}} \sim 500$ nm $= 5 \times 10^{-7}$ m.

**Near critical point**: $\xi \sim 10^{-7}$ m—comparable to light wavelength!

**Scattering cross section**: $\sigma \propto \xi^6$ (Rayleigh scattering for large structures)

When $\xi \sim \lambda$, scattering becomes **extremely strong**—system appears opaque/milky.

**Physical interpretation**: Critical fluctuations span all length scales up to $\xi$, including visible wavelengths—dramatic visual manifestation of critical phenomena.

**Answer:** Correlation length $\sim$ light wavelength → strong scattering (critical opalescence)
