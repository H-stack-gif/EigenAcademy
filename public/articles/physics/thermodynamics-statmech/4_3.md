# Article 4.3: Response Functions and Stability

When we squeeze a material, it compresses. When we heat it, it expands. These responses to external changes are quantified by **response functions**—derivatives that measure how systems react to perturbations. The isothermal compressibility $\kappa_T$ measures volume's sensitivity to pressure. The coefficient of thermal expansion $\alpha$ measures volume's sensitivity to temperature. These aren't just convenient experimental parameters—they encode fundamental information about material properties and thermodynamic stability.

Stability analysis reveals deep connections between response functions and the structure of thermodynamic potentials. For a system to be thermodynamically stable, certain response functions must be positive: heat capacities $C_V > 0$ and $C_P > 0$ (adding heat increases temperature), compressibilities $\kappa_T > 0$ and $\kappa_S > 0$ (pressure compresses materials). These stability criteria emerge from requiring thermodynamic potentials to be convex functions—mathematically, their second derivatives must have the right sign.

This article defines the major response functions, derives relations between them using Maxwell relations, develops thermodynamic stability criteria through second derivative analysis, introduces Le Chatelier's principle, and explores how response functions behave near critical points. The mathematics involves second derivatives, Hessian matrices, inequality analysis, and stability theory for multivariable functions.

## Isothermal Compressibility

**Isothermal compressibility** measures fractional volume change per unit pressure increase at constant temperature:

$$\kappa_T = -\frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T$$

The negative sign ensures $\kappa_T > 0$ (increasing pressure decreases volume).

**Units**: Pa$^{-1}$ or atm$^{-1}$

**Physical interpretation**: Large $\kappa_T$ means easily compressible (gases). Small $\kappa_T$ means nearly incompressible (solids, liquids).

**Typical values**:
- Gases: $\kappa_T \sim 10^{-5}$ Pa$^{-1}$ (very compressible)
- Liquids: $\kappa_T \sim 10^{-10}$ Pa$^{-1}$
- Solids: $\kappa_T \sim 10^{-11}$ Pa$^{-1}$ (nearly incompressible)

## Adiabatic Compressibility

**Adiabatic compressibility** measures fractional volume change under adiabatic (isentropic) compression:

$$\kappa_S = -\frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_S$$

**Relation to speed of sound**:

$$v_{sound} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{\rho \kappa_S}}$$

where $\rho$ is density. Faster sound in less compressible materials.

**Comparison**: $\kappa_S < \kappa_T$ always (adiabatic process is "stiffer" because no heat exchange to soften response).

## Coefficient of Thermal Expansion

**Coefficient of thermal expansion** measures fractional volume change per unit temperature increase at constant pressure:

$$\alpha = \frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P$$

**Units**: K$^{-1}$

**Physical interpretation**: Large $\alpha$ means strong thermal expansion (gases). Small $\alpha$ means weak expansion (most solids).

**Typical values**:
- Gases: $\alpha \sim 10^{-3}$ K$^{-1}$ (for ideal gas: $\alpha = 1/T$)
- Liquids: $\alpha \sim 10^{-4}$ K$^{-1}$
- Solids: $\alpha \sim 10^{-5}$ K$^{-1}$

**Anomaly**: Water between 0-4°C has $\alpha < 0$ (contracts upon heating)—this is why ice floats!

## Relations Between Response Functions

Using Maxwell relations and thermodynamic identities:

**Relation 1**: Connecting $C_P$ and $C_V$:

$$C_P - C_V = \frac{TV\alpha^2}{\kappa_T}$$

**Derivation**: Start from general relation (Example 4 from Article 4.2):

$$C_P - C_V = -T\frac{(\partial P/\partial T)_V^2}{(\partial P/\partial V)_T}$$

Express in terms of response functions:

$$\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V = \frac{(\partial V/\partial T)_P}{-(\partial V/\partial P)_T} = \frac{V\alpha}{V\kappa_T} = \frac{\alpha}{\kappa_T}$$

$$\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\right)_T = -\frac{1}{V\kappa_T}$$

Substituting:

$$C_P - C_V = T \cdot \frac{\alpha^2}{\kappa_T^2} \cdot V\kappa_T = \frac{TV\alpha^2}{\kappa_T}$$

**Relation 2**: Connecting compressibilities:

$$\frac{\kappa_T}{\kappa_S} = \frac{C_P}{C_V} = \gamma$$

This connects compressibility ratio to heat capacity ratio (adiabatic index).

## Thermodynamic Stability Criteria

For thermodynamic stability, certain second derivatives must be positive.

**Stability criterion 1**: Heat capacity at constant volume must be positive:

$$C_V = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_V = T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_V > 0$$

This requires: $(\partial S/\partial T)_V > 0$

**Physical meaning**: Adding heat increases temperature (not decreases!). System is stable against thermal fluctuations.

**Stability criterion 2**: Isothermal compressibility must be positive:

$$\kappa_T = -\frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T > 0$$

This requires: $(\partial V/\partial P)_T < 0$

**Physical meaning**: Increasing pressure decreases volume. System is stable against pressure fluctuations.

**Mathematical origin**: These arise from requiring Helmholtz free energy $F(T,V)$ to be convex (concave up). Second derivatives of $F$ must satisfy:

$$\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial T^2} > 0, \quad \frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial V^2} > 0$$

## Convexity Conditions and Hessian Matrix

For function $f(x,y)$ to be convex, its **Hessian matrix**:

$$H = \begin{pmatrix}
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \partial y} \\
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y \partial x} & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}
\end{pmatrix}$$

must be positive definite: all eigenvalues positive, or equivalently:

$$\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} > 0, \quad \det(H) > 0$$

For Helmholtz free energy $F(T,V)$:

$$\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial T^2} = -\frac{C_V}{T} > 0 \implies C_V > 0$$

$$\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial V^2} = -\frac{1}{V^2 \kappa_T} > 0 \implies \kappa_T > 0$$

These are fundamental stability requirements.

## Le Chatelier's Principle

**Le Chatelier's principle**: When a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it responds in a way that partially counteracts the disturbance.

**Thermodynamic formulation**: Response functions satisfy inequalities that ensure stability:

- $C_V > 0$: Heating increases temperature (counteracts cooling)
- $\kappa_T > 0$: Compression increases pressure (counteracts external pressure)
- $\alpha$ generally positive: Heating causes expansion (reduces temperature rise by doing work)

**Mathematical expression**: For perturbations:

$$\left(\frac{\partial X}{\partial Y}\right)_{constrained} < \left(\frac{\partial X}{\partial Y}\right)_{unconstrained}$$

Example: $\kappa_S < \kappa_T$ (adiabatic compression produces larger pressure increase than isothermal because temperature rises, providing additional restoring force).

## Behavior Near Critical Points

Near **critical points**, response functions diverge:

$$C_V \to \infty, \quad \kappa_T \to \infty, \quad \alpha \to \infty$$

**Physical interpretation**: System becomes "soft"—very sensitive to perturbations. Fluctuations grow without bound. This signals phase transition or critical phenomena.

**Example**: Liquid-gas critical point (CO₂ at 304 K, 73 atm): 
- Small temperature changes cause huge density fluctuations
- Compressibility diverges
- Critical opalescence (light scattering from density fluctuations)

## Example 1: Response Functions for Ideal Gas

Calculate $\kappa_T$, $\alpha$, and verify $C_P - C_V = TV\alpha^2/\kappa_T$ for ideal gas.

**Solution:**

Ideal gas: $PV = nRT$

**Isothermal compressibility**:

$$\kappa_T = -\frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T$$

From $V = nRT/P$:

$$\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T = -\frac{nRT}{P^2} = -\frac{V}{P}$$

$$\kappa_T = -\frac{1}{V} \cdot \left(-\frac{V}{P}\right) = \frac{1}{P}$$

**Thermal expansion**:

$$\alpha = \frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P$$

From $V = nRT/P$:

$$\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P = \frac{nR}{P} = \frac{V}{T}$$

$$\alpha = \frac{1}{V} \cdot \frac{V}{T} = \frac{1}{T}$$

**Verify relation**:

$$\frac{TV\alpha^2}{\kappa_T} = \frac{TV(1/T)^2}{1/P} = \frac{TV}{T^2} \cdot P = \frac{VP}{T} = nR$$

And indeed: $C_P - C_V = nR$ for ideal gas ✓

**Answer:** $\kappa_T = 1/P$, $\alpha = 1/T$, relation verified

## Example 2: Compressibility Ratio

Show that $\kappa_T/\kappa_S = \gamma = C_P/C_V$.

**Solution:**

From definitions:

$$\kappa_T = -\frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T, \quad \kappa_S = -\frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_S$$

Using cyclic relation on $(P, V, S)$ at constant $T$:

$$\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\right)_S \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right)_P \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\right)_V = -1$$

Through thermodynamic manipulations (using Maxwell relations and heat capacity definitions):

$$\frac{\kappa_T}{\kappa_S} = \frac{(\partial V/\partial P)_T}{(\partial V/\partial P)_S} = \frac{C_P}{C_V} = \gamma$$

For **ideal gas**: $\gamma = C_P/C_V$, so:

$$\frac{\kappa_T}{\kappa_S} = \gamma$$

**Physical interpretation**: Isothermal process is "softer" (more compressible) than adiabatic because heat exchange allows temperature to remain constant, reducing pressure rise.

**Answer:** $\kappa_T/\kappa_S = C_P/C_V = \gamma$

## Example 3: Stability Analysis

Show that requiring $C_V > 0$ implies $(\partial^2 F/\partial T^2) < 0$ where $F$ is Helmholtz free energy.

**Solution:**

From $F = F(T, V)$:

$$\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\right)_V = -S$$

Taking second derivative:

$$\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial T^2} = \frac{\partial(-S)}{\partial T} = -\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_V$$

From $C_V = T(\partial S/\partial T)_V$:

$$\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_V = \frac{C_V}{T}$$

Therefore:

$$\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial T^2} = -\frac{C_V}{T}$$

For stability, require $C_V > 0$:

$$\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial T^2} = -\frac{C_V}{T} < 0$$

**Physical interpretation**: Helmholtz free energy is **concave down** in temperature. This ensures thermal stability—perturbations restore equilibrium.

**Answer:** $C_V > 0 \implies \partial^2 F/\partial T^2 < 0$

## Example 4: Relating Response Functions

Express $C_P - C_V$ for water at 20°C given: $\alpha = 2.07 \times 10^{-4}$ K$^{-1}$, $\kappa_T = 4.6 \times 10^{-10}$ Pa$^{-1}$, $V_m = 18$ cm³/mol.

**Solution:**

Use relation:

$$C_P - C_V = \frac{TV\alpha^2}{\kappa_T}$$

Per mole ($V = V_m$):

$$\bar{C}_P - \bar{C}_V = \frac{TV_m\alpha^2}{\kappa_T}$$

Substitute values ($T = 293$ K):

$$= \frac{(293)(18 \times 10^{-6})(2.07 \times 10^{-4})^2}{4.6 \times 10^{-10}}$$

$$= \frac{(293)(18 \times 10^{-6})(4.28 \times 10^{-8})}{4.6 \times 10^{-10}}$$

$$= \frac{2.26 \times 10^{-10}}{4.6 \times 10^{-10}} = 0.49 \text{ J/(mol·K)}$$

**Comparison**: Measured $\bar{C}_P \approx 75.3$ J/(mol·K), $\bar{C}_V \approx 74.8$ J/(mol·K), difference $\approx 0.5$ J/(mol·K) ✓

**Physical interpretation**: For liquids and solids, $C_P - C_V$ is small (unlike gases where it's $R = 8.314$ J/(mol·K)).

**Answer:** $\bar{C}_P - \bar{C}_V \approx 0.49$ J/(mol·K)

## Example 5: Ideal Gas Compressibility

For ideal gas, show $\kappa_S = \kappa_T/\gamma$.

**Solution:**

From general relation: $\kappa_T/\kappa_S = \gamma$

Therefore:

$$\kappa_S = \frac{\kappa_T}{\gamma}$$

For ideal gas: $\kappa_T = 1/P$ and $\gamma = C_P/C_V$

$$\kappa_S = \frac{1/P}{\gamma} = \frac{1}{\gamma P}$$

**Verification**: From adiabatic relation $PV^\gamma = \text{const}$:

$$P = \frac{\text{const}}{V^\gamma}$$

$$\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\right)_S = -\gamma \frac{\text{const}}{V^{\gamma+1}} = -\gamma \frac{P}{V}$$

$$\kappa_S = -\frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_S = \frac{1}{V} \cdot \frac{V}{\gamma P} = \frac{1}{\gamma P}$$ ✓

**Answer:** $\kappa_S = \kappa_T/\gamma = 1/(\gamma P)$ for ideal gas
