# Article 5.3: Canonical Ensemble and Partition Function

The canonical ensemble describes the most common experimental situation: a system in **thermal contact with a reservoir** (heat bath) at temperature $T$. Unlike the microcanonical ensemble where energy is strictly fixed, the canonical system can exchange energy with its surroundings. Energy fluctuates, but temperature remains constant. This scenario better matches laboratory conditions—we control temperature, not total energy.

The **partition function** $Z = \sum_i e^{-\beta E_i}$ is the central object in canonical ensemble theory. It's a generating function containing all thermodynamic information. From $Z$, we extract Helmholtz free energy $F = -k_B T \ln Z$, average energy $\langle E \rangle = -\partial \ln Z/\partial \beta$, entropy, heat capacity, and all other thermodynamic quantities. The **Boltzmann distribution** $P_i = e^{-\beta E_i}/Z$ gives the probability of finding the system in microstate $i$—exponentially suppressed at high energy.

This article derives the Boltzmann distribution from fundamental principles, introduces the partition function and its properties, connects it to Helmholtz free energy, calculates thermodynamic quantities from $Z$, explores energy fluctuations, and demonstrates the power of the canonical formalism. The mathematics involves probability theory, expectation values, variance calculations, logarithmic derivatives, infinite series, and connections between microscopic and macroscopic quantities.

## System in Thermal Contact with Reservoir

**Setup**: Small system $S$ in contact with large reservoir $R$ at temperature $T$.

- System: Energy $E_s$, can fluctuate
- Reservoir: Energy $E_r$, very large
- Total: $E_{total} = E_s + E_r$ (fixed, microcanonical for combined system)

**Key assumption**: Reservoir so large that its temperature unchanged by energy exchange with system.

## Deriving the Boltzmann Distribution

Total number of microstates for combined system with system in state $i$ (energy $E_i$):

$$\Omega_{total} = \Omega_s(E_i) \times \Omega_r(E_{total} - E_i)$$

where $\Omega_s = 1$ (system in specific state) and $\Omega_r$ is reservoir density of states.

**Probability** of finding system in state $i$:

$$P_i \propto \Omega_r(E_{total} - E_i)$$

Taking logarithm:

$$\ln P_i = \ln \Omega_r(E_{total} - E_i) + \text{const}$$

Since $E_i \ll E_{total}$ (system much smaller than reservoir), Taylor expand:

$$\ln \Omega_r(E_{total} - E_i) \approx \ln \Omega_r(E_{total}) - E_i \frac{\partial \ln \Omega_r}{\partial E_{total}}$$

Define $\beta = \partial \ln \Omega_r/\partial E_{total}$. From microcanonical ensemble:

$$\beta = \frac{1}{k_B T}$$

Therefore:

$$\ln P_i = \text{const} - \beta E_i$$

$$\boxed{P_i = \frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z}}$$

where normalization constant $Z = \sum_j e^{-\beta E_j}$ ensures $\sum P_i = 1$.

This is the **Boltzmann distribution**.

## Canonical Partition Function

The **canonical partition function**:

$$\boxed{Z = \sum_i e^{-\beta E_i}}$$

where sum is over all microstates $i$, and $\beta = 1/(k_B T)$.

**Properties**:
- $Z$ is dimensionless
- $Z \geq 1$ always (sum of positive terms)
- $Z$ depends on $T$, $V$, $N$: $Z = Z(T, V, N)$
- $Z$ is a **generating function** for thermodynamics

**Physical meaning**: $Z$ weights each microstate by its Boltzmann factor $e^{-\beta E_i}$. Low-energy states contribute more.

## Helmholtz Free Energy

The **Helmholtz free energy**:

$$\boxed{F = -k_B T \ln Z}$$

This connects partition function to thermodynamic potential.

**Derivation**: From thermodynamics, $F = U - TS$. In canonical ensemble:

$$S = -k_B \sum_i P_i \ln P_i = k_B(\ln Z + \beta \langle E \rangle)$$

$$F = \langle E \rangle - T \cdot k_B(\ln Z + \beta \langle E \rangle) = -k_B T \ln Z$$

**All thermodynamic quantities** derived from $F(T, V, N)$:

$$S = -\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\right)_{V,N}, \quad P = -\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial V}\right)_{T,N}, \quad \mu = \left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial N}\right)_{T,V}$$

## Average Energy

**Average energy** (internal energy):

$$\langle E \rangle = \sum_i P_i E_i = \frac{\sum_i E_i e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z}$$

**Alternative form** using partition function:

$$\langle E \rangle = -\frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial \beta}\bigg|_{V,N}$$

**Derivation**:

$$\frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial \beta} = \frac{1}{Z}\frac{\partial Z}{\partial \beta} = \frac{1}{Z}\sum_i (-E_i) e^{-\beta E_i} = -\langle E \rangle$$

**Temperature derivative**:

$$\langle E \rangle = k_B T^2 \frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial T}\bigg|_{V,N}$$

## Heat Capacity

**Heat capacity at constant volume**:

$$C_V = \left(\frac{\partial \langle E \rangle}{\partial T}\right)_{V,N}$$

From $\langle E \rangle = -\partial \ln Z/\partial \beta$:

$$C_V = \frac{\partial \langle E \rangle}{\partial T} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\left(\frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial \beta}\right)$$

Using chain rule with $\beta = 1/(k_B T)$:

$$\boxed{C_V = k_B \beta^2 \left(\frac{\partial^2 \ln Z}{\partial \beta^2}\right)_{V,N}}$$

Or equivalently:

$$C_V = k_B \beta^2 [\langle E^2 \rangle - \langle E \rangle^2]$$

Heat capacity relates to **energy fluctuations**!

## Entropy

**Entropy** in canonical ensemble:

$$S = k_B(\ln Z + \beta \langle E \rangle)$$

**Derivation**:

$$S = -k_B \sum_i P_i \ln P_i = -k_B \sum_i \frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z}\left(-\beta E_i - \ln Z\right)$$

$$= k_B \beta \langle E \rangle + k_B \ln Z$$

**Verification**: Check $F = \langle E \rangle - TS$:

$$F = \langle E \rangle - T \cdot k_B(\ln Z + \beta \langle E \rangle) = \langle E \rangle - k_B T \ln Z - \langle E \rangle = -k_B T \ln Z$$ ✓

## Energy Fluctuations

**Variance of energy**:

$$\sigma_E^2 = \langle E^2 \rangle - \langle E \rangle^2 = \langle (\Delta E)^2 \rangle$$

Using thermodynamics:

$$\boxed{\langle (\Delta E)^2 \rangle = k_B T^2 C_V}$$

**Relative fluctuations**:

$$\frac{\sigma_E}{\langle E \rangle} = \frac{\sqrt{k_B T^2 C_V}}{\langle E \rangle} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$$

For large systems ($N \sim 10^{23}$), relative fluctuations are **tiny**:

$$\frac{\sigma_E}{\langle E \rangle} \sim 10^{-12}$$

This justifies thermodynamics—macroscopic quantities are essentially deterministic despite underlying statistical nature.

## Example 1: Two-State System Partition Function

System has two states: ground state $E_0 = 0$ and excited state $E_1 = \epsilon$. Find $Z$, $\langle E \rangle$, and $C_V$.

**Solution:**

**Partition function**:

$$Z = e^{-\beta \cdot 0} + e^{-\beta \epsilon} = 1 + e^{-\beta \epsilon}$$

**Average energy**:

$$\langle E \rangle = -\frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial \beta} = -\frac{1}{Z}\frac{\partial Z}{\partial \beta}$$

$$= -\frac{1}{1 + e^{-\beta \epsilon}} \cdot (-\epsilon e^{-\beta \epsilon}) = \frac{\epsilon e^{-\beta \epsilon}}{1 + e^{-\beta \epsilon}}$$

$$= \frac{\epsilon}{e^{\beta \epsilon} + 1}$$

**Heat capacity**:

$$C_V = k_B \beta^2 \frac{\partial \langle E \rangle}{\partial \beta}$$

After differentiation (using quotient rule):

$$C_V = k_B (\beta \epsilon)^2 \frac{e^{\beta \epsilon}}{(e^{\beta \epsilon} + 1)^2}$$

**Limits**:
- $T \to 0$ ($\beta \to \infty$): $\langle E \rangle \to 0$, $C_V \to 0$
- $T \to \infty$ ($\beta \to 0$): $\langle E \rangle \to \epsilon/2$, $C_V \to 0$
- Maximum $C_V$ at $k_B T \approx \epsilon/2$

**Answer:** $Z = 1 + e^{-\beta \epsilon}$, $\langle E \rangle = \epsilon/(e^{\beta \epsilon} + 1)$

## Example 2: Harmonic Oscillator Partition Function

Quantum harmonic oscillator: $E_n = \hbar\omega(n + 1/2)$, $n = 0, 1, 2, ...$. Find $Z$.

**Solution:**

$$Z = \sum_{n=0}^\infty e^{-\beta \hbar\omega(n+1/2)} = e^{-\beta \hbar\omega/2} \sum_{n=0}^\infty e^{-\beta \hbar\omega n}$$

$$= e^{-\beta \hbar\omega/2} \sum_{n=0}^\infty (e^{-\beta \hbar\omega})^n$$

This is geometric series with ratio $r = e^{-\beta \hbar\omega}$:

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty r^n = \frac{1}{1-r}$$

$$\boxed{Z = \frac{e^{-\beta \hbar\omega/2}}{1 - e^{-\beta \hbar\omega}}}$$

**Average energy**:

$$\langle E \rangle = -\frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial \beta} = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2} + \frac{\hbar\omega}{e^{\beta \hbar\omega} - 1}$$

First term: zero-point energy. Second term: thermal energy (goes to $k_B T$ in classical limit).

**Answer:** $Z = e^{-\beta \hbar\omega/2}/(1 - e^{-\beta \hbar\omega})$

## Example 3: Free Energy Calculation

System with $Z = 2e^{-\beta \epsilon}$ at temperature $T$. Find Helmholtz free energy.

**Solution:**

$$F = -k_B T \ln Z = -k_B T \ln(2e^{-\beta \epsilon})$$

$$= -k_B T[\ln 2 + \ln(e^{-\beta \epsilon})] = -k_B T[\ln 2 - \beta \epsilon]$$

$$= -k_B T \ln 2 + k_B T \cdot \frac{\epsilon}{k_B T} = -k_B T \ln 2 + \epsilon$$

**Physical interpretation**: Free energy has two contributions:
- $-k_B T \ln 2$: entropic contribution (2 states available)
- $+\epsilon$: energetic contribution

At low $T$: $F \to \epsilon$ (energy dominates)
At high $T$: $F \to -k_B T \ln 2$ (entropy dominates)

**Answer:** $F = \epsilon - k_B T \ln 2$

## Example 4: Energy Fluctuations

System at $T = 300$ K with $C_V = 20$ J/K. Calculate energy fluctuation $\sigma_E$.

**Solution:**

Fluctuation formula:

$$\sigma_E^2 = k_B T^2 C_V$$

$$\sigma_E = \sqrt{k_B T^2 C_V} = T\sqrt{k_B C_V}$$

$$= 300\sqrt{(1.381 \times 10^{-23})(20)}$$

$$= 300\sqrt{2.76 \times 10^{-22}} = 300 \times 5.25 \times 10^{-11}$$

$$= 1.58 \times 10^{-8} \text{ J}$$

If $\langle E \rangle \sim 1000$ J (typical):

$$\frac{\sigma_E}{\langle E \rangle} = \frac{1.58 \times 10^{-8}}{1000} = 1.58 \times 10^{-11}$$

Extremely small relative fluctuation!

**Answer:** $\sigma_E = 1.58 \times 10^{-8}$ J (tiny fluctuations)

## Example 5: Probability Calculation

Three-state system: $E_0 = 0$, $E_1 = \epsilon$, $E_2 = 2\epsilon$ at temperature $T$ with $k_B T = \epsilon$. Find probability of each state.

**Solution:**

**Partition function**:

$$Z = e^0 + e^{-\beta \epsilon} + e^{-2\beta \epsilon}$$

With $\beta \epsilon = 1$:

$$Z = 1 + e^{-1} + e^{-2} = 1 + 0.368 + 0.135 = 1.503$$

**Probabilities**:

$$P_0 = \frac{e^0}{Z} = \frac{1}{1.503} = 0.665 = 66.5\%$$

$$P_1 = \frac{e^{-1}}{Z} = \frac{0.368}{1.503} = 0.245 = 24.5\%$$

$$P_2 = \frac{e^{-2}}{Z} = \frac{0.135}{1.503} = 0.090 = 9.0\%$$

Check: $0.665 + 0.245 + 0.090 = 1.000$ ✓

**Physical interpretation**: Ground state most probable. Higher states exponentially suppressed.

**Answer:** $P_0 = 66.5\%$, $P_1 = 24.5\%$, $P_2 = 9.0\%$
