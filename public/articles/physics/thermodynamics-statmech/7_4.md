# Article 7.4: Transport Phenomena I: Diffusion and Viscosity

When a gas is **not in equilibrium**—concentration gradients, velocity gradients, temperature gradients—transport processes arise to restore equilibrium. Particles diffuse from high to low concentration (**diffusion**), momentum transfers between layers moving at different speeds (**viscosity**), and energy flows from hot to cold regions (**thermal conductivity**). These **transport phenomena** are fundamentally connected to molecular collisions and mean free path.

**Diffusion** follows **Fick's first law**: particle flux proportional to concentration gradient. The **diffusion coefficient** $D \sim \lambda \langle v \rangle/3$ depends on molecular speed and mean free path. Diffusion can be modeled as a **random walk**, with mean-square displacement $\langle x^2 \rangle = 2Dt$—the basis for Brownian motion. **Viscosity** describes momentum transport, with viscosity coefficient $\eta \sim nm\lambda \langle v \rangle/3$. Surprisingly, viscosity is nearly **independent of pressure** (density and mean free path cancel), a counterintuitive prediction of kinetic theory confirmed by experiments.

This article derives Fick's laws from random walk arguments, solves the diffusion equation using separation of variables and Gaussian solutions, introduces the random walk model and its connection to diffusion, derives viscosity from momentum transport, explores Newton's law of viscosity, examines temperature dependence of transport coefficients, and demonstrates applications to mass and momentum transport. The mathematics involves partial differential equations (parabolic type), Green's functions, Gaussian solutions, dimensional analysis, boundary conditions, and statistical random walk theory.

## Diffusion and Fick's First Law

**Setup**: Gas with concentration gradient $\partial n/\partial x$ (number density varies with position).

Molecules undergo random walk with step size $\sim \lambda$ (mean free path).

**Flux**: Consider plane at $x$. Molecules cross from both sides:
- From left ($x - \lambda$): concentration $n(x - \lambda) \approx n(x) - \lambda \frac{\partial n}{\partial x}$
- From right ($x + \lambda$): concentration $n(x + \lambda) \approx n(x) + \lambda \frac{\partial n}{\partial x}$

**Net flux** (particles per area per time):

Number crossing from left: $\frac{1}{6}n(x - \lambda)\langle v \rangle$

(Factor $1/6$: molecules moving in $+x$ direction with random isotropic motion)

Number crossing from right: $\frac{1}{6}n(x + \lambda)\langle v \rangle$

**Net flux**:

$$J_x = \frac{1}{6}\langle v \rangle [n(x - \lambda) - n(x + \lambda)]$$

$$\approx \frac{1}{6}\langle v \rangle \left[-2\lambda \frac{\partial n}{\partial x}\right] = -\frac{1}{3}\lambda \langle v \rangle \frac{\partial n}{\partial x}$$

**Fick's first law**:

$$\boxed{J = -D\frac{\partial n}{\partial x}}$$

where **diffusion coefficient**:

$$\boxed{D = \frac{1}{3}\lambda \langle v \rangle}$$

**Negative sign**: Flux opposes gradient (particles diffuse from high to low concentration).

## Diffusion Coefficient

From $D = \frac{1}{3}\lambda \langle v \rangle$ and $\lambda = 1/(\sqrt{2}n\sigma)$, $\langle v \rangle = \sqrt{8k_B T/(\pi m)}$:

$$D = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{2}\pi n d^2} \sqrt{\frac{8k_B T}{\pi m}}$$

Using ideal gas $n = P/(k_B T)$:

$$\boxed{D = \frac{k_B T}{3\sqrt{2}\pi d^2 P} \sqrt{\frac{8k_B T}{\pi m}} \propto \frac{T^{3/2}}{P}}$$

**Properties**:
- $D \propto 1/P$ (diffusion slower at high pressure—more collisions)
- $D \propto T^{3/2}$ (faster at higher temperature)
- $D \propto 1/\sqrt{m}$ (lighter molecules diffuse faster)

**Typical values** at STP:
- $D_{\text{air}} \approx 2 \times 10^{-5}$ m²/s

## Fick's Second Law (Diffusion Equation)

**Mass conservation**: Rate of change of density = negative divergence of flux

$$\frac{\partial n}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial J}{\partial x}$$

Substituting Fick's first law $J = -D\partial n/\partial x$:

$$\frac{\partial n}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(-D\frac{\partial n}{\partial x}\right)$$

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial n}{\partial t} = D\frac{\partial^2 n}{\partial x^2}}$$

This is **Fick's second law** or **diffusion equation** (1D).

**3D form**:

$$\frac{\partial n}{\partial t} = D\nabla^2 n$$

**Type**: Parabolic partial differential equation (like heat equation).

## Solutions to Diffusion Equation

**Example 1: Gaussian diffusion**

Initial condition: $N$ particles at origin at $t = 0$

$$n(x, 0) = N\delta(x)$$

**Solution**:

$$\boxed{n(x,t) = \frac{N}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}} e^{-x^2/(4Dt)}}$$

**Properties**:
- Gaussian spreading with width $\sigma = \sqrt{2Dt}$
- Total particles conserved: $\int n(x,t) dx = N$
- Peak concentration decreases as $1/\sqrt{t}$

**Verification**: Substitute into diffusion equation (straightforward but tedious).

**Physical interpretation**: Initially localized particles spread out symmetrically, with width growing as $\sqrt{t}$.

## Random Walk Model

**Random walk**: Particle takes steps of length $\ell$ in random directions.

After $N$ steps, position:

$$x = \sum_{i=1}^N \ell_i$$

where $\ell_i = \pm\ell$ (1D) randomly.

**Mean position**: $\langle x \rangle = 0$ (equal probability both directions)

**Mean square displacement**:

$$\langle x^2 \rangle = \left\langle \left(\sum_i \ell_i\right)^2 \right\rangle = \sum_i \langle \ell_i^2 \rangle + \sum_{i \neq j} \langle \ell_i \ell_j \rangle$$

Since steps uncorrelated: $\langle \ell_i \ell_j \rangle = 0$ for $i \neq j$

$$\langle x^2 \rangle = N\ell^2$$

**Time**: $N = t/\tau$ where $\tau = \ell/v$ is time per step

$$\langle x^2 \rangle = \frac{t\ell^2}{\tau} = tv\ell$$

With $v = \langle v \rangle$ and $\ell = \lambda$:

$$\langle x^2 \rangle = t\lambda \langle v \rangle = 3Dt$$

(Factor 3 from three dimensions; 1D gives $\langle x^2 \rangle = 2Dt$)

**Einstein relation**:

$$\boxed{\langle x^2 \rangle = 2Dt} \quad \text{(1D)}$$

$$\boxed{\langle r^2 \rangle = 6Dt} \quad \text{(3D)}$$

**Physical meaning**: Diffusive spreading—distance grows as $\sqrt{t}$, not linearly.

## Viscosity

**Viscosity**: Resistance to flow; momentum transport between layers.

**Setup**: Fluid with velocity gradient $dv_x/dy$ (flow in $x$-direction varies with $y$).

Faster layers "drag" slower layers via molecular collisions transferring momentum.

**Momentum flux**: Consider molecules crossing plane at $y$:
- From below ($y - \lambda$): carry momentum $m v_x(y - \lambda) \approx m v_x - m\lambda \frac{dv_x}{dy}$
- From above ($y + \lambda$): carry momentum $m v_x(y + \lambda) \approx m v_x + m\lambda \frac{dv_x}{dy}$

**Net momentum transfer per molecule**: $\Delta p_x = 2m\lambda \frac{dv_x}{dy}$

**Flux of molecules**: $\frac{1}{6}n\langle v \rangle$ (as in diffusion)

**Force per area** (momentum flux):

$$\frac{F}{A} = \frac{1}{6}n\langle v \rangle \cdot 2m\lambda \frac{dv_x}{dy} = \frac{1}{3}nm\lambda \langle v \rangle \frac{dv_x}{dy}$$

**Newton's law of viscosity**:

$$\boxed{\frac{F}{A} = \eta \frac{dv_x}{dy}}$$

where **viscosity coefficient**:

$$\boxed{\eta = \frac{1}{3}nm\lambda \langle v \rangle}$$

## Viscosity Coefficient Properties

Substituting $\lambda = 1/(\sqrt{2}n\sigma)$ and $\langle v \rangle = \sqrt{8k_B T/(\pi m)}$:

$$\eta = \frac{1}{3}nm \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}n\sigma} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{8k_B T}{\pi m}}$$

$$\boxed{\eta = \frac{m}{\sqrt{2}\pi d^2} \sqrt{\frac{k_B T}{\pi m}} \propto \sqrt{mT}}$$

**Remarkable property**: $\eta$ **independent of pressure** (or density)!

**Physical explanation**: Higher density → more momentum carriers (increases $\eta$), but shorter mean free path → less momentum transfer per carrier (decreases $\eta$). Effects exactly cancel.

**Temperature dependence**: $\eta \propto \sqrt{T}$

Experiments confirm this for moderate temperatures (deviations at very high or low $T$).

## Example 1: Diffusion Coefficient of CO₂

CO₂ at STP: $d = 3.3 \times 10^{-10}$ m, $m = 44$ u. Calculate $D$.

**Solution:**

$$D = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{2}\pi n d^2} \sqrt{\frac{8k_B T}{\pi m}}$$

At STP: $n = P/(k_B T) = \frac{1.01 \times 10^5}{(1.381 \times 10^{-23})(273)} = 2.68 \times 10^{25}$ m⁻³

$$D = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{2}\pi (2.68 \times 10^{25})(3.3 \times 10^{-10})^2} \sqrt{\frac{8(1.381 \times 10^{-23})(273)}{\pi(44)(1.66 \times 10^{-27})}}$$

$$= \frac{1}{2.62 \times 10^7} \times 235 = 8.97 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}^2\text{/s}$$

**Experimental value**: $D \approx 1.6 \times 10^{-5}$ m²/s

Factor ~2 difference (model is approximate).

**Answer:** $D \approx 9 \times 10^{-6}$ m²/s

## Example 2: Diffusion Time Scale

How long for molecule to diffuse distance $L = 1$ cm in air at STP ($D = 2 \times 10^{-5}$ m²/s)?

**Solution:**

From $\langle x^2 \rangle = 2Dt$:

$$t = \frac{\langle x^2 \rangle}{2D} = \frac{L^2}{2D} = \frac{(0.01)^2}{2(2 \times 10^{-5})}$$

$$= \frac{10^{-4}}{4 \times 10^{-5}} = 2.5 \text{ s}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Diffusion is **slow** process over macroscopic distances. This is why fans are useful—convection much faster than diffusion!

**Answer:** $t = 2.5$ s

---

## Example 3: Viscosity of Air

Air at 300 K: $m = 29$ u (average), $d = 3.7 \times 10^{-10}$ m. Calculate $\eta$.

**Solution:**

$$\eta = \frac{m}{\sqrt{2}\pi d^2} \sqrt{\frac{k_B T}{\pi m}}$$

$$= \frac{(29)(1.66 \times 10^{-27})}{\sqrt{2}\pi(3.7 \times 10^{-10})^2} \sqrt{\frac{(1.381 \times 10^{-23})(300)}{\pi(29)(1.66 \times 10^{-27})}}$$

$$= \frac{4.81 \times 10^{-26}}{6.09 \times 10^{-19}} \times 246 = 1.94 \times 10^{-5} \text{ Pa·s}$$

**Experimental value**: $\eta_{\text{air}} = 1.85 \times 10^{-5}$ Pa·s at 300 K

Excellent agreement!

**Answer:** $\eta = 1.9 \times 10^{-5}$ Pa·s

---

## Example 4: Pressure Independence

Viscosity of N₂ at 1 atm is $1.75 \times 10^{-5}$ Pa·s. Predict at 0.1 atm (same temperature).

**Solution:**

From kinetic theory: $\eta$ **independent of pressure**

$$\eta(0.1 \text{ atm}) = \eta(1 \text{ atm}) = 1.75 \times 10^{-5} \text{ Pa·s}$$

**Counterintuitive** but experimentally verified!

**Breakdown**: At very low pressure ($\lambda > L$ where $L$ is system size), viscosity does depend on pressure (molecular flow regime).

**Answer:** $\eta = 1.75 \times 10^{-5}$ Pa·s (same!)

---

## Example 5: Random Walk Distance

Particle with $D = 10^{-5}$ m²/s. After $t = 100$ s, what is RMS displacement?

**Solution:**

$$\langle x^2 \rangle = 2Dt$$

$$x_{rms} = \sqrt{\langle x^2 \rangle} = \sqrt{2Dt}$$

$$= \sqrt{2(10^{-5})(100)} = \sqrt{2 \times 10^{-3}}$$

$$= 0.0447 \text{ m} = 4.5 \text{ cm}$$

**Physical interpretation**: After 100 seconds, typical particle has diffused ~4.5 cm from starting point.

**Answer:** $x_{rms} = 4.5$ cm
