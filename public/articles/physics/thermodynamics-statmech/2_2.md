# Article 2.2: Heat Capacity and Enthalpy

When we add heat to a substance, its temperature rises. But how much does it rise? The answer depends on the substance's **heat capacity**—the energy required per degree of temperature change. Water has high heat capacity (it takes a lot of energy to warm), while metals have low heat capacity (they warm quickly). But there's a subtlety: heat capacity depends on how we heat the substance. Heating at constant volume (in a sealed rigid container) differs from heating at constant pressure (in a container free to expand).

This distinction leads to two different heat capacities: $C_V$ (constant volume) and $C_P$ (constant pressure). For gases, $C_P > C_V$ because at constant pressure, some energy goes into expansion work, not just temperature increase. Understanding this relationship requires introducing a new state function: **enthalpy** $H = U + PV$, which naturally describes energy changes at constant pressure.

This article develops heat capacities rigorously through partial derivatives, introduces enthalpy and its physical significance, derives the relationship between $C_P$ and $C_V$, and explores applications from heating processes to phase changes. The mathematics centers on partial derivatives, exact differentials, and integration of thermodynamic functions.

## Heat Capacity Definitions

**Heat capacity** is the energy required to raise temperature by one degree:

$$C = \frac{\delta Q}{dT}$$

But since heat is path-dependent, we must specify the constraint.

**Heat capacity at constant volume**:

$$C_V = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_V$$

This follows from the first law at constant volume: $dU = \delta Q$ (no work since $dV = 0$).

**Heat capacity at constant pressure**:

$$C_P = \left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial T}\right)_P$$

where $H$ is enthalpy (defined below). At constant pressure: $dH = \delta Q$.

Both $C_V$ and $C_P$ are **extensive** properties (depend on amount of substance). The **specific heat** or **molar heat capacity** is intensive:

$$c_V = \frac{C_V}{m} \quad \text{or} \quad \bar{c}_V = \frac{C_V}{n}$$

## Enthalpy: The Natural Variable for Constant Pressure

Define **enthalpy**:

$$H = U + PV$$

Enthalpy is a state function (sum of state functions). Its differential:

$$dH = dU + P dV + V dP$$

Using first law $dU = \delta Q - P dV$:

$$dH = (\delta Q - P dV) + P dV + V dP = \delta Q + V dP$$

At **constant pressure** ($dP = 0$):

$$dH = \delta Q \quad \text{(constant pressure)}$$

This is why enthalpy is the natural variable for constant pressure processes—it directly equals heat transferred.

For finite constant-pressure process:

$$\Delta H = Q_P$$

The total differential of enthalpy:

$$dH = \left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial T}\right)_P dT + \left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial P}\right)_T dP$$

Identifying the first term with heat capacity:

$$C_P = \left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial T}\right)_P$$

## Relation Between $C_P$ and $C_V$

For any substance, $C_P > C_V$. Why? At constant pressure, some heat goes into expansion work; at constant volume, all heat increases internal energy.

**General relation** (any substance):

$$C_P - C_V = -T\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V^2 \bigg/ \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\right)_T$$

This is derived using Maxwell relations and thermodynamic identities.

**For ideal gas**, this simplifies beautifully:

From $PV = nRT$:

$$\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V = \frac{nR}{V} = \frac{P}{T}$$

$$\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\right)_T = -\frac{nRT}{V^2} = -\frac{P}{V}$$

Substituting:

$$C_P - C_V = -T \cdot \frac{(P/T)^2}{-P/V} = T \cdot \frac{P^2/T^2}{P/V} = \frac{P \cdot V}{T} = nR$$

**Mayer's relation** (ideal gas):

$$C_P - C_V = nR$$

or per mole:

$$\bar{C}_P - \bar{C}_V = R$$

## Adiabatic Index

The ratio of heat capacities:

$$\gamma = \frac{C_P}{C_V}$$

is the **adiabatic index** (or heat capacity ratio). For ideal gases:

- **Monatomic** (He, Ar): $C_V = \frac{3}{2}nR$, $C_P = \frac{5}{2}nR$, $\gamma = \frac{5}{3} \approx 1.67$
- **Diatomic** (N₂, O₂): $C_V = \frac{5}{2}nR$, $C_P = \frac{7}{2}nR$, $\gamma = \frac{7}{5} = 1.40$
- **Polyatomic** (CO₂, H₂O): $C_V \approx 3nR$, $C_P \approx 4nR$, $\gamma \approx 1.33$

The value of $\gamma$ determines adiabatic process behavior: $PV^\gamma = \text{const}$.

## Temperature Dependence of Heat Capacity

For real substances, heat capacity varies with temperature. Empirical fits:

$$C_P(T) = a + bT + cT^2 + \frac{d}{T^2}$$

At high temperatures, quantum effects (vibrational modes) increase heat capacity. At low temperatures approaching absolute zero, $C_V \to 0$ (third law).

For ideal gases, classical kinetic theory predicts constant heat capacities (equipartition theorem), which works well at moderate temperatures.

## Enthalpy Changes and Integration

For processes with temperature-dependent heat capacity:

$$\Delta H = \int_{T_1}^{T_2} C_P(T) dT$$

If $C_P$ is approximately constant:

$$\Delta H \approx C_P(T_2 - T_1)$$

For phase changes (melting, boiling), enthalpy changes without temperature change:

$$\Delta H = nL$$

where $L$ is latent heat (fusion or vaporization).

## Example 1: Calculating Internal Energy Change

One mole of monatomic ideal gas ($C_V = \frac{3}{2}R$) is heated from $300$ K to $500$ K at constant volume. Find $\Delta U$ and $Q$.

**Solution:**

At constant volume, heat equals internal energy change:

$$\Delta U = Q = nC_V \Delta T = (1)\left(\frac{3}{2} \times 8.314\right)(500 - 300)$$

$$= 12.471 \times 200 = 2494 \text{ J} = 2.49 \text{ kJ}$$

**Physical interpretation**: All heat goes into increasing molecular kinetic energy (temperature). No expansion work since volume is constant.

**Answer:** $\Delta U = Q = 2.49$ kJ

## Example 2: Constant Pressure Heating

Two moles of diatomic ideal gas ($C_P = \frac{7}{2}R$) are heated at constant pressure from $300$ K to $450$ K. Find:
(a) $\Delta H$
(b) $Q$
(c) $W$
(d) $\Delta U$

**Solution:**

(a) Change in enthalpy:

$$\Delta H = nC_P \Delta T = (2)\left(\frac{7}{2} \times 8.314\right)(450 - 300)$$

$$= 58.198 \times 150 = 8730 \text{ J} = 8.73 \text{ kJ}$$

(b) At constant pressure: $Q = \Delta H$

$$Q = 8.73 \text{ kJ}$$

(c) For ideal gas at constant pressure:

$$W = nR\Delta T = (2)(8.314)(150) = 2494 \text{ J} = 2.49 \text{ kJ}$$

(d) From first law:

$$\Delta U = Q - W = 8.73 - 2.49 = 6.24 \text{ kJ}$$

Or directly: $\Delta U = nC_V \Delta T = (2)(\frac{5}{2} \times 8.314)(150) = 6.24$ kJ ✓

**Answer:** (a) 8.73 kJ, (b) 8.73 kJ, (c) 2.49 kJ, (d) 6.24 kJ

## Example 3: Verifying Mayer's Relation

For nitrogen gas: $\bar{C}_V = 20.8$ J/(mol} \cdot text {K) and $\bar{C}_P = 29.1$ J/(mol} \cdot text {K). Verify Mayer's relation.

**Solution:**

Mayer's relation for ideal gas:

$$\bar{C}_P - \bar{C}_V = R$$

Calculate:

$$\bar{C}_P - \bar{C}_V = 29.1 - 20.8 = 8.3 \text{ J/(mol} \cdot text {K)}$$

Compare with $R = 8.314$ J/(mol} \cdot text {K):

$$8.3 \approx 8.314$$ ✓

The small difference is experimental error—the agreement confirms nitrogen behaves as an ideal diatomic gas.

**Answer:** Verified: $\bar{C}_P - \bar{C}_V \approx R$

## Example 4: Enthalpy Change with Temperature-Dependent Heat Capacity

A substance has $C_P = (25 + 0.02T)$ J/(mol} \cdot text {K) where $T$ is in kelvin. Find $\Delta H$ for one mole heated from $300$ K to $600$ K.

**Solution:**

Integrate with temperature-dependent heat capacity:

$$\Delta H = \int_{300}^{600} C_P(T) dT = \int_{300}^{600} (25 + 0.02T) dT$$

$$= \left[25T + 0.01T^2\right]_{300}^{600}$$

$$= [25(600) + 0.01(600)^2] - [25(300) + 0.01(300)^2]$$

$$= [15000 + 3600] - [7500 + 900]$$

$$= 18600 - 8400 = 10200 \text{ J} = 10.2 \text{ kJ}$$

If we'd used constant $C_P = 25$ J/(mol} \cdot text {K):

$$\Delta H \approx 25(300) = 7500 \text{ J}$$

The temperature dependence adds significant energy (36% more).

**Answer:** $\Delta H = 10.2$ kJ

## Example 5: Phase Change Enthalpy

One mole of water at $100°\text{C}$ boils to steam at $100°\text{C}$. Latent heat of vaporization is $L_v = 40.7$ kJ/mol. Find:
(a) $\Delta H$
(b) $\Delta U$ (assume steam behaves ideally)

**Solution:**

(a) During phase change at constant $T$ and $P$:

$$\Delta H = nL_v = (1)(40.7) = 40.7 \text{ kJ}$$

(b) From $H = U + PV$:

$$\Delta U = \Delta H - \Delta(PV) = \Delta H - P\Delta V$$

At constant pressure:

$$\Delta U = \Delta H - P(V_{steam} - V_{water})$$

For ideal gas steam: $PV_{steam} = RT$

Water volume is negligible: $V_{water} \approx 0$

$$\Delta U = \Delta H - RT = 40.7 - (8.314 \times 10^{-3})(373) = 40.7 - 3.1 = 37.6 \text{ kJ}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Of the 40.7 kJ absorbed, 37.6 kJ increases internal energy (molecular separation against attractive forces), and 3.1 kJ does expansion work.

**Answer:** (a) 40.7 kJ, (b) 37.6 kJ
