# Article 4.5: Open Systems and Chemical Reactions

All previous articles assumed closed systems—fixed particle number. But real chemistry involves reactions where molecules break apart and recombine. Hydrogen and oxygen react to form water: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Particle numbers change even as atoms are conserved. Understanding chemical reactions requires **open system thermodynamics** where the Gibbs free energy becomes a function of all species' particle numbers: $G(T, P, N_1, N_2, ..., N_k)$.

Chemical equilibrium occurs at the minimum of $G$ with respect to the **reaction coordinate** $\xi$—a parameter tracking reaction progress. The equilibrium condition $(\partial G/\partial \xi)_{T,P} = 0$ leads to the **equilibrium constant** $K$, which relates product and reactant concentrations: $K = \exp(-\Delta G°/RT)$. Temperature affects equilibrium through the **van't Hoff equation**: $d\ln K/dT = \Delta H°/(RT^2)$, showing how enthalpy change drives temperature dependence.

This article develops open system thermodynamics, introduces the reaction coordinate formalism, derives the equilibrium constant from free energy minimization, obtains the van't Hoff equation, explores Le Chatelier's principle quantitatively, and previews the grand canonical ensemble. The mathematics involves multivariable function minimization, exponential relations, ordinary differential equations, stoichiometric constraints, and logarithmic differentiation.

## Open System Gibbs Free Energy

For system with multiple species, Gibbs free energy:

$$G = G(T, P, N_1, N_2, ..., N_k)$$

Its differential:

$$dG = -S dT + V dP + \sum_{i=1}^k \mu_i dN_i$$

where $\mu_i = (\partial G/\partial N_i)_{T,P,N_{j \neq i}}$ is chemical potential of species $i$.

For **closed system at constant** $T$ and $P$:

$$dG = \sum_i \mu_i dN_i$$

At equilibrium: $dG = 0$ for any allowed particle exchange.

## Reaction Coordinate

For chemical reaction:

$$\nu_A A + \nu_B B \rightleftharpoons \nu_C C + \nu_D D$$

where $\nu_i$ are stoichiometric coefficients.

Define **reaction coordinate** $\xi$ (extent of reaction):

$$N_A = N_A^0 - \nu_A \xi$$
$$N_B = N_B^0 - \nu_B \xi$$
$$N_C = N_C^0 + \nu_C \xi$$
$$N_D = N_D^0 + \nu_D \xi$$

When $\xi$ increases by $d\xi$:
- Reactants decrease: $dN_A = -\nu_A d\xi$, $dN_B = -\nu_B d\xi$
- Products increase: $dN_C = +\nu_C d\xi$, $dN_D = +\nu_D d\xi$

**Physical meaning**: $\xi = 0$ at initial state; $\xi$ increases as reaction proceeds toward products.

## Equilibrium Condition

Gibbs free energy change:

$$dG = \mu_A dN_A + \mu_B dN_B + \mu_C dN_C + \mu_D dN_D$$

$$= (-\nu_A \mu_A - \nu_B \mu_B + \nu_C \mu_C + \nu_D \mu_D) d\xi$$

Define **reaction Gibbs free energy**:

$$\Delta_r G = \nu_C \mu_C + \nu_D \mu_D - \nu_A \mu_A - \nu_B \mu_B$$

Then:

$$dG = \Delta_r G \, d\xi$$

At equilibrium ($dG = 0$):

$$\boxed{\Delta_r G = 0}$$

Or more generally:

$$\sum_i \nu_i \mu_i = 0$$

where $\nu_i < 0$ for reactants, $\nu_i > 0$ for products.

## Equilibrium Constant

For ideal gas or ideal solution, chemical potential:

$$\mu_i = \mu_i° + RT \ln\frac{P_i}{P°}$$

(for gases, with $P° = 1$ bar standard pressure)

Or for solutions:

$$\mu_i = \mu_i° + RT \ln\frac{c_i}{c°}$$

At equilibrium ($\Delta_r G = 0$):

$$\sum \nu_i \mu_i° + RT \sum \nu_i \ln P_i = 0$$

Define **standard Gibbs free energy change**:

$$\Delta G° = \sum \nu_i \mu_i°$$

Then:

$$\Delta G° = -RT \ln\left(\frac{P_C^{\nu_C} P_D^{\nu_D}}{P_A^{\nu_A} P_B^{\nu_B}}\right)_{eq}$$

Define **equilibrium constant**:

$$K = \frac{P_C^{\nu_C} P_D^{\nu_D}}{P_A^{\nu_A} P_B^{\nu_B}}\bigg|_{eq}$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\Delta G° = -RT \ln K}$$

Or:

$$\boxed{K = e^{-\Delta G°/(RT)}}$$

**Physical interpretation**: 
- $\Delta G° < 0 \Rightarrow K > 1$ (products favored)
- $\Delta G° > 0 \Rightarrow K < 1$ (reactants favored)
- $\Delta G° = 0 \Rightarrow K = 1$ (equal amounts)

## Van't Hoff Equation

How does equilibrium constant vary with temperature?

From $\Delta G° = \Delta H° - T\Delta S°$:

$$\ln K = -\frac{\Delta G°}{RT} = -\frac{\Delta H°}{RT} + \frac{\Delta S°}{R}$$

Differentiate with respect to temperature:

$$\frac{d\ln K}{dT} = \frac{\Delta H°}{RT^2}$$

This is the **van't Hoff equation**:

$$\boxed{\frac{d\ln K}{dT} = \frac{\Delta H°}{RT^2}}$$

Integrated form (assuming $\Delta H°$ constant):

$$\ln\frac{K_2}{K_1} = -\frac{\Delta H°}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right)$$

**Physical interpretation**:
- Exothermic ($\Delta H° < 0$): $K$ decreases with temperature (Le Chatelier: adding heat shifts backward)
- Endothermic ($\Delta H° > 0$): $K$ increases with temperature (adding heat shifts forward)

## Le Chatelier's Principle

**Le Chatelier's principle**: When equilibrium system is disturbed, it responds to partially counteract the disturbance.

**Quantitative formulation**:

1. **Temperature increase**:
   - If $\Delta H° > 0$ (endothermic): equilibrium shifts toward products
   - If $\Delta H° < 0$ (exothermic): equilibrium shifts toward reactants

2. **Pressure increase** (for gases):
   - If $\Delta n < 0$ (fewer moles of products): shifts toward products
   - If $\Delta n > 0$ (more moles of products): shifts toward reactants

3. **Adding reactant**: Shifts toward products

4. **Removing product**: Shifts toward products

All predicted by minimization of $G$ under constraints.

## Grand Canonical Ensemble Preview

For systems exchanging both energy and particles with reservoir, use **grand canonical ensemble** (Unit 5):

**Grand potential**:

$$\Omega = F - \mu N = -PV$$

**Grand partition function**:

$$\mathcal{Z} = \sum_{N=0}^\infty \sum_i e^{-\beta(E_i - \mu N)}$$

where $\beta = 1/(k_B T)$.

This naturally handles open systems and will be developed fully in statistical mechanics.

## Example 1: Equilibrium Constant Calculation

Reaction: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ has $\Delta G° = -33.0$ kJ/mol at 298 K. Calculate equilibrium constant.

**Solution:**

From $K = e^{-\Delta G°/(RT)}$:

$$K = \exp\left(-\frac{-33000}{(8.314)(298)}\right) = \exp\left(\frac{33000}{2478}\right)$$

$$= \exp(13.32) = 6.1 \times 10^5$$

**Physical interpretation**: Very large $K$ means equilibrium strongly favors products. At equilibrium, ammonia concentration is much higher than nitrogen/hydrogen.

**Units**: For this reaction, $K$ has units (bar)$^{-2}$ due to stoichiometry.

**Answer:** $K = 6.1 \times 10^5$

## Example 2: Van't Hoff Equation Application

Reaction has $K_1 = 100$ at $T_1 = 300$ K and $\Delta H° = 50$ kJ/mol. Find $K$ at $T_2 = 400$ K.

**Solution:**

Van't Hoff equation:

$$\ln\frac{K_2}{K_1} = -\frac{\Delta H°}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right)$$

$$= -\frac{50000}{8.314}\left(\frac{1}{400} - \frac{1}{300}\right)$$

$$= -6015(0.0025 - 0.00333) = -6015(-0.00083)$$

$$= 4.99$$

$$\frac{K_2}{K_1} = e^{4.99} = 147$$

$$K_2 = 100 \times 147 = 14700$$

**Physical interpretation**: Endothermic reaction ($\Delta H° > 0$) strongly favors products at higher temperature—equilibrium constant increases by factor of 147!

**Answer:** $K_2 = 1.47 \times 10^4$

## Example 3: Reaction Spontaneity

For reaction with $\Delta G° = 20$ kJ/mol at 298 K, determine if spontaneous at standard conditions.

**Solution:**

At standard conditions ($Q = 1$ where $Q$ is reaction quotient):

$$\Delta G = \Delta G° + RT \ln Q = \Delta G° + 0 = +20 \text{ kJ/mol}$$

Since $\Delta G > 0$: reaction is **non-spontaneous** in forward direction at standard conditions.

**Equilibrium constant**:

$$K = e^{-\Delta G°/(RT)} = \exp\left(-\frac{20000}{(8.314)(298)}\right)$$

$$= \exp(-8.07) = 3.1 \times 10^{-4}$$

$K \ll 1$ confirms products not favored.

**Physical interpretation**: Need to shift conditions (temperature, pressure, concentrations) to make reaction spontaneous.

**Answer:** Non-spontaneous at standard conditions; $K = 3.1 \times 10^{-4}$

## Example 4: Le Chatelier's Principle

For 2NO(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2NO₂(g) with $\Delta H° = -114$ kJ/mol:
(a) Effect of increasing temperature?
(b) Effect of increasing pressure?

**Solution:**

(a) **Temperature increase**:

Reaction is exothermic ($\Delta H° < 0$). Van't Hoff equation:

$$\frac{d\ln K}{dT} = \frac{\Delta H°}{RT^2} = \frac{-114000}{RT^2} < 0$$

$K$ **decreases** with temperature. Equilibrium shifts toward reactants.

**Le Chatelier**: Adding heat to exothermic reaction shifts backward to absorb heat.

(b) **Pressure increase**:

Stoichiometry: 3 moles reactants → 2 moles products ($\Delta n = -1$)

Increasing pressure favors side with fewer moles. Equilibrium shifts toward **products** (right side).

**Quantitative**: $K$ itself unchanged (depends only on $T$), but reaction quotient $Q$ adjusts to restore equilibrium at higher pressure with more NO₂.

**Answer:** (a) Shifts toward reactants, (b) Shifts toward products

## Example 5: Stoichiometric Constraint

For reaction A + 2B ⇌ C, starting with $N_A^0 = 1$ mol, $N_B^0 = 3$ mol, $N_C^0 = 0$, express final amounts in terms of $\xi$.

**Solution:**

Using reaction coordinate:

$$N_A = N_A^0 - \nu_A \xi = 1 - \xi$$

$$N_B = N_B^0 - \nu_B \xi = 3 - 2\xi$$

$$N_C = N_C^0 + \nu_C \xi = 0 + \xi = \xi$$

**Constraints**:
- $N_A \geq 0 \Rightarrow \xi \leq 1$
- $N_B \geq 0 \Rightarrow \xi \leq 1.5$

Therefore: $0 \leq \xi \leq 1$ (A is limiting reactant).

At equilibrium, find $\xi$ by minimizing $G(\xi)$ or using $K$:

$$K = \frac{P_C}{P_A P_B^2} = \frac{\xi / V_{total}}{[(1-\xi)/V_{total}][(3-2\xi)/V_{total}]^2}$$

Solving for $\xi$ given $K$ determines equilibrium composition.

**Answer:** $N_A = 1 - \xi$, $N_B = 3 - 2\xi$, $N_C = \xi$ with $0 \leq \xi \leq 1$

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C** - Reaction coordinate $\xi$: **extent of reaction** (how far reaction has proceeded).

**Problem 2: C** - Equilibrium: $\Delta_r G = 0$ (reaction Gibbs free energy equals zero).

**Problem 3: C** - Equilibrium constant: $K = e^{-\Delta G°/(RT)}$ (exponential relation).

**Problem 4: B** - Van't Hoff: $d\ln K/dT = \Delta H°/(RT^2)$ (positive $\Delta H°$ increases $K$ with $T$).

**Problem 5: B** - Exothermic: increasing $T$ **decreases** $K$ (Le Chatelier: heat added shifts backward).
