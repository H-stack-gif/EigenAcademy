# Article 4.2: Maxwell Relations and Thermodynamic Identities

The four thermodynamic potentials—$U$, $H$, $F$, $G$—each have exact differentials with specific forms. From Unit 1, we know that exact differentials satisfy a special condition: their mixed partial derivatives are equal. This mathematical property, when applied to thermodynamic potentials, generates powerful relationships called **Maxwell relations**. These elegant identities connect seemingly unrelated thermodynamic derivatives, allowing us to calculate unmeasurable quantities from measurable ones.

Maxwell relations are indispensable tools in experimental thermodynamics. Want to know how entropy changes with volume at constant temperature? Difficult to measure directly—but Maxwell relations show it equals how pressure changes with temperature at constant volume, which is straightforward to measure. This transformation of impossible measurements into practical ones makes Maxwell relations essential for both theoretical understanding and experimental design.

This article derives the four primary Maxwell relations from exactness conditions, develops systematic techniques for deriving thermodynamic identities, introduces the thermodynamic square mnemonic, explores applications to response functions, and demonstrates how to calculate inaccessible derivatives. The mathematics centers on mixed partial derivatives, exactness conditions, Jacobian methods, and systematic manipulation of thermodynamic derivatives.

## Exactness and Mixed Partial Derivatives

For exact differential $df = M dx + N dy$:

$$M = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)_y, \quad N = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)_x$$

**Exactness condition** (equality of mixed partials):

$$\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y \partial x} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \partial y} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}$$

This simple mathematical fact generates all Maxwell relations when applied to thermodynamic potentials.

## Maxwell Relations from Internal Energy

Internal energy: $dU = T dS - P dV$ (closed system, $dN = 0$)

Identify: $M = T$, $N = -P$

Apply exactness:

$$\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\bigg|_S = \frac{\partial}{\partial V}\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial S}\right)_V = \frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial V \partial S}$$

$$\frac{\partial(-P)}{\partial S}\bigg|_V = \frac{\partial}{\partial S}\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_S = \frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial S \partial V}$$

Since mixed partials are equal:

$$\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\right)_S = -\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial S}\right)_V}$$

This is the **first Maxwell relation**.

## Maxwell Relations from Enthalpy

Enthalpy: $dH = T dS + V dP$

Identify: $M = T$, $N = V$

Apply exactness:

$$\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_S = \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right)_P}$$

**Second Maxwell relation**.

## Maxwell Relations from Helmholtz Free Energy

Helmholtz: $dF = -S dT - P dV$

Identify: $M = -S$, $N = -P$

Apply exactness:

$$\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T = \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V}$$

**Third Maxwell relation** (most commonly used!).

## Maxwell Relations from Gibbs Free Energy

Gibbs: $dG = -S dT + V dP$

Identify: $M = -S$, $N = V$

Apply exactness:

$$\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\right)_T = -\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P}$$

**Fourth Maxwell relation**.

## The Four Primary Maxwell Relations

Summary:

| From Potential | Maxwell Relation |
|----------------|------------------|
| $U(S,V)$ | $\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\right)_S = -\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial S}\right)_V$ |
| $H(S,P)$ | $\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_S = \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right)_P$ |
| $F(T,V)$ | $\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T = \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V$ |
| $G(T,P)$ | $\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\right)_T = -\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P$ |

**Pattern recognition**: Each relation connects a pair of conjugate variables. The signs follow from the signs in the differential forms.

## Applications to Derive Unmeasurable Quantities

Maxwell relations transform difficult-to-measure derivatives into accessible ones.

**Example**: How does entropy change with volume at constant temperature?

$$\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T = \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V$$

The right side is straightforward: measure pressure as temperature changes at fixed volume. The left side would require measuring entropy (difficult) as volume changes.

**Example**: For ideal gas with $P = nRT/V$:

$$\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T = \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V = \frac{\partial}{\partial T}\left(\frac{nRT}{V}\right) = \frac{nR}{V}$$

This matches our result from Unit 3: $\Delta S = nR\ln(V_f/V_i)$ gives $dS/dV = nR/V$ ✓

## Thermodynamic Square (Mnemonic Device)

The **thermodynamic square** is a mnemonic for remembering Maxwell relations:

```
        V           P
         \         /
          \       /
           \     /
            \   /
             \ /
              X
             / \
            /   \
           /     \
          /       \
         /         \
        S           T
```

**Rules**:
1. Diagonals are conjugate pairs: $(V,P)$ and $(S,T)$
2. Going around square gives Maxwell relations
3. Adjacent corners connected by thermodynamic potential

This visual tool helps reconstruct Maxwell relations quickly.

## Response Functions from Maxwell Relations

**Coefficient of thermal expansion**:

$$\alpha = \frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P$$

Using fourth Maxwell relation:

$$\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\right)_T = -\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P = -V\alpha$$

**Physical meaning**: Entropy decreases with pressure at constant temperature (compression reduces disorder) proportional to thermal expansion coefficient.

## Systematic Derivation Techniques

To derive thermodynamic identity:

**Method 1**: Use Maxwell relations directly

**Method 2**: Use Jacobian transformations
$$\left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\right)_z = \frac{\partial(x,z)}{\partial(y,z)}$$

**Method 3**: Chain rule and cyclic relation
$$\left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\right)_z \left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial z}\right)_x \left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\right)_y = -1$$

**Example derivation**: Show $(\partial U/\partial V)_T = T(\partial P/\partial T)_V - P$

Starting with $dU = T dS - P dV$:

$$\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T = T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T - P$$

Use Maxwell relation: $(\partial S/\partial V)_T = (\partial P/\partial T)_V$:

$$\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T = T\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V - P$$

This is a fundamental relation for internal energy's volume dependence!

## Example 1: Applying Third Maxwell Relation

For ideal gas, verify the Maxwell relation $(\partial S/\partial V)_T = (\partial P/\partial T)_V$.

**Solution:**

**Left side**: From $S = nC_V\ln T + nR\ln V + \text{const}$:

$$\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T = \frac{nR}{V}$$

**Right side**: From $P = nRT/V$:

$$\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V = \frac{nR}{V}$$

**Verification**: Both sides equal $nR/V$ ✓

**Answer:** Maxwell relation verified for ideal gas

## Example 2: Entropy Change with Pressure

For substance with thermal expansion $\alpha = (1/V)(\partial V/\partial T)_P$, find $(\partial S/\partial P)_T$ in terms of $\alpha$.

**Solution:**

Fourth Maxwell relation:

$$\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\right)_T = -\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P$$

Using definition of $\alpha$:

$$\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P = V\alpha$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\right)_T = -V\alpha}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Increasing pressure at constant temperature decreases entropy (compression reduces disorder). Effect is larger for materials with high thermal expansion (molecules have more room to respond).

**Answer:** $(\partial S/\partial P)_T = -V\alpha$

---

## Example 3: Internal Energy Volume Dependence

Show for ideal gas: $(\partial U/\partial V)_T = 0$ using Maxwell relations.

**Solution:**

General relation (derived above):

$$\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T = T\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V - P$$

For ideal gas: $P = nRT/V$

$$\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V = \frac{nR}{V} = \frac{P}{T}$$

Substituting:

$$\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T = T \cdot \frac{P}{T} - P = P - P = 0$$

This is **Joule's law**: ideal gas internal energy depends only on temperature, not volume.

**Answer:** $(\partial U/\partial V)_T = 0$ for ideal gas

## Example 4: Relating Heat Capacities

Use Maxwell relations to show: $C_P - C_V = -T(\partial P/\partial T)_V^2 / (\partial P/\partial V)_T$.

**Solution:**

Start with definitions:
- $C_V = (\partial U/\partial T)_V$
- $C_P = (\partial H/\partial T)_P$

From $H = U + PV$:

$$C_P = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_P + P\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P$$

Express $(\partial U/\partial T)_P$ using chain rule:

$$\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_P = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_V + \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P$$

Using $(\partial U/\partial V)_T = T(\partial P/\partial T)_V - P$:

$$C_P = C_V + \left[T\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V - P\right]\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P + P\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P$$

$$= C_V + T\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P$$

Using cyclic relation: $(\partial P/\partial T)_V (\partial T/\partial V)_P (\partial V/\partial P)_T = -1$:

$$\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P = -\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T$$

Therefore:

$$C_P - C_V = -T\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V^2 \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T = -T\frac{(\partial P/\partial T)_V^2}{(\partial P/\partial V)_T}$$

**Answer:** Derived general relation between $C_P$ and $C_V$

## Example 5: Joule-Thomson Coefficient from Maxwell Relations

Express Joule-Thomson coefficient $\mu_{JT} = (\partial T/\partial P)_H$ in terms of measurable quantities.

**Solution:**

Using implicit differentiation on $H = H(T,P)$:

$$dH = \left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial T}\right)_P dT + \left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial P}\right)_T dP$$

At constant $H$ ($dH = 0$):

$$0 = C_P dT + \left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial P}\right)_T dP$$

Therefore:

$$\mu_{JT} = \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_H = -\frac{1}{C_P}\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial P}\right)_T$$

From $H = U + PV$:

$$\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial P}\right)_T = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial P}\right)_T + P\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T + V$$

Using thermodynamic identity and Maxwell relations (lengthy derivation):

$$\mu_{JT} = \frac{1}{C_P}\left[T\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P - V\right] = \frac{V}{C_P}(T\alpha - 1)$$

where $\alpha = (1/V)(\partial V/\partial T)_P$ is thermal expansion coefficient.

**Answer:** $\mu_{JT} = \frac{V}{C_P}(T\alpha - 1)$
