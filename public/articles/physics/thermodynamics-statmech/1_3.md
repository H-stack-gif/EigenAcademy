# Article 1.3: Equations of State

Every thermodynamic system has an **equation of state**—a mathematical relationship connecting macroscopic variables. For simple systems, this relates pressure $P$, volume $V$, and temperature $T$: $f(P, V, T) = 0$. The equation of state encodes molecular properties into macroscopic behavior, bridging microscopic physics and thermodynamics.

The simplest is the ideal gas law $PV = nRT$, accurate for dilute gases. Real substances deviate—molecules occupy volume and interact. Understanding deviations requires sophisticated equations like van der Waals, which corrects for molecular size and intermolecular forces. Near critical points where liquid and gas become indistinguishable, equations reveal universal scaling through the law of corresponding states.

This article explores equations from ideal gas through real gas corrections, critical phenomena, and dimensionless scaling, using multivariable calculus—Taylor expansions, critical point analysis, dimensional analysis.

## Ideal Gas Equation of State

$$PV = nRT$$

where $R = 8.314$ J/(mol} \cdot text {K) or using molecules $N = nN_A$:
$$PV = Nk_BT$$

where $k_B = 1.381 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K and $R = N_A k_B$.

Emerges from:
1. Boyle's law ($T, n$ const): $PV = \text{const}$
2. Charles's law ($P, n$ const): $V/T = \text{const}$
3. Avogadro's law ($P, T$ const): $V/n = \text{const}$

Assumptions: point particles, no intermolecular forces, elastic collisions, random motion. Excellent at low pressure (molecules far apart) and high temperature (kinetic energy dominates).

## Van der Waals Equation

Real gases deviate due to:
1. Finite molecular volume
2. Intermolecular forces

**Van der Waals equation**:
$$\left(P + \frac{an^2}{V^2}\right)(V - nb) = nRT$$

or per mole:
$$\left(P + \frac{a}{V_m^2}\right)(V_m - b) = RT$$

Parameters:
- $a$: attractive force strength
- $b$: excluded volume per mole

Common values (atm, L, mol):
- N₂: $a = 1.370$, $b = 0.0387$
- CO₂: $a = 3.658$, $b = 0.0429$

## Virial Expansion

**Compressibility factor**:
$$Z = \frac{PV}{nRT}$$

For ideal gas, $Z = 1$. **Virial equation**:
$$Z = 1 + \frac{B(T)}{V_m} + \frac{C(T)}{V_m^2} + \cdot \text{s}$$

where $B(T)$ is second virial coefficient, $C(T)$ is third, etc.

Van der Waals gives:
$$B(T) = b - \frac{a}{RT}, \quad C(T) = b^2$$

**Boyle temperature** $T_B$ where $B = 0$:
$$T_B = \frac{a}{Rb}$$

At $T_B$, gas behaves ideally to first order in density.

## Critical Points and Critical Constants

At critical point $(P_c, V_c, T_c)$:
$$\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\right)_T = 0, \quad \left(\frac{\partial^2 P}{\partial V^2}\right)_T = 0$$

For van der Waals:
$$V_c = 3b, \quad T_c = \frac{8a}{27Rb}, \quad P_c = \frac{a}{27b^2}$$

Critical compressibility:
$$Z_c = \frac{P_c V_c}{RT_c} = \frac{3}{8} = 0.375$$

Real substances: $Z_c \approx 0.23$ to $0.31$.

## Reduced Variables and Law of Corresponding States

**Reduced variables**:
$$P_r = \frac{P}{P_c}, \quad V_r = \frac{V_m}{V_c}, \quad T_r = \frac{T}{T_c}$$

Van der Waals becomes:
$$\left(P_r + \frac{3}{V_r^2}\right)\left(V_r - \frac{1}{3}\right) = \frac{8}{3}T_r$$

**Universal**—independent of $a$ and $b$! **Law of corresponding states**: substances at same reduced conditions behave similarly.

## Example 1: Ideal Gas Volume

Calculate volume of $n = 3.0$ moles helium at $T = 350$ K, $P = 2.0 \times 10^5$ Pa.

**Solution:**

$$V = \frac{nRT}{P} = \frac{(3.0)(8.314)(350)}{2.0 \times 10^5} = 0.0436 \text{ m}^3 = 43.6 \text{ L}$$

**Answer:** $V = 43.6$ L

---

## Example 2: Van der Waals vs. Ideal

Calculate pressure of $n = 1.0$ mol CO₂ in $V = 5.0$ L at $T = 300$ K using:
(a) Ideal gas (b) Van der Waals

For CO₂: $a = 3.658$ atm} \cdot text {L²/mol², $b = 0.0429$ L/mol

**Solution:**

(a) Ideal:
$$P = \frac{nRT}{V} = \frac{(1.0)(0.08206)(300)}{5.0} = 4.92 \text{ atm}$$

(b) Van der Waals:
$$P = \frac{nRT}{V - nb} - \frac{an^2}{V^2}$$
$$= \frac{24.618}{4.9571} - \frac{3.658}{25} = 4.967 - 0.146 = 4.82 \text{ atm}$$

Van der Waals gives **lower pressure** (attractions dominate).

**Answer:** (a) 4.92 atm, (b) 4.82 atm

---

## Example 3: Critical Constants

For nitrogen ($a = 1.370$, $b = 0.0387$), find critical temperature.

**Solution:**

$$T_c = \frac{8a}{27Rb} = \frac{8(1.370)}{27(0.08206)(0.0387)} = 128 \text{ K}$$

Experimental: $126.2$ K (excellent agreement!)

**Answer:** $T_c = 128$ K

---

## Example 4: Boyle Temperature

For CO₂ ($a = 3.658$, $b = 0.0429$), find Boyle temperature.

**Solution:**

$$T_B = \frac{a}{Rb} = \frac{3.658}{(0.08206)(0.0429)} = 1039 \text{ K}$$

At this temperature, attractive and repulsive effects cancel to first order.

**Answer:** $T_B = 1039$ K

---

## Example 5: Reduced Variables

For N₂ at $T = 200$ K, $P = 40$ atm, express in reduced variables. Use $T_c = 126.2$ K, $P_c = 33.5$ atm.

**Solution:**

$$T_r = \frac{200}{126.2} = 1.585, \quad P_r = \frac{40}{33.5} = 1.194$$

Both above critical point.

**Answer:** $T_r = 1.59$, $P_r = 1.19$
