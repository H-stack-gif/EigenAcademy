# Article 4.1: Thermodynamic Potentials and Legendre Transforms

Internal energy $U$ is the fundamental thermodynamic potential—it contains all thermodynamic information about a system. But $U$ has natural variables $(S, V, N)$: entropy, volume, and particle number. These aren't always convenient. In a laboratory, we typically control temperature and pressure, not entropy and volume. Working with $U(S, V, N)$ when we measure $(T, P, N)$ is like trying to fit a square peg in a round hole.

The solution is remarkably elegant: **Legendre transformations** systematically convert between natural variable sets, creating new thermodynamic potentials optimized for different experimental conditions. From internal energy $U(S, V, N)$, we construct enthalpy $H(S, P, N)$ for constant pressure, Helmholtz free energy $F(T, V, N)$ for constant temperature, and Gibbs free energy $G(T, P, N)$ for constant temperature and pressure. Each potential is a complete thermodynamic description—you can derive all properties from any of them.

This article develops Legendre transformation theory, derives the four fundamental thermodynamic potentials, identifies their natural variables and exact differential forms, and explores when to use each potential. The mathematics involves multivariable calculus, exact differentials, convex function theory, and the geometric interpretation of Legendre transforms as tangent plane constructions.

## Internal Energy: The Fundamental Potential

**Internal energy** $U$ is the starting point. For a simple system:

$$U = U(S, V, N)$$

Its exact differential:

$$dU = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial S}\right)_{V,N} dS + \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_{S,N} dV + \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial N}\right)_{S,V} dN$$

Identifying partial derivatives with physical quantities:

$$\boxed{dU = T dS - P dV + \mu dN}$$

where:
- $T = (\partial U/\partial S)_{V,N}$ = temperature
- $-P = (\partial U/\partial V)_{S,N}$ = negative pressure
- $\mu = (\partial U/\partial N)_{S,V}$ = chemical potential

The natural variables for $U$ are $(S, V, N)$—these are the independent variables that make $U$ easiest to work with.

## Legendre Transformation: The Mathematical Framework

A **Legendre transformation** exchanges an independent variable with its conjugate derivative. For function $f(x)$, the transform replaces $x$ with $p = df/dx$:

$$g(p) = px - f(x)$$

where $x = x(p)$ is determined by $p = df/dx$.

**Geometric interpretation**: $g(p)$ is the $y$-intercept of the tangent line to $f(x)$ with slope $p$.

**Key properties**:
1. Transform of transform returns original: $f(x) = px - g(p)$
2. $dg = x dp + p dx - df = x dp$ (using $df = p dx$)
3. So: $\partial g/\partial p = x$ (variable conjugacy)

**Convexity requirement**: Legendre transforms work for convex (or concave) functions. Internal energy is convex in extensive variables.

## Enthalpy: Constant Pressure Conditions

To work at constant pressure instead of constant volume, perform Legendre transform on $V$:

$$H = U + PV$$

This is **enthalpy**. Its differential:

$$dH = dU + P dV + V dP = (T dS - P dV + \mu dN) + P dV + V dP$$

$$\boxed{dH = T dS + V dP + \mu dN}$$

Natural variables: $H = H(S, P, N)$

Conjugate pairs:
- $(S, T)$: both appear
- $(V, P)$: transformed from $V$ to $P$
- $(N, \mu)$: both appear

**When to use**: Constant pressure processes (most common in chemistry and engineering). At constant $P$: $dH = T dS$ for reversible process, so $\Delta H = Q_P$ (heat at constant pressure).

## Helmholtz Free Energy: Constant Temperature

To work at constant temperature instead of constant entropy:

$$F = U - TS$$

This is **Helmholtz free energy**. Its differential:

$$dF = dU - T dS - S dT = (T dS - P dV + \mu dN) - T dS - S dT$$

$$\boxed{dF = -S dT - P dV + \mu dN}$$

Natural variables: $F = F(T, V, N)$

**When to use**: Constant temperature and volume (canonical ensemble in statistical mechanics). At constant $T$ and $V$: spontaneous processes decrease $F$ (equilibrium at minimum $F$).

**Physical interpretation**: $F$ is available energy to do work at constant temperature. Maximum work: $W_{max} = -\Delta F$ (isothermal process).

## Gibbs Free Energy: Constant Temperature and Pressure

Perform both transforms ($S \to T$ and $V \to P$):

$$G = U - TS + PV = H - TS = F + PV$$

This is **Gibbs free energy**. Its differential:

$$dG = dH - T dS - S dT = (T dS + V dP + \mu dN) - T dS - S dT$$

$$\boxed{dG = -S dT + V dP + \mu dN}$$

Natural variables: $G = G(T, P, N)$

**When to use**: Constant temperature and pressure (most chemistry and biology). These are easily controlled laboratory conditions. Spontaneous processes at constant $T$ and $P$ decrease $G$. Equilibrium occurs at minimum $G$.

**Physical interpretation**: 
- $G$ determines spontaneity and equilibrium
- $\mu = (\partial G/\partial N)_{T,P}$ = chemical potential
- Phase transitions occur when $G$ of phases are equal

## Summary of Potentials

| Potential | Definition | Natural Variables | Differential Form |
|-----------|------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Internal energy $U$ | Fundamental | $(S, V, N)$ | $dU = T dS - P dV + \mu dN$ |
| Enthalpy $H$ | $U + PV$ | $(S, P, N)$ | $dH = T dS + V dP + \mu dN$ |
| Helmholtz $F$ | $U - TS$ | $(T, V, N)$ | $dF = -S dT - P dV + \mu dN$ |
| Gibbs $G$ | $U - TS + PV$ | $(T, P, N)$ | $dG = -S dT + V dP + \mu dN$ |

**Conjugate pairs**:
- $(S, T)$: entropy and temperature
- $(V, P)$: volume and pressure
- $(N, \mu)$: particle number and chemical potential

## Variable Conjugacy

From exact differentials, we identify conjugate relationships:

From $dU = T dS - P dV + \mu dN$:
- $T = (\partial U/\partial S)_{V,N}$
- $-P = (\partial U/\partial V)_{S,N}$
- $\mu = (\partial U/\partial N)_{S,V}$

From $dG = -S dT + V dP + \mu dN$:
- $-S = (\partial G/\partial T)_{P,N}$
- $V = (\partial G/\partial P)_{T,N}$
- $\mu = (\partial G/\partial N)_{T,P}$

These relationships allow us to extract any thermodynamic quantity from the appropriate potential.

## Which Potential to Use?

**Decision tree**:

1. **Constant $T$ and $P$** (most common): Use $G$
   - Chemical reactions
   - Phase transitions
   - Biological systems

2. **Constant $T$ and $V$**: Use $F$
   - Statistical mechanics (canonical ensemble)
   - Fixed volume chemistry

3. **Constant $S$ and $P$**: Use $H$
   - Adiabatic flow processes
   - Jet engines

4. **Constant $S$ and $V$**: Use $U$
   - Isolated systems
   - Fundamental theory

**Practical tip**: Use the potential whose natural variables match your controlled variables.

## Example 1: Deriving Enthalpy Differential

Starting from $H = U + PV$, derive $dH$ and identify its natural variables.

**Solution:**

Take differential:

$$dH = dU + d(PV) = dU + P dV + V dP$$

Substitute $dU = T dS - P dV + \mu dN$:

$$dH = (T dS - P dV + \mu dN) + P dV + V dP$$

$$= T dS + V dP + \mu dN$$

Compare with general form $dH = (\partial H/\partial S)_{P,N} dS + (\partial H/\partial P)_{S,N} dP + (\partial H/\partial N)_{S,P} dN$:

**Natural variables**: $(S, P, N)$

**Conjugate derivatives**:
- $T = (\partial H/\partial S)_{P,N}$
- $V = (\partial H/\partial P)_{S,N}$
- $\mu = (\partial H/\partial N)_{S,P}$

**Answer:** $dH = T dS + V dP + \mu dN$; natural variables $(S, P, N)$

## Example 2: Gibbs Free Energy Change

For isothermal, isobaric process, show $\Delta G = W_{non-PV}$ where $W_{non-PV}$ is non-expansion work.

**Solution:**

At constant $T$ and $P$:

$$dG = -S dT + V dP + \mu dN$$

With $dT = 0$ and $dP = 0$:

$$dG = \mu dN$$

For closed system ($dN = 0$):

$$dG = 0 \quad \text{(reversible, no other work)}$$

**With non-PV work** (electrical, magnetic, etc.):

First law: $dU = \delta Q - P dV + \delta W_{non-PV}$

At constant $T$ and $P$:

$$dG = dU - T dS + P dV = \delta Q - P dV + \delta W_{non-PV} - T dS + P dV$$

For reversible process: $\delta Q = T dS$

$$dG = T dS - P dV + \delta W_{non-PV} - T dS + P dV = \delta W_{non-PV}$$

Integrating:

$$\boxed{\Delta G = W_{non-PV}}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Change in Gibbs free energy equals non-expansion work at constant $T$ and $P$. Maximum useful work available is $-\Delta G$.

**Answer:** $\Delta G = W_{non-PV}$ for isothermal, isobaric process

## Example 3: Helmholtz Free Energy for Ideal Gas

For one mole of ideal gas, express Helmholtz free energy $F(T, V)$.

**Solution:**

From $F = U - TS$:

For ideal gas:
- $U = C_V T$ (depends only on $T$)
- Need to find $S(T, V)$

From Unit 3: $S = C_V \ln T + R\ln V + \text{const}$

Therefore:

$$F = C_V T - T(C_V \ln T + R\ln V + s_0)$$

$$= C_V T - TC_V\ln T - TR\ln V - Ts_0$$

where $s_0$ is integration constant.

More commonly written:

$$F(T, V) = F_0(T) - RT\ln V$$

where $F_0(T)$ contains temperature-dependent terms.

**Verification**: Check $P = -(\partial F/\partial V)_T$:

$$P = -\frac{\partial}{\partial V}\left[F_0(T) - RT\ln V\right] = -\left(0 - \frac{RT}{V}\right) = \frac{RT}{V}$$

This is ideal gas law! ✓

**Answer:** $F = C_V T - TC_V\ln T - TR\ln V - Ts_0$

## Example 4: Spontaneity Criterion Using Gibbs Free Energy

Show that at constant $T$ and $P$, spontaneous processes have $\Delta G < 0$.

**Solution:**

Second law: $dS \geq \delta Q/T$ with equality for reversible processes.

For any process:

$$T dS \geq \delta Q$$

First law: $dU = \delta Q - P dV$ (assuming only expansion work)

So: $\delta Q = dU + P dV$

Therefore:

$$T dS \geq dU + P dV$$

At **constant $T$ and $P$**:

$$T dS \geq dU + P dV$$

Rearranging:

$$dU - T dS + P dV \leq 0$$

But: $dG = dU - T dS - S dT + P dV + V dP$

At constant $T$ and $P$ ($dT = 0$, $dP = 0$):

$$dG = dU - T dS + P dV$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{dG \leq 0}$$

- $dG < 0$: spontaneous (irreversible) process
- $dG = 0$: equilibrium (reversible process)
- $dG > 0$: impossible spontaneous process

**Physical interpretation**: Systems at constant $T$ and $P$ spontaneously evolve toward minimum Gibbs free energy. Equilibrium occurs at the minimum.

**Answer:** At constant $T$, $P$: spontaneous processes have $dG < 0$

## Example 5: Relationship Between Potentials

Express Gibbs free energy $G$ in terms of all other potentials.

**Solution:**

Starting from definitions:

$$G = U - TS + PV$$

But we also know:
- $H = U + PV$
- $F = U - TS$

Therefore:

**In terms of $H$**:
$$G = H - TS$$

**In terms of $F$**:
$$G = F + PV$$

**In terms of $U$**:
$$G = U - TS + PV$$

**Verification**: All three give same $dG$:

From $G = H - TS$:
$$dG = dH - T dS - S dT = (T dS + V dP + \mu dN) - T dS - S dT$$
$$= -S dT + V dP + \mu dN$$ ✓

From $G = F + PV$:
$$dG = dF + P dV + V dP = (-S dT - P dV + \mu dN) + P dV + V dP$$
$$= -S dT + V dP + \mu dN$$ ✓

**Answer:** $G = H - TS = F + PV = U - TS + PV$

**Problem 1: C** - Helmholtz $F$: natural variables $(T, V, N)$ from $dF = -S dT - P dV + \mu dN$.
