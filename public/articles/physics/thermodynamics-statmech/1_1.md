# Article 1.1: Thermodynamic Systems and State Variables

Thermodynamics describes systems containing vast numbers of particles—on the order of $10^{23}$ molecules—using just a handful of macroscopic variables. A cylinder of gas is completely specified thermodynamically by its pressure, volume, temperature, and chemical composition, even though tracking every individual molecule would require $10^{23}$ position and momentum coordinates. This reduction from microscopic to macroscopic description is both necessary (we can't track every molecule) and powerful (the laws we derive apply universally).

The first task in thermodynamics is to carefully define what constitutes a "system" and identify which variables completely determine its state. Unlike mechanics, where position and momentum uniquely specify a particle's state, thermodynamic systems require multiple variables whose relationships are governed by equations of state. Understanding the distinction between different types of systems (isolated, closed, open) and different types of variables (extensive, intensive, state functions, path functions) is essential for applying thermodynamic reasoning correctly.

The mathematical framework relies heavily on multivariable calculus. State variables are functions of multiple independent quantities, and their changes involve partial derivatives with specific variables held constant. This article establishes the fundamental concepts of thermodynamic systems, introduces the crucial distinction between extensive and intensive properties, and develops Euler's theorem for homogeneous functions—a powerful result connecting thermodynamic variables.

## Thermodynamic Systems: Isolated, Closed, and Open

A **thermodynamic system** is the portion of the universe we choose to study. Everything outside constitutes the **surroundings**, and the **boundary** separates system from surroundings. Boundaries can be physical (container walls) or imaginary (control volumes).

Systems are classified by what crosses their boundaries:

**Isolated system**: Neither energy nor matter crosses the boundary. The system is completely cut off from surroundings. Example: the universe as a whole.

**Closed system**: Energy can cross the boundary (as heat or work), but matter cannot. The amount of substance remains constant. Example: a sealed piston-cylinder device.

**Open system**: Both energy and matter can cross the boundary. The mass can change. Examples: jet engines, boiling pots, biological organisms.

Most real systems are open, but we often approximate them as closed when mass transfer is negligible.

## Extensive vs. Intensive Variables

Thermodynamic variables are either extensive or intensive:

**Extensive variables** scale proportionally with system size:
- Volume $V$
- Number of particles $N$ (or moles $n$)
- Mass $m$
- Internal energy $U$
- Entropy $S$

Mathematically, an extensive variable $X$ satisfies:
$$X(\lambda n, \lambda V, \lambda U, ...) = \lambda X(n, V, U, ...)$$

This is **homogeneity of degree one**.

**Intensive variables** are independent of system size:
- Temperature $T$
- Pressure $P$
- Density $\rho = m/V$
- Chemical potential $\mu$

Intensive variables often result from ratios of extensive variables.

## State Functions and Path Functions

A **state function** depends only on the current equilibrium state, not the path taken:
$$\oint df = 0$$

Examples: $P$, $V$, $T$, $U$, $S$, $H$

**Path functions** depend on the process connecting states. Heat $Q$ and work $W$ are path functions:
$$\oint \delta Q \neq 0, \quad \oint \delta W \neq 0$$

We denote path functions with $\delta$ (not $d$) to emphasize path-dependence.

## Homogeneous Functions and Euler's Theorem

A function $f(x_1, x_2, ..., x_n)$ is **homogeneous of degree $k$** if:
$$f(\lambda x_1, \lambda x_2, ..., \lambda x_n) = \lambda^k f(x_1, x_2, ..., x_n)$$

**Euler's theorem** states that for homogeneous functions of degree $k$:
$$\sum_{i=1}^n x_i \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} = k f$$

For internal energy $U = U(S, V, N)$ (extensive, degree 1):
$$S\frac{\partial U}{\partial S} + V\frac{\partial U}{\partial V} + N\frac{\partial U}{\partial N} = U$$

Identifying partial derivatives as thermodynamic variables:
$$T = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial S}\right)_{V,N}, \quad -P = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_{S,N}, \quad \mu = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial N}\right)_{S,V}$$

This gives **Euler's relation**:
$$U = TS - PV + \mu N$$

## Thermal Equilibrium and Quasi-Static Processes

A system is in **thermal equilibrium** when macroscopic properties are uniform and time-independent, requiring no temperature, pressure, or concentration gradients.

**Quasi-static processes** proceed infinitely slowly through continuous equilibrium states. They're reversible idealizations but provide upper bounds on real process performance.

## Mathematical Framework: State Space

The **state space** is the collection of all equilibrium states. For a simple system, we need two independent variables—the third is determined by the equation of state $f(P, V, T) = 0$.

The **Gibbs phase rule**:
$$f = c - p + 2$$

where $f$ is degrees of freedom, $c$ is number of components, and $p$ is number of phases.

## Example 1: Extensive and Intensive Classification

Classify: (a) Molar volume $V_m$, (b) Total heat capacity $C_V$, (c) Pressure

**Solution:**

(a) **Intensive**. $V_m = V/n$ is a ratio of extensive quantities. Scaling the system: both $V \to \lambda V$ and $n \to \lambda n$, so $V_m$ unchanged.

(b) **Extensive**. Heat capacity $C_V = (\partial U/\partial T)_V$ scales with system size.

(c) **Intensive**. Pressure doesn't depend on system size.

**Answer:** (a) intensive, (b) extensive, (c) intensive

## Example 2: Verifying Euler's Theorem

For ideal gas $U = \frac{3}{2}nRT$, verify homogeneity of degree one.

**Solution:**

Scaling amount at constant temperature:
$$U(\lambda n, T) = \frac{3}{2}(\lambda n)RT = \lambda \cdot \frac{3}{2}nRT = \lambda U(n,T)$$

Confirmed: homogeneous of degree one. ✓

In proper extensive coordinates $U = U(S, V, n)$, Euler's theorem gives:
$$U = TS - PV + \mu n$$

**Answer:** Verified: $U$ is homogeneous of degree one

---

## Example 3: State Function Property

A gas expands from $(P_A, V_A) = (2 \text{ atm}, 5 \text{ L})$ to $(P_B, V_B) = (1 \text{ atm}, 10 \text{ L})$ along two paths. Calculate $\Delta V$.

**Solution:**

Volume is a state function:
$$\Delta V = V_B - V_A = 10 - 5 = 5 \text{ L}$$

This is the same for any path connecting A to B. State functions have path-independent changes.

**Answer:** $\Delta V = 5$ L (same for all paths)

---

## Example 4: Scaling Properties

Container holds $n = 2$ moles ideal gas at $T = 300$ K, $P = 1$ atm. Add 2 more moles at same $T, P$. Find how properties change.

**Solution:**

At constant $T$ and $P$:

(a) **Volume** (extensive): $V \to 2V$ (doubles)
(b) **Internal energy** (extensive): For ideal gas $U = \frac{3}{2}nRT$, so $U \to 2U$ (doubles)
(c) **Temperature** (intensive): $T$ unchanged
(d) **Pressure** (intensive): $P$ unchanged

**Answer:** Volume and internal energy double; temperature and pressure unchanged

## Example 5: Euler's Theorem Application

For $U = aS^{5/3}V^{-2/3}$, verify Euler's theorem and find $T$ and $P$.

**Solution:**

Check homogeneity:
$$U(\lambda S, \lambda V) = a(\lambda S)^{5/3}(\lambda V)^{-2/3} = \lambda^1 U(S,V)$$ ✓

Euler's theorem:
$$U = S\frac{\partial U}{\partial S} + V\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}$$

Compute:
$$\frac{\partial U}{\partial S} = \frac{5}{3} \cdot \frac{U}{S}, \quad \frac{\partial U}{\partial V} = -\frac{2}{3} \cdot \frac{U}{V}$$

Check:
$$S \cdot \frac{5U}{3S} + V \cdot \left(-\frac{2U}{3V}\right) = \frac{5U}{3} - \frac{2U}{3} = U$$ ✓

Temperature and pressure:
$$T = \frac{\partial U}{\partial S} = \frac{5aS^{2/3}}{3V^{2/3}}, \quad P = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial V} = \frac{2aS^{5/3}}{3V^{5/3}}$$

**Answer:** Verified; $T = \frac{5aS^{2/3}}{3V^{2/3}}$, $P = \frac{2aS^{5/3}}{3V^{5/3}}$
