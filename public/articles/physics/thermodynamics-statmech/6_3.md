# Article 6.3: Bose-Einstein Statistics

Bosons—particles with integer spin—exhibit radically different behavior than fermions. Unlike fermions, which avoid each other due to Pauli exclusion, **bosons prefer to occupy the same quantum state**. This "bosonic enhancement" becomes dramatic at low temperature, leading to **Bose-Einstein condensation** (BEC)—a macroscopic occupation of the ground state. Below a critical temperature $T_c$, a finite fraction of bosons spontaneously condense into the lowest energy state, creating a quantum-coherent macroscopic system.

The **Bose-Einstein distribution** $\langle n_i \rangle = 1/(e^{\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)} - 1)$ governs boson statistics. Unlike Fermi-Dirac statistics with its "+1" in the denominator, Bose-Einstein has "−1", allowing $\langle n_i \rangle$ to diverge as chemical potential approaches the ground state energy. This divergence signals the phase transition to BEC. The chemical potential must always remain less than the ground state energy ($\mu < \epsilon_0$)—a constraint absent for fermions.

This article derives the Bose-Einstein distribution, explores its properties and the chemical potential constraint, calculates critical temperature for BEC using density of states, examines the condensate fraction below $T_c$, treats photon gas as a special case with $\mu = 0$, and applies the formalism to superfluid helium-4 and ultracold atomic gases. The mathematics involves integrals with Bose functions, special functions including Riemann zeta function, phase transition analysis, and asymptotic behavior near critical points.

## Bose-Einstein Distribution

In **grand canonical ensemble** for bosons, average occupation of single-particle state with energy $\epsilon_i$:

$$\boxed{\langle n_i \rangle = f_{BE}(\epsilon_i) = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)} - 1}}$$

where $\beta = 1/(k_B T)$ and $\mu$ is chemical potential.

This is the **Bose-Einstein distribution**.

**Properties**:
- $\langle n_i \rangle \geq 0$ (non-negative)
- No upper bound on $\langle n_i \rangle$ (unlimited occupancy)
- Diverges as $\epsilon_i \to \mu^+$ (from above)
- High energy ($\epsilon \gg \mu$): $f_{BE} \to e^{-\beta(\epsilon - \mu)}$ (classical limit)

**Derivation**: Grand canonical for single boson state:

$$\mathcal{Z}_i = \sum_{n=0}^\infty e^{-\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)n} = \frac{1}{1 - e^{-\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)}}$$

(geometric series, valid for $e^{-\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)} < 1$)

$$\langle n_i \rangle = \frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\partial \ln \mathcal{Z}_i}{\partial \mu} = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)} - 1}$$

## Chemical Potential Constraint

For geometric series to converge:

$$e^{-\beta(\epsilon_i - \mu)} < 1$$

$$\epsilon_i > \mu$$

**Critical constraint**: Chemical potential must remain **below all energy levels**:

$$\boxed{\mu < \epsilon_0}$$

where $\epsilon_0$ is ground state energy (usually set to zero: $\epsilon_0 = 0$, so $\mu < 0$).

**Physical meaning**: As $\mu \to \epsilon_0^-$, ground state occupation diverges → Bose-Einstein condensation.

**Contrast with fermions**: Fermi-Dirac has no such constraint—$\mu$ can be arbitrarily positive.

## Bose-Einstein Condensation (BEC)

**BEC phase transition**: Below critical temperature $T_c$, macroscopic number of bosons occupy ground state.

**Total particle number**:

$$N = N_0 + N_{\text{excited}}$$

where:
- $N_0$: number in ground state ($\epsilon = 0$)
- $N_{\text{excited}} = \int_0^\infty g(\epsilon) f_{BE}(\epsilon) d\epsilon$: number in excited states

**Above $T_c$**: All particles in excited states, $\mu < 0$ adjusts to satisfy:

$$N = \int_0^\infty g(\epsilon) f_{BE}(\epsilon) d\epsilon$$

**At $T_c$**: Chemical potential reaches $\mu = 0$, integral saturates:

$$N = \int_0^\infty \frac{g(\epsilon)}{e^{\beta_c \epsilon} - 1} d\epsilon = N_{\text{critical}}$$

**Below $T_c$**: Chemical potential pinned at $\mu \approx 0$, excess particles condense into ground state:

$$N_0 = N - N_{\text{excited}}(T)$$

## Critical Temperature

For **free bosons** in 3D with density of states $g(\epsilon) \propto \epsilon^{1/2}$:

$$g(\epsilon) = \frac{V(2s+1)}{4\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2} \sqrt{\epsilon}$$

At critical temperature with $\mu = 0$:

$$N = \int_0^\infty \frac{g(\epsilon)}{e^{\beta_c \epsilon} - 1} d\epsilon$$

Converting to dimensionless variable $x = \beta_c \epsilon$:

$$N = \frac{V(2s+1)}{4\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2} (k_B T_c)^{3/2} \int_0^\infty \frac{x^{1/2}}{e^x - 1} dx$$

The integral equals $\Gamma(3/2)\zeta(3/2) = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2} \times 2.612 = 2.315$.

**Critical temperature**:

$$\boxed{k_B T_c = \frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{m}\left[\frac{n}{\zeta(3/2)(2s+1)}\right]^{2/3}}$$

where $n = N/V$ is density and $\zeta(3/2) \approx 2.612$ is Riemann zeta function.

For spin-0 bosons ($s = 0$):

$$k_B T_c = 3.31\frac{\hbar^2 n^{2/3}}{m}$$

**Scaling**: $T_c \propto n^{2/3}$ (higher density → higher critical temperature).

## Condensate Fraction

**Condensate fraction**: Proportion in ground state:

$$\frac{N_0}{N} = 1 - \frac{N_{\text{excited}}}{N}$$

For $T < T_c$:

$$N_{\text{excited}} = N\left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^{3/2}$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\frac{N_0}{N} = 1 - \left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^{3/2}}$$

**Behavior**:
- $T = 0$: $N_0/N = 1$ (all in ground state)
- $T = T_c$: $N_0/N = 0$ (phase transition)
- $T > T_c$: $N_0/N = 0$ (normal phase)

**Order parameter**: $N_0/N$ is order parameter for BEC phase transition (continuous, second-order).

## Photon Gas (Special Case)

**Photons**: Massless bosons with spin $s = 1$, but only two polarization states (transverse).

**Key property**: Photon number not conserved → **$\mu = 0$** always (not just at $T_c$).

**Bose-Einstein distribution** for photons:

$$\langle n_\omega \rangle = \frac{1}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1}$$

This is **Planck distribution** (derived in next article on blackbody radiation).

**No BEC for photons**: Since $\mu = 0$ always and photons massless, no condensation occurs. Ground state ($\omega = 0$) not physical for photons.

## Riemann Zeta Function

The **Riemann zeta function**:

$$\boxed{\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n^s} = 1 + \frac{1}{2^s} + \frac{1}{3^s} + ...}$$

**Special values** (important for BEC):
- $\zeta(3/2) \approx 2.612$
- $\zeta(2) = \pi^2/6 \approx 1.645$
- $\zeta(5/2) \approx 1.341$
- $\zeta(3) \approx 1.202$ (Apéry's constant)

**Connection to BEC**: Integrals of form:

$$\int_0^\infty \frac{x^{s-1}}{e^x - 1} dx = \Gamma(s)\zeta(s)$$

appear throughout BEC theory.

## Phase Transition Analysis

BEC is a **second-order phase transition** (continuous, no latent heat).

**Order parameter**: $\Psi_0 = \sqrt{N_0/V}$ (condensate density)

**Below $T_c$**:

$$\Psi_0 \propto \left(1 - \frac{T}{T_c}\right)^{\beta_c}$$

where $\beta_c = 1/2$ (critical exponent for mean-field BEC).

**Heat capacity**: Discontinuity in $C_V$ at $T = T_c$ (lambda-point anomaly).

**Coherence**: Condensate has macroscopic phase coherence—all particles in same quantum state.

## Example 1: Critical Temperature for Helium-4

Liquid helium-4: mass $m = 6.64 \times 10^{-27}$ kg, density $n = 2.18 \times 10^{28}$ m⁻³. Estimate $T_c$.

**Solution:**

For spin-0 bosons:

$$k_B T_c = 3.31\frac{\hbar^2 n^{2/3}}{m}$$

$$= 3.31\frac{(1.055 \times 10^{-34})^2 (2.18 \times 10^{28})^{2/3}}{6.64 \times 10^{-27}}$$

$$= 3.31\frac{(1.113 \times 10^{-68})(8.09 \times 10^{18})}{6.64 \times 10^{-27}}$$

$$= 3.31 \times 1.36 \times 10^{-22} = 4.50 \times 10^{-22} \text{ J}$$

$$T_c = \frac{4.50 \times 10^{-22}}{1.381 \times 10^{-23}} = 3.26 \text{ K}$$

**Experimental value**: $T_\lambda = 2.17$ K (lambda point)

Estimate in right ballpark! Difference due to interactions (model assumes non-interacting).

**Answer:** $T_c \approx 3.3$ K (theory), 2.17 K (experiment)

## Example 2: Condensate Fraction

At $T = 1$ K with $T_c = 3$ K, what fraction of particles in ground state?

**Solution:**

$$\frac{N_0}{N} = 1 - \left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^{3/2} = 1 - \left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{3/2}$$

$$= 1 - \frac{1}{3^{3/2}} = 1 - \frac{1}{5.196} = 1 - 0.193 = 0.807 = 80.7\%$$

**Physical interpretation**: At $T = T_c/3$, about 81% of particles condensed into ground state—substantial macroscopic occupation!

**Answer:** $N_0/N = 80.7\%$

## Example 3: Chemical Potential Constraint

Boson system with ground state energy $\epsilon_0 = 0$. If $\mu = -0.01k_B T$, is this above or below $T_c$?

**Solution:**

**Above $T_c$**: $\mu < 0$ (significantly negative)

**At $T_c$**: $\mu \to 0^-$

**Below $T_c$**: $\mu \approx 0$ (pinned at ground state energy)

Given $\mu = -0.01k_B T$, chemical potential is slightly negative but very close to zero.

**Conclusion**: System is **near $T_c$**, likely just above critical temperature.

If below $T_c$, would have $\mu \approx 0$ within numerical precision, with $N_0$ macroscopic.

**Answer:** Near $T_c$, likely just above critical temperature

## Example 4: Bose-Einstein vs Classical

At high temperature, show Bose-Einstein distribution reduces to classical (Maxwell-Boltzmann).

**Solution:**

High temperature or low density: $\mu \ll -k_B T$ (very negative)

$$f_{BE} = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon - \mu)} - 1}$$

When $\beta(\epsilon - \mu) \gg 1$ (high energy or large $|\mu|$):

$$e^{\beta(\epsilon - \mu)} \gg 1$$

$$f_{BE} \approx \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon - \mu)}} = e^{-\beta\epsilon} \cdot e^{\beta\mu}$$

This is **Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution** with fugacity $z = e^{\beta\mu}$.

**Physical interpretation**: At high temperature, quantum statistics → classical statistics. Particles rarely in same state, so indistinguishability and quantum exchange effects negligible.

**Answer:** $f_{BE} \to e^{\beta\mu} e^{-\beta\epsilon}$ (classical limit when $e^{\beta(\epsilon - \mu)} \gg 1$)

## Example 5: Zeta Function Calculation

Calculate $N_{\text{excited}}/N$ at $T = T_c/2$ using zeta function.

**Solution:**

$$N_{\text{excited}} = N\left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^{3/2}$$

At $T = T_c/2$:

$$\frac{N_{\text{excited}}}{N} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{3/2} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{2.828} = 0.354$$

**Condensate fraction**:

$$\frac{N_0}{N} = 1 - 0.354 = 0.646 = 64.6\%$$

**Physical interpretation**: At half the critical temperature, about 65% of particles in ground state—deeply in BEC phase.

**Answer:** $N_{\text{excited}}/N = 35.4\%$, $N_0/N = 64.6\%$

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B** - Bose-Einstein: denominator is **$e^{\beta(\epsilon - \mu)} - 1$** (minus sign).

**Problem 2: C** - Bosons: $\mu < \epsilon_0$ (must stay below ground state energy).

**Problem 3: B** - Condensate fraction: **$1 - (T/T_c)^{3/2}$** for $T < T_c$.

**Problem 4: C** - Critical temperature: $T_c \propto n^{2/3}$ (from particle counting).

**Problem 5: C** - Photons: **$\mu = 0$** always (number not conserved).
