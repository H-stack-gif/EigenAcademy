# Article 4.4: Chemical Potential and Phase Equilibrium

When ice melts or water boils, molecules don't change identity—H₂O remains H₂O. Yet dramatic transformations occur: solid becomes liquid, liquid becomes gas. What determines when these **phase transitions** happen? The answer lies in the **chemical potential** $\mu$—the energy cost of adding one particle to the system. Phase transitions occur when the chemical potentials of coexisting phases become equal, ensuring thermodynamic equilibrium.

The **Clausius-Clapeyron equation** quantifies how phase boundaries shift with temperature and pressure, governing phenomena from mountain-top cooking to cloud formation. The equation relates the slope of phase boundaries to latent heat and volume change: $dP/dT = L/(T\Delta V)$. This connects microscopic energetics (latent heat) to macroscopic phase diagrams. Understanding phase equilibrium requires the **Gibbs phase rule**, which counts thermodynamic degrees of freedom: for single-component systems, $f = 3 - p$ where $p$ is the number of coexisting phases.

This article develops chemical potential from Gibbs free energy, derives phase equilibrium conditions, obtains the Clausius-Clapeyron equation through thermodynamic reasoning, explores phase diagrams systematically, identifies triple points and critical points, and applies the Gibbs phase rule. The mathematics involves partial derivatives, ordinary differential equations, implicit differentiation, integration of vapor pressure curves, and multivariate function analysis.

## Chemical Potential Definition

**Chemical potential** is the change in Gibbs free energy per particle added at constant temperature and pressure:

$$\mu = \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial N}\right)_{T,P}$$

Or per mole:

$$\mu = \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial n}\right)_{T,P}$$

**Alternative definitions** from other potentials:

$$\mu = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial N}\right)_{S,V} = \left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial N}\right)_{S,P} = \left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial N}\right)_{T,V}$$

All give the same value—chemical potential is the same quantity in all representations.

**Physical interpretation**: $\mu$ is the "escaping tendency" of particles. High $\mu$ means particles want to leave (high free energy per particle). Low $\mu$ means particles want to stay. Systems spontaneously flow from high $\mu$ to low $\mu$ regions.

## Gibbs-Duhem Relation

For single-component system, $G = \mu N$ (Gibbs free energy is extensive). Taking differential:

$$dG = \mu dN + N d\mu$$

But also: $dG = -S dT + V dP + \mu dN$

Comparing:

$$\mu dN + N d\mu = -S dT + V dP + \mu dN$$

$$N d\mu = -S dT + V dP$$

**Gibbs-Duhem relation**:

$$\boxed{S dT - V dP + N d\mu = 0}$$

Or per mole:

$$s dT - v dP + d\mu = 0$$

where $s = S/n$ and $v = V/n$ are molar quantities.

**Physical meaning**: Chemical potential is not independent—it's determined by $(T, P)$ through Gibbs-Duhem.

## Phase Equilibrium Conditions

For two phases $\alpha$ and $\beta$ in equilibrium at constant temperature and pressure, the equilibrium conditions are:

$$\boxed{T_\alpha = T_\beta, \quad P_\alpha = P_\beta, \quad \mu_\alpha = \mu_\beta}$$

**Derivation**: At equilibrium, Gibbs free energy is minimized. For system with both phases:

$$G_{total} = N_\alpha \mu_\alpha + N_\beta \mu_\beta$$

with constraint $N_\alpha + N_\beta = N_{total}$ (constant).

At minimum: $dG_{total} = 0$ for infinitesimal particle transfer $dN_\alpha = -dN_\beta$:

$$\mu_\alpha dN_\alpha + \mu_\beta dN_\beta = (\mu_\alpha - \mu_\beta) dN_\alpha = 0$$

Therefore: $\mu_\alpha = \mu_\beta$

**Physical interpretation**: Particles flow between phases until chemical potentials equalize. No net flow at equilibrium.

## Clausius-Clapeyron Equation

Along a phase boundary, $\mu_\alpha(T, P) = \mu_\beta(T, P)$ must hold for all $(T, P)$ on the curve.

Taking differential:

$$d\mu_\alpha = d\mu_\beta$$

From Gibbs-Duhem (per mole): $d\mu = -s dT + v dP$

$$-s_\alpha dT + v_\alpha dP = -s_\beta dT + v_\beta dP$$

$$(v_\alpha - v_\beta) dP = (s_\alpha - s_\beta) dT$$

$$\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{s_\alpha - s_\beta}{v_\alpha - v_\beta} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta v}$$

Using $\Delta s = L/T$ where $L$ is latent heat per mole:

$$\boxed{\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{L}{T \Delta v}}$$

This is the **Clausius-Clapeyron equation**.

**Alternative form**:

$$\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta v} = \frac{\Delta h}{T \Delta v}$$

where $\Delta h$ is molar enthalpy change.

## Application: Liquid-Vapor Equilibrium

For liquid-vapor transition, assume:
1. $v_{vapor} \gg v_{liquid}$ (gas volume much larger)
2. Vapor behaves ideally: $v_{vapor} = RT/P$

Approximations:

$$\Delta v \approx v_{vapor} = \frac{RT}{P}$$

Clausius-Clapeyron becomes:

$$\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{LP}{RT^2}$$

Separating variables:

$$\frac{dP}{P} = \frac{L}{RT^2} dT$$

Integrating (assuming $L$ constant):

$$\ln P = -\frac{L}{RT} + \text{const}$$

$$\boxed{P = P_0 e^{-L/(RT)}}$$

Or between two points:

$$\ln\frac{P_2}{P_1} = -\frac{L}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right)$$

This predicts vapor pressure as function of temperature!

## Phase Diagrams

**Phase diagram** plots pressure vs. temperature, showing regions where different phases are stable.

**Key features**:

1. **Phase boundaries**: Curves where two phases coexist
   - Solid-liquid: fusion curve
   - Liquid-vapor: vaporization curve  
   - Solid-vapor: sublimation curve

2. **Triple point**: Point where all three phases coexist simultaneously
   - $T$, $P$, $\mu$ all equal for solid, liquid, gas
   - For water: $T_{tp} = 273.16$ K, $P_{tp} = 611$ Pa

3. **Critical point**: End of liquid-vapor curve
   - Above critical point: no distinction between liquid and gas
   - **Supercritical fluid** exists
   - For water: $T_c = 647$ K, $P_c = 22.1$ MPa
   - For CO₂: $T_c = 304$ K, $P_c = 7.4$ MPa

## Gibbs Phase Rule

For system with $c$ components and $p$ phases in equilibrium:

$$\boxed{f = c - p + 2}$$

where $f$ is number of **degrees of freedom** (intensive variables we can independently vary while maintaining equilibrium).

**Derivation**: 
- Each phase has $c + 1$ intensive variables: $T$, $P$, and $c-1$ mole fractions
- Equilibrium requires $T$ and $P$ equal across phases: removes $2(p-1)$ variables
- Chemical potentials equal across phases: removes $c(p-1)$ variables
- Counting: $f = (c+1)p - 2(p-1) - c(p-1) = c - p + 2$

**For single component** ($c = 1$):

$$f = 3 - p$$

- Single phase ($p = 1$): $f = 2$ (can vary both $T$ and $P$)
- Two phases ($p = 2$): $f = 1$ (fixing $T$ determines $P$, or vice versa)
- Triple point ($p = 3$): $f = 0$ (unique point, can't vary anything)

## Example 1: Clausius-Clapeyron for Water

Water at 100°C and 1 atm has latent heat of vaporization $L = 40.7$ kJ/mol. Estimate vapor pressure at 110°C.

**Solution:**

Clausius-Clapeyron:

$$\ln\frac{P_2}{P_1} = -\frac{L}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right)$$

Given:
- $T_1 = 373$ K, $P_1 = 1$ atm
- $T_2 = 383$ K, $P_2 = ?$
- $L = 40700$ J/mol, $R = 8.314$ J/(mol·K)

Calculate:

$$\ln\frac{P_2}{1} = -\frac{40700}{8.314}\left(\frac{1}{383} - \frac{1}{373}\right)$$

$$= -4895\left(0.002611 - 0.002681\right) = -4895(-0.000070)$$

$$= 0.343$$

$$P_2 = e^{0.343} = 1.41 \text{ atm}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Small temperature increase (10 K) gives significant pressure increase (41%). This is why pressure cookers work—higher pressure allows higher boiling temperature.

**Answer:** $P_2 \approx 1.41$ atm

## Example 2: Ice Skating Physics

Ice melts at 0°C and 1 atm with $\Delta h = -6.01$ kJ/mol and $\Delta v = -1.6$ cm³/mol. Estimate melting point at 10 atm.

**Solution:**

Clausius-Clapeyron for solid-liquid:

$$\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{\Delta h}{T \Delta v}$$

At $T_1 = 273$ K:

$$\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{-6010}{(273)(-1.6 \times 10^{-6})} = \frac{-6010}{-4.37 \times 10^{-4}} = 1.375 \times 10^7 \text{ Pa/K}$$

$$= 136 \text{ atm/K}$$

For pressure change $\Delta P = 10 - 1 = 9$ atm:

$$\Delta T = \frac{\Delta P}{dP/dT} = \frac{9}{136} = 0.066 \text{ K}$$

New melting point:

$$T_2 = 273 - 0.066 = 272.93 \text{ K} = -0.07°\text{C}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Increasing pressure lowers melting point (because $\Delta v < 0$—ice is less dense than water). This is unusual! Ice skating works because pressure lowers melting point, creating thin water layer for lubrication.

**Answer:** $T_2 \approx -0.07°\text{C}$

## Example 3: Chemical Potential Equality

Show that at solid-liquid equilibrium, $\mu_{solid} = \mu_{liquid}$.

**Solution:**

At equilibrium at constant $T$ and $P$, Gibbs free energy is minimized.

For system with $N_{solid}$ and $N_{liquid}$ particles:

$$G_{total} = N_{solid} \mu_{solid} + N_{liquid} \mu_{liquid}$$

with constraint: $N_{solid} + N_{liquid} = N_{total}$ (constant).

At minimum, variation gives zero:

$$\delta G = \mu_{solid} \delta N_{solid} + \mu_{liquid} \delta N_{liquid} = 0$$

With constraint: $\delta N_{liquid} = -\delta N_{solid}$

$$(\mu_{solid} - \mu_{liquid}) \delta N_{solid} = 0$$

Since $\delta N_{solid}$ is arbitrary:

$$\mu_{solid} = \mu_{liquid}$$

**Physical interpretation**: If $\mu_{solid} > \mu_{liquid}$, particles would spontaneously transfer from solid to liquid, lowering free energy. Equilibrium requires equal chemical potentials.

**Answer:** Equilibrium: $\mu_{solid} = \mu_{liquid}$

## Example 4: Gibbs Phase Rule Application

For water system:
(a) Single liquid phase: how many degrees of freedom?
(b) Liquid-vapor equilibrium: degrees of freedom?
(c) Triple point: degrees of freedom?

**Solution:**

Gibbs phase rule for single component: $f = 3 - p$

(a) **Single liquid** ($p = 1$):

$$f = 3 - 1 = 2$$

Can independently vary both $T$ and $P$ while remaining in liquid phase.

(b) **Liquid-vapor** ($p = 2$):

$$f = 3 - 2 = 1$$

Can vary either $T$ or $P$, but not independently—they're related by vapor pressure curve.

(c) **Triple point** ($p = 3$):

$$f = 3 - 3 = 0$$

No degrees of freedom—triple point is unique. Cannot vary anything while maintaining three-phase coexistence.

**Answer:** (a) $f = 2$, (b) $f = 1$, (c) $f = 0$

## Example 5: Vapor Pressure Calculation

CO₂ has normal sublimation point at $T_1 = 194.7$ K, $P_1 = 1$ atm with $L_{sub} = 25.2$ kJ/mol. Find vapor pressure at $T_2 = 180$ K.

**Solution:**

Clausius-Clapeyron:

$$\ln\frac{P_2}{P_1} = -\frac{L_{sub}}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right)$$

$$= -\frac{25200}{8.314}\left(\frac{1}{180} - \frac{1}{194.7}\right)$$

$$= -3031(0.005556 - 0.005136) = -3031(0.000420)$$

$$= -1.273$$

$$P_2 = P_1 e^{-1.273} = (1)(0.280) = 0.280 \text{ atm}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Lower temperature significantly reduces vapor pressure. At 180 K, CO₂ vapor pressure is only 28% of its value at 194.7 K.

**Answer:** $P_2 = 0.280$ atm

**Problem 1: B** - Chemical potential: $\mu = (\partial G/\partial N)_{T,P}$ (change in Gibbs per particle added).
