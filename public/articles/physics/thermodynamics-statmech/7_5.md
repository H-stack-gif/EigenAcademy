# Article 7.5: Transport Phenomena II: Thermal Conductivity

Heat flows spontaneously from hot to cold, driven by temperature gradients. **Thermal conductivity** $\kappa$ quantifies this transport, connecting heat flux to temperature gradient through **Fourier's law** $\mathbf{q} = -\kappa \nabla T$. Like diffusion and viscosity, thermal conductivity arises from molecular collisions: fast-moving (hot) molecules transfer energy to slow-moving (cold) molecules through collisions. The thermal conductivity coefficient $\kappa \sim nC_V \lambda \langle v \rangle/3$ depends on heat capacity, mean free path, and molecular speed.

The **heat equation** $\partial T/\partial t = \alpha \nabla^2 T$ governs temperature evolution, where $\alpha = \kappa/(nC_V)$ is **thermal diffusivity**. Solutions describe steady-state heat conduction (Laplace equation) and transient heat transfer (time-dependent solutions using separation of variables or Fourier series). For **metals**, electron conduction dominates, leading to the **Wiedemann-Franz law**: the ratio $\kappa/(\sigma T)$ is nearly universal for all metals, connecting thermal and electrical conductivity through the Lorenz number.

This article derives Fourier's law from energy transport arguments, calculates thermal conductivity coefficient, introduces the heat diffusion equation and its solutions in one, two, and three dimensions, explores steady-state and transient problems using separation of variables and Fourier series, treats the Wiedemann-Franz law for metals, and demonstrates applications to heat conduction in materials. The mathematics involves parabolic partial differential equations, boundary value problems, separation of variables, Fourier series solutions, and dimensional analysis.

## Heat Flux and Temperature Gradient

**Setup**: Temperature varies with position: $T = T(x)$

Molecules carry kinetic energy $E \sim C_V T$ where $C_V$ is heat capacity per particle.

**Energy transport**: Similar to diffusion and viscosity—molecules crossing plane at $x$ carry energy from neighboring regions.

From left ($x - \lambda$): energy per molecule $\sim C_V T(x - \lambda) \approx C_V T - C_V \lambda \frac{\partial T}{\partial x}$

From right ($x + \lambda$): energy per molecule $\sim C_V T(x + \lambda) \approx C_V T + C_V \lambda \frac{\partial T}{\partial x}$

**Net energy flux**: (energy per area per time)

$$q_x = \frac{1}{6}n\langle v \rangle C_V \left[-2\lambda \frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\right] = -\frac{1}{3}nC_V \lambda \langle v \rangle \frac{\partial T}{\partial x}$$

**Fourier's law**:

$$\boxed{\mathbf{q} = -\kappa \nabla T}$$

where **thermal conductivity**:

$$\boxed{\kappa = \frac{1}{3}nC_V \lambda \langle v \rangle}$$

**Negative sign**: Heat flows from hot to cold (opposes gradient).

## Thermal Conductivity Coefficient

From $\kappa = \frac{1}{3}nC_V \lambda \langle v \rangle$ with:
- $C_V = \frac{3}{2}k_B$ (monatomic gas)
- $\lambda = 1/(\sqrt{2}n\sigma)$
- $\langle v \rangle = \sqrt{8k_B T/(\pi m)}$

$$\kappa = \frac{1}{3}n \cdot \frac{3k_B}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}n\sigma} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{8k_B T}{\pi m}}$$

$$\boxed{\kappa = \frac{k_B}{2\sqrt{2}\pi d^2} \sqrt{\frac{k_B T}{\pi m}} \propto \sqrt{\frac{T}{m}}}$$

**Properties**:
- $\kappa$ **independent of pressure** (like viscosity—$n$ cancels with $\lambda$)
- $\kappa \propto \sqrt{T}$ (increases with temperature)
- $\kappa \propto 1/\sqrt{m}$ (lighter molecules conduct heat better)
- $\kappa \propto 1/d^2$ (smaller molecules more effective)

**Typical values** at 300 K:
- Air: $\kappa \approx 0.026$ W/(m·K)
- Helium: $\kappa \approx 0.15$ W/(m·K) (light atoms → good conductor)

## Heat Diffusion Equation

**Energy conservation**:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = -\nabla \cdot \mathbf{q}$$

where $u = nC_V T$ is energy density.

$$nC_V \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = -\nabla \cdot (-\kappa \nabla T) = \kappa \nabla^2 T$$

**Heat equation**:

$$\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \alpha \nabla^2 T$$

where **thermal diffusivity**:

$$\boxed{\alpha = \frac{\kappa}{nC_V}}$$

**1D form**:

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \alpha \frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2}}$$

Same mathematical form as diffusion equation!

**Typical values**:
- Air: $\alpha \approx 2 \times 10^{-5}$ m²/s
- Water: $\alpha \approx 1.4 \times 10^{-7}$ m²/s
- Metals: $\alpha \approx 10^{-5}$ to $10^{-4}$ m²/s

## Steady-State Heat Conduction

**Steady-state**: $\partial T/\partial t = 0$

Heat equation becomes **Laplace equation**:

$$\boxed{\nabla^2 T = 0}$$

**1D steady state** between two temperatures:

$$\frac{d^2T}{dx^2} = 0$$

General solution: $T(x) = Ax + B$

With boundary conditions $T(0) = T_1$, $T(L) = T_2$:

$$\boxed{T(x) = T_1 + \frac{T_2 - T_1}{L}x}$$

**Heat flux**:

$$q = -\kappa \frac{dT}{dx} = -\kappa \frac{T_2 - T_1}{L} = \kappa \frac{T_1 - T_2}{L}$$

**Thermal resistance**: $R_{th} = L/(\kappa A)$ (analogous to electrical resistance)

**Heat current**: $Q = \Delta T/R_{th} = \kappa A(T_1 - T_2)/L$

## Transient Heat Transfer

**Initial value problem**: Given $T(x, 0)$, find $T(x, t)$ for $t > 0$.

**Method**: Separation of variables

Assume: $T(x, t) = X(x)G(t)$

Substituting into heat equation:

$$X(x)G'(t) = \alpha X''(x)G(t)$$

$$\frac{G'(t)}{\alpha G(t)} = \frac{X''(x)}{X(x)} = -\lambda^2$$

(Constant, denoted $-\lambda^2$)

**Two ODEs**:

$$G'(t) = -\alpha \lambda^2 G(t) \implies G(t) = e^{-\alpha \lambda^2 t}$$

$$X''(x) = -\lambda^2 X(x) \implies X(x) = A\cos(\lambda x) + B\sin(\lambda x)$$

**Boundary conditions** determine $\lambda$ (e.g., $T(0,t) = T(L,t) = 0$ gives $\lambda_n = n\pi/L$).

**General solution**: Superposition (Fourier series)

$$\boxed{T(x,t) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty c_n \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) e^{-\alpha(n\pi/L)^2 t}}$$

Coefficients $c_n$ determined by initial condition using Fourier series.

**Physical interpretation**: Each mode decays exponentially with time constant $\tau_n = L^2/(\alpha n^2 \pi^2)$.

## Fourier Series Solutions

**Example**: Rod of length $L$, ends held at $T = 0$, initial temperature $T(x, 0) = T_0 \sin(\pi x/L)$.

**Solution**:

$$T(x,t) = T_0 \sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right) e^{-\alpha(\pi/L)^2 t}$$

Single mode ($n = 1$) with decay time:

$$\tau = \frac{L^2}{\alpha \pi^2}$$

**Physical meaning**: Temperature profile maintains sinusoidal shape but amplitude decays exponentially.

**Time to equilibrium**: $t \sim L^2/\alpha$ (diffusive scaling—time increases as square of length).

## Wiedemann-Franz Law

For **metals**, heat conduction dominated by **electrons** (not atoms).

**Electrical conductivity**: $\sigma$ (electrons carry charge)

**Thermal conductivity**: $\kappa$ (electrons carry energy)

**Wiedemann-Franz law**:

$$\boxed{\frac{\kappa}{\sigma T} = L_0}$$

where **Lorenz number**:

$$L_0 = \frac{\pi^2 k_B^2}{3e^2} = 2.44 \times 10^{-8} \text{ W}\Omega\text{/K}^2$$

**Universal constant** for metals!

**Physical origin**: Both transport processes depend on electron mean free path $\lambda_e$ similarly, so ratio nearly independent of material details.

**Experimental verification**: Excellent for most metals at moderate temperatures.

**Examples**:
- Copper: $L = 2.23 \times 10^{-8}$ W·Ω/K²
- Silver: $L = 2.31 \times 10^{-8}$ W·Ω/K²
- Aluminum: $L = 2.14 \times 10^{-8}$ W·Ω/K²

All close to theoretical $L_0$!

## Example 1: Thermal Conductivity of Helium

Helium at 300 K: $d = 2.2 \times 10^{-10}$ m, $m = 4$ u. Calculate $\kappa$.

**Solution:**

$$\kappa = \frac{k_B}{2\sqrt{2}\pi d^2} \sqrt{\frac{k_B T}{\pi m}}$$

$$= \frac{1.381 \times 10^{-23}}{2\sqrt{2}\pi (2.2 \times 10^{-10})^2} \sqrt{\frac{(1.381 \times 10^{-23})(300)}{\pi(4)(1.66 \times 10^{-27})}}$$

$$= \frac{1.381 \times 10^{-23}}{3.04 \times 10^{-19}} \times 331$$

$$= 0.150 \text{ W/(m·K)}$$

**Experimental value**: $\kappa_{\text{He}} = 0.156$ W/(m·K) at 300 K

Good agreement!

**Answer:** $\kappa = 0.15$ W/(m·K)

## Example 2: Steady-State Heat Flow

Rod length $L = 1$ m, $\kappa = 50$ W/(m·K), cross section $A = 0.01$ m². Ends at $T_1 = 100°C$ and $T_2 = 20°C$. Find heat flow rate.

**Solution:**

$$Q = \frac{\kappa A(T_1 - T_2)}{L}$$

$$= \frac{(50)(0.01)(100 - 20)}{1}$$

$$= 40 \text{ W}$$

**Physical interpretation**: 40 Joules per second flow through rod from hot to cold end.

**Thermal resistance**: $R_{th} = L/(\kappa A) = 1/(50 \times 0.01) = 2$ K/W

$$Q = \Delta T/R_{th} = 80/2 = 40$ W ✓

**Answer:** $Q = 40$ W

## Example 3: Thermal Diffusion Time

How long for temperature disturbance to diffuse distance $L = 10$ cm in air ($\alpha = 2 \times 10^{-5}$ m²/s)?

**Solution:**

Diffusive time scale:

$$t \sim \frac{L^2}{\alpha} = \frac{(0.1)^2}{2 \times 10^{-5}}$$

$$= \frac{0.01}{2 \times 10^{-5}} = 500 \text{ s} \approx 8.3 \text{ min}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Heat diffusion is slow process—takes several minutes to equilibrate over 10 cm distance.

This is why insulation works—air gaps prevent rapid heat transfer.

**Answer:** $t \approx 8$ minutes

---

## Example 4: Wiedemann-Franz for Copper

Copper at 300 K: $\sigma = 6.0 \times 10^7$ (Ω·m)⁻¹, $\kappa = 400$ W/(m·K). Verify Wiedemann-Franz law.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{\kappa}{\sigma T} = \frac{400}{(6.0 \times 10^7)(300)}$$

$$= \frac{400}{1.8 \times 10^{10}} = 2.22 \times 10^{-8} \text{ W}\Omega\text{/K}^2$$

**Theoretical Lorenz number**: $L_0 = 2.44 \times 10^{-8}$ W·Ω/K²

**Ratio**: $L/L_0 = 2.22/2.44 = 0.91$

Within ~10% of theoretical value—good agreement!

**Answer:** $L = 2.22 \times 10^{-8}$ W·Ω/K² (close to $L_0$)

## Example 5: Cooling Rod

Rod initially at $T_0 = 100°C$ with ends suddenly cooled to $0°C$. If $L = 1$ m and $\alpha = 10^{-5}$ m²/s, estimate time for center to cool to $50°C$.

**Solution:**

Characteristic cooling time (fundamental mode):

$$\tau = \frac{L^2}{\alpha \pi^2} = \frac{(1)^2}{(10^{-5})(\pi^2)}$$

$$= \frac{1}{9.87 \times 10^{-5}} = 1.01 \times 10^4 \text{ s} \approx 2.8 \text{ hours}$$

Temperature at center: $T(L/2, t) \approx T_0 e^{-t/\tau}$

For $T = T_0/2$: $e^{-t/\tau} = 1/2$

$$t = \tau \ln 2 = (1.01 \times 10^4)(0.693) = 7.0 \times 10^3 \text{ s} \approx 2 \text{ hours}$$

**Physical interpretation**: Thermal equilibration over 1 m scale takes ~2 hours for typical thermal diffusivity.

**Answer:** $t \approx 2$ hours
