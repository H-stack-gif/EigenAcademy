# Article 7.1: Kinetic Theory of Gases

Statistical mechanics provides microscopic foundations for thermodynamics through partition functions and ensemble averages, but it often obscures the physical mechanisms underlying gas behavior. **Kinetic theory** bridges this gap, deriving macroscopic properties directly from molecular motion. The key insight: gas pressure arises from countless molecular collisions with container walls, each transferring momentum. Temperature reflects average molecular kinetic energy. These simple ideas, combined with statistical reasoning, yield quantitative predictions for pressure, energy, and molecular speeds.

The **ideal gas model** assumes molecules are point particles undergoing elastic collisions, with negligible interaction time compared to free flight between collisions. While crude, this approximation works remarkably well for dilute gases. From it, we derive the fundamental relation $P = \frac{1}{3}nm\langle v^2 \rangle$ connecting pressure to molecular speed. The **equipartition theorem** emerges naturally: each translational degree of freedom contributes $\frac{1}{2}k_B T$ to average energy. **Molecular effusion**—gas escaping through small holes—follows Graham's law, with lighter molecules effusing faster.

This article develops kinetic theory from molecular assumptions, derives pressure from collision dynamics, calculates root-mean-square speed, establishes the connection between temperature and kinetic energy, explores equipartition for different molecular types, treats molecular effusion and Graham's law, and demonstrates applications to gas properties. The mathematics involves statistical averages over velocity distributions, momentum transfer calculations, dimensional analysis, and integration techniques.

## Molecular Assumptions and Ideal Gas Model

**Kinetic theory assumptions**:

1. **Large number** of molecules: $N \sim 10^{23}$ (statistical treatment valid)
2. **Point particles**: Molecular size $\ll$ intermolecular separation
3. **Random motion**: Velocities distributed isotropically (no preferred direction)
4. **Elastic collisions**: Kinetic energy conserved in collisions
5. **No intermolecular forces**: Molecules interact only during brief collisions
6. **Newtonian mechanics**: Molecules obey classical laws

These assumptions define the **ideal gas model**. Real gases deviate at high pressure (assumption 2 breaks down) or low temperature (assumption 5 breaks down), but for many conditions, ideal gas theory is excellent.

**Molecular parameters**:
- Mass: $m$ (single molecule)
- Diameter: $d$ (collision size, typically $d \sim 10^{-10}$ m)
- Number density: $n = N/V$ (molecules per unit volume)

## Pressure from Molecular Collisions

Consider **cubic container** with side length $L$, containing $N$ molecules.

**Single molecule** with velocity component $v_x$ perpendicular to wall:
- Time between wall collisions: $\Delta t = 2L/|v_x|$
- Momentum change per collision: $\Delta p = 2m|v_x|$ (elastic reversal)
- Force on wall from this molecule: $F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \frac{2m|v_x|}{2L/|v_x|} = \frac{mv_x^2}{L}$

**All molecules**: Average force on wall:

$$F = \frac{m}{L}\sum_{i=1}^N v_{x,i}^2 = \frac{Nm}{L}\langle v_x^2 \rangle$$

**Pressure** (force per unit area $A = L^2$):

$$P = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{Nm\langle v_x^2 \rangle}{L^3} = nm\langle v_x^2 \rangle$$

**Isotropy**: For random motion, $\langle v_x^2 \rangle = \langle v_y^2 \rangle = \langle v_z^2 \rangle$

$$\langle v^2 \rangle = \langle v_x^2 \rangle + \langle v_y^2 \rangle + \langle v_z^2 \rangle = 3\langle v_x^2 \rangle$$

$$\langle v_x^2 \rangle = \frac{1}{3}\langle v^2 \rangle$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{P = \frac{1}{3}nm\langle v^2 \rangle}$$

This is the **fundamental kinetic theory result** for pressure.

## Root-Mean-Square Speed

Define **root-mean-square (RMS) speed**:

$$\boxed{v_{rms} = \sqrt{\langle v^2 \rangle}}$$

From ideal gas law $PV = Nk_B T$ and kinetic result $P = \frac{1}{3}nm\langle v^2 \rangle$:

$$\frac{1}{3}nm\langle v^2 \rangle \cdot V = Nk_B T$$

$$\frac{1}{3}Nm\langle v^2 \rangle = Nk_B T$$

$$\langle v^2 \rangle = \frac{3k_B T}{m}$$

**Root-mean-square speed**:

$$\boxed{v_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_B T}{m}}}$$

**Physical interpretation**: RMS speed increases with temperature and decreases with molecular mass.

**Examples at $T = 300$ K**:
- Hydrogen ($m = 2$ u): $v_{rms} = 1930$ m/s
- Nitrogen ($m = 28$ u): $v_{rms} = 517$ m/s
- Oxygen ($m = 32$ u): $v_{rms} = 483$ m/s

where $1$ u $= 1.66 \times 10^{-27}$ kg.

Molecular speeds are **very fast** (hundreds of meters per second at room temperature)!

## Internal Energy

**Average translational kinetic energy** per molecule:

$$\langle E_k \rangle = \frac{1}{2}m\langle v^2 \rangle = \frac{1}{2}m \cdot \frac{3k_B T}{m} = \frac{3}{2}k_B T$$

**Total internal energy** (monatomic ideal gas):

$$\boxed{U = N \langle E_k \rangle = \frac{3}{2}Nk_B T}$$

This confirms the result from statistical mechanics (canonical ensemble, Article 5.3).

**Temperature interpretation**: Temperature is measure of average kinetic energy per degree of freedom:

$$k_B T = \frac{2}{3}\langle E_k \rangle$$

Temperature is **not** average energy (off by factor $2/3$), but proportional to it.

## Equipartition Theorem

**Equipartition theorem** (from Article 5.4): Each quadratic term in energy contributes $\frac{1}{2}k_B T$ to average energy.

**Translational motion**: 3 degrees of freedom ($v_x^2$, $v_y^2$, $v_z^2$)

$$U_{trans} = 3 \times \frac{1}{2}Nk_B T = \frac{3}{2}Nk_B T$$

**Molecular types**:

1. **Monatomic** (He, Ne, Ar): Only translation
   $$U = \frac{3}{2}Nk_B T, \quad C_V = \frac{3}{2}Nk_B = \frac{3}{2}nR$$

2. **Diatomic** (H₂, N₂, O₂): Translation + rotation (2 axes perpendicular to bond)
   $$U = \frac{5}{2}Nk_B T, \quad C_V = \frac{5}{2}nR$$
   
   (Classical approximation; vibrational modes frozen at room temperature)

3. **Polyatomic** (CO₂, NH₃): Translation + rotation (3 axes for nonlinear)
   $$U = 3Nk_B T, \quad C_V = 3nR$$

**Heat capacity ratio**:

$$\gamma = \frac{C_P}{C_V} = \frac{C_V + nR}{C_V}$$

- Monatomic: $\gamma = 5/3 = 1.67$
- Diatomic: $\gamma = 7/5 = 1.40$
- Polyatomic: $\gamma = 4/3 = 1.33$

These predictions match experiments well at moderate temperatures.

## Molecular Effusion and Graham's Law

**Effusion**: Gas molecules escaping through small hole into vacuum.

**Assumptions**:
- Hole diameter $\ll$ mean free path (molecular flow, no collisions near hole)
- Hole area $A$ small (container pressure nearly constant)

**Effusion rate**: Number of molecules hitting wall area $A$ per unit time.

Consider molecules with velocity component $v_x$ (perpendicular to wall):
- Number density with velocity $(v_x, v_y, v_z)$: $n \cdot f(\mathbf{v}) d^3v$
- Flux hitting wall: $v_x \cdot n f(\mathbf{v}) d^3v$ (distance traveled per time × density)

**Total effusion rate**:

$$\frac{dN}{dt} = A \int_{v_x > 0} v_x \cdot n f(\mathbf{v}) dv_x dv_y dv_z$$

For isotropic distribution with average speed $\langle v \rangle$:

$$\boxed{\frac{dN}{dt} = \frac{1}{4}nA\langle v \rangle}$$

Factor $1/4$ arises from integration over hemisphere and weighting by $v_x$.

**Graham's law**: Effusion rate depends on molecular mass.

For two gases at same temperature:

$$\frac{(dN/dt)_1}{(dN/dt)_2} = \frac{\langle v_1 \rangle}{\langle v_2 \rangle} = \sqrt{\frac{m_2}{m_1}}$$

**Lighter molecules effuse faster** (inversely proportional to $\sqrt{m}$).

**Application**: Uranium isotope separation (enrichment). ²³⁵UF₆ vs ²³⁸UF₆ effuse at slightly different rates:

$$\frac{v_{235}}{v_{238}} = \sqrt{\frac{352}{349}} = 1.0043$$

Small difference requires many stages, but process works!

## Dimensional Analysis

Kinetic theory results can often be estimated by **dimensional analysis**.

**Example**: Predict $v_{rms}$ from $T$, $m$, and $k_B$.

Need velocity (dimensions $[LT^{-1}]$) from:
- $k_B T$: energy (dimensions $[ML^2T^{-2}]$)
- $m$: mass (dimensions $[M]$)

**Combination**: $\frac{k_B T}{m}$ has dimensions $\frac{[ML^2T^{-2}]}{[M]} = [L^2T^{-2}]$

Taking square root: $\sqrt{k_B T/m}$ has dimensions $[LT^{-1}]$ ✓

**Result**: $v_{rms} \sim \sqrt{k_B T/m}$

Kinetic theory gives exact coefficient: $v_{rms} = \sqrt{3k_B T/m}$.

## Example 1: RMS Speed of Nitrogen

Calculate $v_{rms}$ for nitrogen (N₂, $m = 28$ u) at $T = 300$ K.

**Solution:**

Molecular mass: $m = 28 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27}$ kg $= 4.65 \times 10^{-26}$ kg

$$v_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_B T}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3(1.381 \times 10^{-23})(300)}{4.65 \times 10^{-26}}}$$

$$= \sqrt{\frac{1.243 \times 10^{-20}}{4.65 \times 10^{-26}}} = \sqrt{2.67 \times 10^5}$$

$$= 517 \text{ m/s}$$

**Speed of sound** in nitrogen at 300 K: $v_s \approx 350$ m/s

Molecular speed is **faster** than sound speed (sound is collective wave motion, not individual molecular motion).

**Answer:** $v_{rms} = 517$ m/s

## Example 2: Pressure Calculation

Container holds $10^{23}$ nitrogen molecules at $v_{rms} = 500$ m/s in volume $V = 0.1$ m³. Find pressure.

**Solution:**

From $P = \frac{1}{3}nm\langle v^2 \rangle$ and $v_{rms} = \sqrt{\langle v^2 \rangle}$:

$$P = \frac{1}{3}nm v_{rms}^2$$

Number density: $n = \frac{N}{V} = \frac{10^{23}}{0.1} = 10^{24}$ m⁻³

Molecular mass: $m = 4.65 \times 10^{-26}$ kg

$$P = \frac{1}{3}(10^{24})(4.65 \times 10^{-26})(500)^2$$

$$= \frac{1}{3}(10^{24})(4.65 \times 10^{-26})(2.5 \times 10^5)$$

$$= \frac{1}{3}(1.16 \times 10^4) = 3.87 \times 10^3 \text{ Pa}$$

**Verification** using ideal gas law:

$$P = \frac{Nk_B T}{V}$$

From $v_{rms} = 500$ m/s:

$$T = \frac{mv_{rms}^2}{3k_B} = \frac{(4.65 \times 10^{-26})(2.5 \times 10^5)}{3(1.381 \times 10^{-23})} = 280 \text{ K}$$

$$P = \frac{(10^{23})(1.381 \times 10^{-23})(280)}{0.1} = 3.87 \times 10^3 \text{ Pa}$$ ✓

**Answer:** $P = 3.87$ kPa

## Example 3: Internal Energy

Calculate internal energy for 2 moles of helium at $T = 400$ K.

**Solution:**

Helium is **monatomic**: $U = \frac{3}{2}Nk_B T$

Number of molecules: $N = 2 \times N_A = 2(6.02 \times 10^{23}) = 1.204 \times 10^{24}$

$$U = \frac{3}{2}(1.204 \times 10^{24})(1.381 \times 10^{-23})(400)$$

$$= \frac{3}{2}(1.204)(1.381)(400) \times 10^1$$

$$= \frac{3}{2}(665.4) \times 10 = 9981 \text{ J} \approx 10.0 \text{ kJ}$$

**Alternative** using molar form:

$$U = \frac{3}{2}nRT = \frac{3}{2}(2)(8.314)(400) = 9977 \text{ J}$$ ✓

**Answer:** $U = 10.0$ kJ

## Example 4: Effusion Rate Ratio

Hydrogen (H₂, $m = 2$ u) and oxygen (O₂, $m = 32$ u) effuse through same hole at same temperature. Find rate ratio.

**Solution:**

Graham's law:

$$\frac{(dN/dt)_{H_2}}{(dN/dt)_{O_2}} = \sqrt{\frac{m_{O_2}}{m_{H_2}}} = \sqrt{\frac{32}{2}} = \sqrt{16} = 4$$

Hydrogen effuses **4 times faster** than oxygen.

**Physical explanation**: Lighter molecules move faster at same temperature, so hit hole more frequently.

**Application**: This principle used in gas separation processes.

**Answer:** Hydrogen effuses 4 times faster than oxygen

---

## Example 5: Heat Capacity Ratio

Predict $\gamma = C_P/C_V$ for diatomic nitrogen at room temperature.

**Solution:**

Diatomic molecule: 3 translational + 2 rotational degrees of freedom (classical)

$$C_V = \frac{5}{2}nR$$

$$C_P = C_V + nR = \frac{5}{2}nR + nR = \frac{7}{2}nR$$

$$\gamma = \frac{C_P}{C_V} = \frac{7/2}{5/2} = \frac{7}{5} = 1.40$$

**Experimental value**: $\gamma_{N_2} = 1.40$ (excellent agreement!)

**Note**: At room temperature, vibrational modes are not excited (quantum effect—vibrational energy spacing $\hbar\omega \gg k_B T$ for N₂).

**Answer:** $\gamma = 1.40$
