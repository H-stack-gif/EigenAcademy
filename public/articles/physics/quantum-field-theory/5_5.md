# Quantum Field Theory Topic 5.5: Running Coupling and Renormalization Group Equations

The renormalization group describes how coupling constants and masses evolve with the energy scale at which they are measured, encoding the fundamental principle that physics should be independent of the arbitrary renormalization scale $\mu$ introduced during regularization. The Callan-Symanzik equation $[\mu\partial/\partial\mu + \beta(g)\partial/\partial g - n\gamma]\Gamma^{(n)} = 0$ captures this scale invariance for $n$-point Green's functions $\Gamma^{(n)}$, where the beta function $\beta(g) = \mu dg/d\mu$ determines how the coupling runs, and the anomalous dimension $\gamma$ describes how composite operators acquire non-canonical scaling. In quantum electrodynamics, the one-loop beta function $\beta(\alpha) = 2\alpha^2/(3\pi) > 0$ is positive, causing the fine structure constant to increase logarithmically with energy toward a Landau pole at exponentially high scales—signaling that QED cannot be extrapolated to arbitrarily high energies without modification. Quantum chromodynamics exhibits the opposite behavior: the one-loop beta function $\beta(\alpha_s) = -(11 - 2n_f/3)\alpha_s^2/(4\pi)$ is negative for $n_f < 17$ quark flavors, yielding **asymptotic freedom** where $\alpha_s$ decreases logarithmically at high energies, allowing perturbative calculations at short distances while growing strong at low energies to produce confinement. Fixed points $\beta(g^*) = 0$ where couplings stop running correspond to scale-invariant theories, often signaling conformal field theories with enhanced symmetries, while anomalous dimensions $\gamma_\phi$ modify the scaling behavior of composite operators beyond their canonical mass dimensions, playing crucial roles in deep inelastic scattering sum rules and conformal field theory applications.

The Callan-Symanzik equation arises from demanding independence of bare parameters on the arbitrary renormalization scale $\mu$. Bare quantities like $g_0$ and $m_0$ are scale-independent:

$$\mu\frac{d}{d\mu}g_0 = 0, \quad \mu\frac{d}{d\mu}m_0 = 0$$

but renormalized parameters depend on $\mu$ through:

$$g_0 = \mu^\epsilon Z_g(\mu, g, \epsilon)g$$

where $\epsilon$ regulates UV divergences in dimensional regularization. Taking the derivative:

$$0 = \mu\frac{d}{d\mu}\left[\mu^\epsilon Z_g g\right] = \epsilon\mu^\epsilon Z_g g + \mu^\epsilon g\mu\frac{dZ_g}{d\mu} + \mu^\epsilon Z_g\mu\frac{dg}{d\mu}$$

As $\epsilon \to 0$, the first term vanishes and we find:

$$\mu\frac{dg}{d\mu} = -\frac{g}{Z_g}\mu\frac{dZ_g}{d\mu}$$

Defining the beta function $\beta(g) = \mu dg/d\mu$ and expanding $Z_g$ in minimal subtraction:

$$Z_g = 1 + \frac{c_1}{\epsilon}g^2 + \frac{c_2}{\epsilon}g^3 + \cdots$$

we extract:

$$\beta(g) = -g\frac{\partial \ln Z_g}{\partial \ln\mu}\bigg|_{\epsilon = 0}$$

Computing this from one-loop Feynman diagrams yields the one-loop beta function for each theory.

### Example 1: Deriving the QED Beta Function

Calculate $\beta(\alpha)$ for QED at one-loop from vacuum polarization.

**Solution:**

The photon wave function renormalization at one-loop:

$$Z_A = 1 - \frac{e^2}{12\pi^2\epsilon} + O(e^4)$$

The bare charge $e_0 = Z_e Z_A^{1/2} e$ where Ward identity gives $Z_e = 1$. Thus:

$$e_0 = \sqrt{Z_A}\,e = \left(1 - \frac{e^2}{24\pi^2\epsilon}\right)e$$

Requiring $\mu de_0/d\mu = 0$:

$$0 = \sqrt{Z_A}\mu\frac{de}{d\mu} + e\mu\frac{d}{d\mu}\sqrt{Z_A}$$

$$\mu\frac{de}{d\mu} = -\frac{e}{2Z_A}\mu\frac{dZ_A}{d\mu}$$

From $Z_A = 1 - e^2/(12\pi^2\epsilon)$:

$$\mu\frac{dZ_A}{d\mu} = -\frac{2e}{12\pi^2\epsilon}\mu\frac{de}{d\mu}$$

Substituting:

$$\mu\frac{de}{d\mu} = -\frac{e}{2}\left(-\frac{2e}{12\pi^2\epsilon}\mu\frac{de}{d\mu}\right)\times \frac{1}{Z_A}$$

Solve for $\beta(e) = \mu de/d\mu$ by taking $\epsilon \to 0$ with finite terms:

$$\beta(e) = \frac{e^3}{12\pi^2} + O(e^5)$$

In terms of fine structure constant $\alpha = e^2/(4\pi)$:

$$\beta(\alpha) = \mu\frac{d\alpha}{d\mu} = \mu\frac{d}{d\mu}\left(\frac{e^2}{4\pi}\right) = \frac{2e\beta(e)}{4\pi} = \frac{2e \cdot e^3/(12\pi^2)}{4\pi}$$

$$= \frac{e^4}{24\pi^3} = \frac{(4\pi\alpha)^2}{24\pi^3} = \frac{\alpha^2}{\pi} \times \frac{4\pi}{6} = \frac{2\alpha^2}{3\pi}$$

The positive sign means $\alpha$ **increases** with $\mu$. The solution:

$$\alpha(\mu) = \frac{\alpha(\mu_0)}{1 - (\alpha(\mu_0)/(3\pi))\ln(\mu/\mu_0)}$$

At higher energy, $\alpha$ grows. For $\mu_0 = m_e$ and $\mu = m_Z$:

$$\alpha(m_Z) \approx \frac{1/137}{1 - (1/137)\times(1/(3\pi))\ln(m_Z/m_e)} \approx \frac{1}{128}$$

**Answer:** QED beta function $\beta(\alpha) = 2\alpha^2/(3\pi) > 0$ (positive); $\alpha$ increases with energy.

The QCD beta function exhibits dramatically different behavior due to non-Abelian gauge structure. Computing the one-loop corrections to the gluon propagator from quark loops (vacuum polarization) and gluon loops (self-energy) plus ghost contributions:

$$\beta(\alpha_s) = -\frac{(11C_A - 4T_F n_f)}{12\pi}\alpha_s^2$$

where $C_A = 3$ (adjoint Casimir for SU(3)), $T_F = 1/2$ (fundamental representation normalization), and $n_f$ is the number of active quark flavors. For $n_f = 6$:

$$\beta(\alpha_s) = -\frac{(33 - 8)}{12\pi}\alpha_s^2 = -\frac{25\alpha_s^2}{12\pi}$$

The negative sign—arising from the $(11C_A - 4T_F n_f)$ factor where gluon self-interactions dominate over quark screening—produces **asymptotic freedom**: $\alpha_s$ decreases at high energies. This revolutionary discovery (Gross, Wilczek, Politzer, 1973) earned the Nobel Prize and established QCD as the theory of strong interactions.

### Example 2: Asymptotic Freedom in QCD

Solve the QCD running coupling equation and demonstrate asymptotic freedom.

**Solution:**

The one-loop beta function:

$$\beta(\alpha_s) = -\frac{b_0 \alpha_s^2}{4\pi}$$

where $b_0 = 11 - 2n_f/3 = 25/3$ for $n_f = 6$. The differential equation:

$$\mu\frac{d\alpha_s}{d\mu} = -\frac{b_0\alpha_s^2}{4\pi}$$

Separate variables:

$$\frac{d\alpha_s}{\alpha_s^2} = -\frac{b_0}{4\pi}\frac{d\mu}{\mu}$$

Integrate from $\mu_0$ to $\mu$:

$$-\frac{1}{\alpha_s(\mu)} + \frac{1}{\alpha_s(\mu_0)} = -\frac{b_0}{4\pi}\ln\frac{\mu}{\mu_0}$$

Solve for $\alpha_s(\mu)$:

$$\alpha_s(\mu) = \frac{\alpha_s(\mu_0)}{1 + \alpha_s(\mu_0)(b_0/(4\pi))\ln(\mu/\mu_0)}$$

Define $\Lambda_{\text{QCD}}$ by setting $\alpha_s(\mu_0) = 4\pi/(b_0\ln(\mu_0/\Lambda))$:

$$\alpha_s(\mu) = \frac{4\pi}{b_0\ln(\mu/\Lambda_{\text{QCD}})}$$

As $\mu \to \infty$, $\alpha_s(\mu) \to 0$ (asymptotic freedom). As $\mu \to \Lambda_{\text{QCD}} \approx 200$ MeV, $\alpha_s \to \infty$ (confinement scale).

Numerically, at Z mass:

$$\alpha_s(m_Z) = 0.1179 \pm 0.0010$$

At charm mass $\mu = m_c \approx 1.3$ GeV:

$$\alpha_s(m_c) = \frac{0.1179}{1 - 0.1179 \times (25/(12\pi))\ln(1.3/91.2)} \approx 0.40$$

Much larger! At $\mu = 1$ GeV:

$$\alpha_s(1\text{ GeV}) \approx 0.5$$

Perturbation theory breaks down below $\sim 1$ GeV, requiring non-perturbative methods (lattice QCD).

**Physical interpretation**: At short distances (high $\mu$), quarks and gluons behave as free particles—asymptotic freedom allows perturbative QCD for jet physics, deep inelastic scattering. At long distances (low $\mu$), $\alpha_s$ grows strong, confining quarks into hadrons.

**Answer:** QCD $\beta(\alpha_s) = -b_0\alpha_s^2/(4\pi)$ with $b_0 = 25/3$ (negative). Solution $\alpha_s(\mu) = 4\pi/(b_0\ln(\mu/\Lambda_{\text{QCD}}))$ decreases at high $\mu$ (asymptotic freedom).

The two-loop and higher-order beta functions modify the running at higher precision. The general form:

$$\beta(\alpha_s) = -\alpha_s\left[\frac{b_0\alpha_s}{4\pi} + \frac{b_1\alpha_s^2}{(4\pi)^2} + \frac{b_2\alpha_s^3}{(4\pi)^3} + \cdots\right]$$

where the coefficients:

$$b_0 = 11 - \frac{2n_f}{3}, \quad b_1 = 102 - \frac{38n_f}{3}$$

are scheme-independent, while $b_2$ and higher depend on renormalization scheme. The two-loop running coupling:

$$\alpha_s(\mu) = \frac{4\pi}{b_0 L}\left[1 - \frac{2b_1\ln L}{b_0^2 L} + O(1/L^2)\right]$$

where $L = \ln(\mu/\Lambda)$ provides next-to-leading-order precision. Modern determinations of $\alpha_s$ from multiple observables (jets, $\tau$ decays, lattice) achieve sub-percent precision, making $\alpha_s$ the least well-known fundamental coupling despite being measured in numerous ways.

### Example 3: Comparing QED and QCD Running

Calculate the ratio $\alpha_s(\mu_1)/\alpha_s(\mu_2)$ versus $\alpha(\mu_1)/\alpha(\mu_2)$ for $\mu_1 = 1$ GeV, $\mu_2 = 100$ GeV.

**Solution:**

**QED**:

$$\frac{\alpha(\mu_2)}{\alpha(\mu_1)} = \frac{1 - (\alpha/3\pi)\ln(\mu_1/m_e)}{1 - (\alpha/3\pi)\ln(\mu_2/m_e)}$$

With $\alpha \approx 1/137$, $m_e = 0.5$ MeV, $\mu_1 = 1$ GeV, $\mu_2 = 100$ GeV:

$$\ln\frac{\mu_1}{m_e} = \ln 2000 \approx 7.6, \quad \ln\frac{\mu_2}{m_e} = \ln(2\times 10^5) \approx 12.2$$

$$\frac{\alpha(\mu_2)}{\alpha(\mu_1)} = \frac{1 - (1/137)/(3\pi) \times 7.6}{1 - (1/137)/(3\pi) \times 12.2} = \frac{1 - 0.0059}{1 - 0.0095} \approx \frac{0.994}{0.990} \approx 1.004$$

QED coupling changes by 0.4% over two orders of magnitude.

**QCD**:

$$\frac{\alpha_s(\mu_1)}{\alpha_s(\mu_2)} = \frac{1 + \alpha_s(\mu_2)(b_0/(4\pi))\ln(\mu_2/\mu_1)}{1}$$

From $\alpha_s(\mu_2)$ to $\alpha_s(\mu_1)$ where $\mu_1 < \mu_2$:

$$\alpha_s(\mu_1) = \frac{\alpha_s(\mu_2)}{1 + \alpha_s(\mu_2)(b_0/(4\pi))\ln(\mu_1/\mu_2)}$$

With $\ln(\mu_1/\mu_2) = \ln(1/100) = -4.6$:

$$\alpha_s(1) = \frac{0.12}{1 + 0.12 \times (25/(12\pi)) \times (-4.6)} = \frac{0.12}{1 - 0.364} \approx \frac{0.12}{0.636} \approx 0.19$$

 ore accurately:

$$\alpha_s(1\text{ GeV}) \approx 0.5, \quad \alpha_s(100\text{ GeV}) \approx 0.10$$

$$\frac{\alpha_s(1)}{\alpha_s(100)} \approx 5$$

QCD coupling changes by factor of 5 (increases at low energy), while QED barely changes!

**Answer:** QED: $\alpha(100)/\alpha(1) \approx 1.004$ (0.4% change); QCD: $\alpha_s(1)/\alpha_s(100) \approx 5$ (factor 5 change—dramatic running).

Anomalous dimensions describe how composite operators scale differently from their canonical dimension due to quantum corrections. For a composite operator $\mathcal{O}$ with canonical dimension $d$, quantum corrections modify its scaling under RG flow:

$$\mu\frac{d}{d\mu}\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle = (d + \gamma_\mathcal{O})\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle$$

where $\gamma_\mathcal{O}$ is the anomalous dimension. For instance, the fermion field has canonical dimension $3/2$ but acquires anomalous dimension from wave function renormalization:

$$\gamma_\psi = \frac{\mu}{2Z_\psi}\frac{dZ_\psi}{d\mu}$$

At one-loop in QED, $Z_\psi = 1 - e^2/(8\pi^2\epsilon)$ gives:

$$\gamma_\psi = -\frac{e^2}{16\pi^2} = -\frac{\alpha}{4\pi}$$

The negative anomalous dimension means fermion fields scale slower than their canonical dimension predicts—a consequence of quantum dressing.

### Example 4: Anomalous Dimension of $\bar{\psi}\psi$ Operator

Calculate the anomalous dimension of the mass operator $\bar{\psi}\psi$ in QED.

**Solution:**

The operator $\mathcal{O} = \bar{\psi}\psi$ has canonical dimension $d = 3$ (two fermions, each dimension $3/2$). Under renormalization:

$$\mathcal{O}_0 = Z_\psi \mathcal{O}$$

The anomalous dimension:

$$\gamma_m = -\mu\frac{d\ln Z_m}{d\mu}$$

where $Z_m = Z_\psi$ for the mass operator. At one-loop:

$$Z_\psi = 1 - \frac{e^2}{8\pi^2\epsilon}$$

$$\gamma_m = \mu\frac{d}{d\mu}\frac{e^2}{8\pi^2\epsilon}$$

Using $\mu de^2/d\mu = 2e\beta(e) = 2e \cdot e^3/(12\pi^2)$:

$$\gamma_m = \frac{2e \cdot e^3/(12\pi^2)}{8\pi^2\epsilon} = \frac{e^4}{48\pi^4\epsilon}$$

Taking the finite part as $\epsilon \to 0$ with $\beta(e)$:

$$\gamma_m = \frac{3\alpha}{2\pi}$$

The mass operator's scaling:

$$m(\mu) = m(\mu_0)\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu_0}\right)^{\gamma_m/\beta}$$

For QED with $\beta(\alpha) = 2\alpha^2/(3\pi)$ and $\gamma_m = 3\alpha/(2\pi)$:

$$\frac{\gamma_m}{\beta} = \frac{3\alpha/(2\pi)}{2\alpha^2/(3\pi)} = \frac{9}{4\alpha}$$

The mass evolves slowly in QED but rapidly in QCD where $\gamma_m^{\text{QCD}} \sim -\alpha_s/\pi$ with larger coupling.

**Deep inelastic scattering**: Anomalous dimensions of twist-2 operators (like $\bar{\psi}\gamma^\mu D^n\psi$) determine the scaling violations in structure functions, precisely measured at HERA and used to extract $\alpha_s$.

**Answer:** Mass operator $\bar{\psi}\psi$ has anomalous dimension $\gamma_m = 3\alpha/(2\pi)$ at one-loop QED; modifies scaling from canonical dimension 3.

Fixed points of the beta function $\beta(g^*) = 0$ correspond to scale-invariant theories where the coupling stops running. At a fixed point, the theory exhibits conformal symmetry (scale plus special conformal invariance), leading to powerful constraints and exact solutions in some cases. The trivial fixed point $g^* = 0$ describes free theories (non-interacting). Non-trivial fixed points with $g^* \neq 0$ correspond to interacting conformal field theories, extensively studied in 2D (minimal models, Virasoro algebra) and recently in higher dimensions (conformal bootstrap).

### Example 5: Fixed Points and Conformal Field Theories

Analyze fixed point structure of $\phi^4$ theory in $d = 4-\epsilon$ dimensions.

**Solution:**

The $\phi^4$ theory in $d$ dimensions has coupling $[\lambda] = 4 - d$ (mass dimension). In $d = 4-\epsilon$:

$$[\lambda] = \epsilon$$

Define dimensionless coupling $g = \lambda\mu^{-\epsilon}$. The beta function:

$$\beta(g) = -\epsilon g + \beta_1 g^2 + O(g^3)$$

where $\beta_1 = 3/(16\pi^2)$ from one-loop calculation (momentum integrals).

Fixed points: $\beta(g^*) = 0$:

$$g^* = \frac{\epsilon}{\beta_1} = \frac{16\pi^2\epsilon}{3}$$

For small $\epsilon$, there's a non-trivial fixed point $g^* \sim \epsilon$. At this fixed point:

$$\beta'(g^*) = -\epsilon + 2\beta_1 g^* = -\epsilon + 2\epsilon = +\epsilon > 0$$

This is an **IR-stable fixed point** (flows toward in IR, away in UV). The theory at $g^*$ is scale-invariant—a non-trivial CFT.

**Critical phenomena**: The Wilson-Fisher fixed point describes second-order phase transitions (Ising model, liquid-gas transition). The critical exponents are determined by anomalous dimensions at the fixed point:

$$\nu = \frac{1}{2 - \eta/2}, \quad \eta = \gamma_\phi(g^*)$$

For $\epsilon = 1$ (3D), predictions match experiments ($\nu \approx 0.63$).

**$d=4$**: In $d=4$, $\epsilon = 0$ and fixed point moves to $g^* = 0$ (free theory). $\phi^4$ theory is **trivial** in $d=4$—no interacting continuum limit. This triviality is related to Landau pole: $\lambda(\mu) \to \infty$ at finite energy, signaling breakdown.

**Answer:** $\phi^4$ in $d=4-\epsilon$ has Wilson-Fisher fixed point $g^* = 16\pi^2\epsilon/3$ (IR stable); describes critical phenomena in 3D; trivial in 4D.

The exact beta function $\beta(g)$ can exhibit complicated structure with multiple fixed points (UV, IR), separating different phases of the theory. Banks-Zaks fixed points occur in QCD-like theories with $n_f$ flavors when $11C_A - 4T_F n_f$ becomes small positive—the theory approaches a conformal window where $\alpha_s$ flows to an IR fixed point rather than confining. Seiberg duality in supersymmetric gauge theories relates different theories with the same IR fixed point, demonstrating remarkable non-perturbative equivalences.

### Example 6: Solving Callan-Symanzik Equation

Solve the Callan-Symanzik equation for a scalar field correlation function.

**Solution:**

The Callan-Symanzik equation for two-point function $G^{(2)}(p; \mu, g, m)$:

$$\left[\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu} + \beta(g)\frac{\partial}{\partial g} + m\gamma_m\frac{\partial}{\partial m} - 2\gamma_\phi\right]G^{(2)} = 0$$

The factor $-2\gamma_\phi$ comes from two external legs. Introduce running coupling $g(t)$ where $t = \ln(\mu/\mu_0)$:

$$\frac{dg}{dt} = \beta(g)$$

And running mass $m(t)$:

$$\frac{dm}{dt} = \gamma_m(g) m$$

Rewrite CS equation:

$$\frac{d}{dt}G^{(2)}(p; t, g(t), m(t)) = 2\gamma_\phi(g(t))G^{(2)}$$

Solution:

$$G^{(2)}(p; t) = G^{(2)}(p; 0)\exp\left[2\int_0^t dt'\,\gamma_\phi(g(t'))\right]$$

For small coupling $\gamma_\phi \approx c\alpha$:

$$G^{(2)}(p; \mu) = G^{(2)}(p; \mu_0)\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu_0}\right)^{2c\alpha/\beta'}$$

where $\beta' = d\beta/dg|_{g=0}$. This gives anomalous scaling dimension:

$$\Delta = 1 + \frac{\gamma_\phi}{\beta'}$$

(canonical dimension 1 for scalar propagator $\sim 1/p^2$ plus anomalous correction).

**Physical meaning**: The correlation function doesn't scale canonically due to quantum corrections. Anomalous dimensions modify operator product expansions, sum rules, and critical exponents.

**Answer:** CS equation $[\mu\partial_\mu + \beta\partial_g - n\gamma_\phi]G^{(n)} = 0$ solved by running $g(t)$, $m(t)$; gives $G \sim \exp[\int \gamma dt]$ (anomalous scaling).

### Example 7: QCD Running and $\Lambda_{\text{QCD}}$ Determination

Determine $\Lambda_{\text{QCD}}$ from measured $\alpha_s(m_Z) = 0.1179$.

**Solution:**

The one-loop running:

$$\alpha_s(\mu) = \frac{4\pi}{b_0\ln(\mu/\Lambda_{\text{QCD}})}$$

Solving for $\Lambda$:

$$b_0(n_f=5) = (33 - 10)/3 = 23/3$$

Matching at $m_b$:

$$\Lambda_{\text{QCD}}^{(5)} = m_b\exp\left[-\frac{4\pi}{b_0(n_f=5)\alpha_s(m_b)}\right]$$

With proper matching through thresholds, the $\overline{\text{MS}}$ value:

$$\Lambda_{\overline{\text{MS}}}^{(5)} \approx 210\text{ MeV}$$

for $n_f = 5$ flavors. This sets the scale where $\alpha_s$ becomes strong.

**Two-loop**: Including $b_1$ correction gives:

$$\Lambda_{\overline{\text{MS}}}^{(5)} = 213 \pm 5\text{ MeV}$$

from global fits. This fundamental QCD scale determines hadron masses, confinement dynamics, and non-perturbative physics.

**Answer:** From $\alpha_s(m_Z) = 0.1179$, running down with flavor matching gives $\Lambda_{\overline{\text{MS}}}^{(5)} \approx 210$ MeV (confinement scale).

The renormalization group provides the conceptual framework for understanding how physics changes with scale, from UV completion at the Planck scale down through grand unification, electroweak symmetry breaking, QCD confinement, to nuclear and atomic physics. The running of couplings encodes the hierarchy of scales in nature and determines which interactions dominate at each energy. Asymptotic freedom in QCD enabled precision tests at colliders and revealed the composite nature of hadrons, while the running of all Standard Model couplings toward unification scales motivates grand unified theories and supersymmetry. Modern effective field theory systematizes this scale dependence through matching and running, allowing systematic improvement of predictions while maintaining control over theoretical uncertainties.

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B** — QED vacuum polarization gives $Z_A = 1 - e^2/(12\pi^2\epsilon)$. Beta function $\beta(\alpha) = 2\alpha^2/(3\pi) > 0$ **positive**. $\alpha$ increases with $\mu$: $\alpha(m_Z) \approx 1/128$ vs $\alpha(0) \approx 1/137$. Landau pole at $\mu_L \sim m_e\exp(3\pi/\alpha) \sim 10^{280}$ GeV where $\alpha \to \infty$ (triviality—QED needs UV completion). Contrast QCD: $\beta < 0$ (asymptotic freedom).

**Problem 2: B** — QCD one-loop $\beta(\alpha_s) = -(11C_A - 4T_F n_f)\alpha_s^2/(12\pi)$ with $C_A = 3$, $T_F = 1/2$, $n_f$ quarks. For $n_f < 17$: $11C_A > 4T_F n_f$ → $\beta < 0$. **Gluon self-interactions** (non-Abelian $C_A$ term) dominate over quark screening ($T_F n_f$ term). $\alpha_s$ decreases at high $\mu$: $\alpha_s(\mu) \sim 1/\ln(\mu/\Lambda_{\text{QCD}})$ → 0 as $\mu \to \infty$ (asymptotic freedom). Grows at low $\mu$ → confinement.

**Problem 3: B** — Callan-Symanzik: $[\mu\partial/\partial\mu + \beta(g)\partial/\partial g - n\gamma_\phi]\Gamma^{(n)}(p_i; \mu, g) = 0$ ensures Green's functions independent of arbitrary renormalization scale $\mu$. Beta function $\beta = \mu dg/d\mu$ describes coupling running, anomalous dimension $\gamma$ modifies operator scaling. Solution gives RG flow relating physics at different scales. Essential for all QFT calculations (resummation, precision predictions).

**Problem 4: B** — Fixed point $\beta(g^*) = 0$: coupling stops running → **scale-invariant** theory. Often enhanced to full conformal symmetry (scale + special conformal). Trivial fixed point $g^* = 0$ = free theory. Non-trivial $g^* \neq 0$ = interacting CFT (Wilson-Fisher, Banks-Zaks). Stability: $\beta'(g^*) > 0$ (IR stable), $\beta'(g^*) < 0$ (UV stable). Critical phenomena, string theory, AdS/CFT all involve CFTs.

**Problem 5: B** — Anomalous dimension $\gamma_\phi = \mu(\partial\ln Z_\phi)/\partial\mu$ from wave function renormalization. Modifies operator scaling: $\langle\mathcal{O}(x)\mathcal{O}(0)\rangle \sim 1/x^{2\Delta}$ where $\Delta = d + \gamma$ (canonical $d$ plus anomalous $\gamma$). QED: $\gamma_\psi = -\alpha/(4\pi)$ (negative), mass operator $\gamma_m = 3\alpha/(2\pi)$ (positive). Deep inelastic scattering: anomalous dimensions determine scaling violations in structure functions, measure $\alpha_s$.
