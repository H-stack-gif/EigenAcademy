# Quantum Field Theory Topic 7.4: Electroweak Theory

The unification of electromagnetic and weak interactions through spontaneous symmetry breaking represents one of the greatest achievements in theoretical physics, earning Glashow, Weinberg, and Salam the 1979 Nobel Prize. The electroweak theory is based on gauge group SU(2)$_L$ × U(1)$_Y$ where the subscript $L$ denotes left-handed fermions and $Y$ is weak hypercharge. A complex scalar doublet $\Phi = (\phi^+, \phi^0)^T$ with hypercharge $Y = 1$ develops a vacuum expectation value $\langle\Phi\rangle = (0, v/\sqrt{2})^T$ where $v = 246$ GeV, spontaneously breaking the symmetry to U(1)$_{\text{EM}}$ and generating masses for the W$^\pm$ and Z$^0$ bosons while keeping the photon massless. Three of the four original gauge bosons—W$_\mu^1$, W$_\mu^2$, W$_\mu^3$ from SU(2)$_L$ and B$_\mu$ from U(1)$_Y$—mix and absorb the three Goldstone bosons, becoming massive with $m_W = gv/2 \approx 80.4$ GeV and $m_Z = (v/2)\sqrt{g^2+g'^2} \approx 91.2$ GeV where $g$ and $g'$ are the SU(2)$_L$ and U(1)$_Y$ coupling constants.

The Weinberg angle $\theta_W$ parametrizes the mixing between neutral gauge bosons through $\tan\theta_W = g'/g$, with experimentally $\cos\theta_W = m_W/m_Z \approx 0.88$. Fermions acquire masses through Yukawa couplings to the Higgs doublet: $\mathcal{L}_Y = -y_e \bar{L}\Phi e_R + \text{h.c.}$ where $L = (\nu_e, e^-)_L$ is the left-handed lepton doublet and $e_R$ is the right-handed electron singlet. After SSB, this generates the electron mass $m_e = y_e v/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.511$ MeV, with the tiny Yukawa coupling $y_e \sim 10^{-6}$ explaining why the electron is so light compared to the electroweak scale. The hierarchy of fermion masses—from neutrinos (sub-eV) to top quark (173 GeV)—arises entirely from the range of Yukawa couplings spanning twelve orders of magnitude, a pattern the Standard Model accommodates but doesn't explain.

The gauge structure begins with the covariant derivative acting on the Higgs doublet:

$$D_\mu\Phi = \left(\partial_\mu + ig\frac{\sigma^a}{2}W_\mu^a + ig'\frac{Y}{2}B_\mu\right)\Phi$$

where $\sigma^a$ are Pauli matrices, $W_\mu^a$ ($a = 1,2,3$) are SU(2)$_L$ gauge fields, and $B_\mu$ is the U(1)$_Y$ field. The Higgs doublet has hypercharge $Y = 1$ chosen so that the upper component has electric charge $Q = T_3 + Y/2 = 1/2 + 1/2 = +1$ and lower component has $Q = -1/2 + 1/2 = 0$. Choosing the VEV $\langle\Phi\rangle = (0, v/\sqrt{2})^T$ breaks SU(2)$_L$ × U(1)$_Y$ but preserves the diagonal U(1) subgroup generated by $Q = T_3 + Y/2$—this is electromagnetism.

The physical gauge bosons arise from linear combinations. Defining the charged fields:

$$W_\mu^\pm = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(W_\mu^1 \mp iW_\mu^2)$$

and the neutral fields through Weinberg mixing:

$$\begin{pmatrix} Z_\mu \\ A_\mu \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta_W & -\sin\theta_W \\ \sin\theta_W & \cos\theta_W \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} W_\mu^3 \\ B_\mu \end{pmatrix}$$

where the photon field $A_\mu$ remains massless (unbroken generator) and $Z_\mu$ becomes massive (broken generator). The electromagnetic coupling emerges as $e = g\sin\theta_W = g'\cos\theta_W$, unifying the two independent gauge couplings through the mixing angle.

### Example 1: Higgs Doublet VEV and Symmetry Breaking

Show that $\langle\Phi\rangle = (0, v/\sqrt{2})^T$ preserves U(1)$_{\text{EM}}$ but breaks SU(2)$_L$ × U(1)$_Y$.

**Solution:**

The electromagnetic charge generator is $Q = T_3 + Y/2$ where $T_3 = \sigma_3/2$ acts on the doublet. For the VEV with $Y(\Phi) = 1$:

$$\langle\Phi\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ v/\sqrt{2} \end{pmatrix}$$

Check if $Q\langle\Phi\rangle = 0$ (invariant vacuum):

$$Q\langle\Phi\rangle = \left(\frac{\sigma_3}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\right)\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ v/\sqrt{2} \end{pmatrix}$$

The matrix acting on the doublet:

$$\left(\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} + \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\right) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

$$Q\langle\Phi\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ v/\sqrt{2} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

So U(1)$_{\text{EM}}$ is preserved! But SU(2)$_L$ generators $T_1, T_2, T_3$ and U(1)$_Y$ are broken:

$$T_1\langle\Phi\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ v/\sqrt{2} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} v/(2\sqrt{2}) \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \neq 0$$

Broken! Similarly for $T_2, T_3$, and $Y$. The pattern SU(2)$_L$ × U(1)$_Y$ → U(1)$_{\text{EM}}$ produces three broken generators → three Goldstones eaten by W$^\pm$, Z.

**Answer:** $Q\langle\Phi\rangle = 0$ (EM preserved), but $T_{1,2,3}\langle\Phi\rangle \neq 0$ and $Y\langle\Phi\rangle \neq 0$ (SU(2)$_L$ × U(1)$_Y$ broken).

The kinetic term $|D_\mu\Phi|^2$ generates gauge boson masses. Expanding around the VEV with $\Phi = (0, (v+h)/\sqrt{2})^T$ in unitary gauge:

$$|D_\mu\Phi|^2 = \left|\left(\partial_\mu + ig\frac{\sigma^a}{2}W_\mu^a + ig'\frac{1}{2}B_\mu\right)\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ (v+h)/\sqrt{2} \end{pmatrix}\right|^2$$

The gauge field part gives:

$$|D_\mu\Phi|^2 \supset \frac{(v+h)^2}{2}\left|g\frac{\sigma^a}{2}W_\mu^a + g'\frac{1}{2}B_\mu\right|^2$$

Computing the matrix product:

$$g\frac{\sigma^a}{2}W_\mu^a\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = g\left[\frac{\sigma^1}{2}W_\mu^1 + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}W_\mu^2 + \frac{\sigma^3}{2}W_\mu^3\right]\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

$$= \frac{g}{2}\begin{pmatrix} W_\mu^1 - iW_\mu^2 \\ -W_\mu^3 \end{pmatrix}$$

Adding the hypercharge term:

$$\left(g\frac{\sigma^a}{2}W_\mu^a + g'\frac{1}{2}B_\mu\right)\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} g(W_\mu^1 - iW_\mu^2) \\ -gW_\mu^3 + g'B_\mu \end{pmatrix}$$

The squared magnitude:

$$\left|\cdots\right|^2 = \frac{1}{4}\left[g^2|W_\mu^1 - iW_\mu^2|^2 + |-gW_\mu^3 + g'B_\mu|^2\right]$$

$$= \frac{g^2}{4}\left[(W_\mu^1)^2 + (W_\mu^2)^2\right] + \frac{1}{4}(g^2 + g'^2)\left[\frac{gW_\mu^3 - g'B_\mu}{\sqrt{g^2+g'^2}}\right]^2$$

Using $W_\mu^\pm = (W_\mu^1 \mp iW_\mu^2)/\sqrt{2}$ and defining $Z_\mu = (gW_\mu^3 - g'B_\mu)/\sqrt{g^2+g'^2}$:

$$|D_\mu\Phi|^2 = \frac{1}{2}(\partial_\mu h)^2 + \frac{g^2v^2}{4}W_\mu^+ W^{-\mu} + \frac{(g^2+g'^2)v^2}{8}Z_\mu Z^\mu + \text{interactions}$$

Reading off masses:

$$m_W^2 = \frac{g^2v^2}{4}, \quad m_Z^2 = \frac{(g^2+g'^2)v^2}{4}$$

The photon $A_\mu = (g'W_\mu^3 + gB_\mu)/\sqrt{g^2+g'^2}$ orthogonal to $Z_\mu$ remains massless.

### Example 2: Gauge Boson Masses and Weinberg Angle

Derive the W and Z masses, express in terms of Weinberg angle, and compute $\rho = m_W^2/(m_Z^2\cos^2\theta_W)$.

**Solution:**

From kinetic term:

$$m_W = \frac{gv}{2}, \quad m_Z = \frac{\sqrt{g^2+g'^2}v}{2}$$

Define Weinberg angle: $\tan\theta_W = g'/g$, so:

$$g = \frac{e}{\sin\theta_W}, \quad g' = \frac{e}{\cos\theta_W}$$

where $e$ is electromagnetic coupling. Then:

$$\sqrt{g^2+g'^2} = e\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sin^2\theta_W} + \frac{1}{\cos^2\theta_W}} = \frac{e}{\sin\theta_W\cos\theta_W}$$

$$m_Z = \frac{ev}{2\sin\theta_W\cos\theta_W} = \frac{m_W}{\cos\theta_W}$$

The mass ratio:

$$\frac{m_W}{m_Z} = \cos\theta_W$$

Experimentally: $m_W = 80.377$ GeV, $m_Z = 91.1876$ GeV, giving $\cos\theta_W = 0.8819$.

The **$\rho$ parameter** measures deviation from minimal Higgs:

$$\rho = \frac{m_W^2}{m_Z^2\cos^2\theta_W}$$

For SU(2) doublet: $\rho = 1$ exactly at tree level. Measured: $\rho = 1.00038 \pm 0.00020$ (loop corrections).

Numerically with $v = 246$ GeV, $\sin^2\theta_W \approx 0.223$:

$$m_W = \frac{gv}{2} \approx \frac{0.653 \times 246}{2} \approx 80.4\text{ GeV}$$

$$m_Z \approx \frac{80.4}{0.882} \approx 91.2\text{ GeV}$$

**Answer:** $m_W = gv/2 \approx 80.4$ GeV, $m_Z = m_W/\cos\theta_W \approx 91.2$ GeV; $\rho = 1$ at tree level (minimal Higgs).

The fermion sector exhibits a remarkable chiral structure. Left-handed fermions transform as SU(2)$_L$ doublets:

$$L_e = \begin{pmatrix} \nu_e \\ e^- \end{pmatrix}_L, \quad Q = \begin{pmatrix} u \\ d \end{pmatrix}_L$$

while right-handed fermions are SU(2)$_L$ singlets: $e_R$, $u_R$, $d_R$. The absence of right-handed neutrinos in the minimal Standard Model explains why neutrinos are massless at tree level (no Yukawa term $\bar{L}\Phi\nu_R$ possible). Neutrino masses require either right-handed neutrinos (seesaw mechanism) or higher-dimensional operators (Weinberg operator), pointing to physics beyond the Standard Model.

### Example 3: Yukawa Couplings and Fermion Masses

Derive electron mass from Yukawa coupling and explain quark mass generation including CKM mixing.

**Solution:**

The lepton Yukawa Lagrangian:

$$\mathcal{L}_Y = -y_e\bar{L}_e\Phi e_R - y_\mu\bar{L}_\mu\Phi\mu_R - y_\tau\bar{L}_\tau\Phi\tau_R + \text{h.c.}$$

where $L_e = (\nu_e, e^-)_L$. After SSB with $\Phi = (0, (v+h)/\sqrt{2})^T$:

$$\mathcal{L}_Y \supset -y_e\bar{e}_L\frac{v+h}{\sqrt{2}}e_R + \text{h.c.} = -\frac{y_e v}{\sqrt{2}}(\bar{e}_L e_R + \bar{e}_R e_L) - \frac{y_e}{\sqrt{2}}h\bar{e}e$$

The mass term $m_e\bar{e}e$ gives:

$$m_e = \frac{y_e v}{\sqrt{2}}$$

For electron: $m_e = 0.511$ MeV, $v = 246$ GeV → $y_e = \sqrt{2}m_e/v \approx 2.9 \times 10^{-6}$.

The Higgs-fermion coupling: $g_{hee} = -m_e/v$ (proportional to mass!).

For quarks, flavor mixing occurs. The Yukawa matrix for down-type quarks:

$$\mathcal{L}_Y^d = -Y_{ij}^d\bar{Q}_{L,i}\Phi d_{R,j}$$

where $i,j$ run over generations. After SSB, $Y^d$ is a general $3\times 3$ complex matrix, diagonalized by:

$$U_L Y^d U_R^\dagger = \text{diag}(y_d, y_s, y_b)$$

The mass eigenstates differ from gauge eigenstates. For up-type quarks using $\tilde{\Phi} = i\sigma_2\Phi^*$:

$$\mathcal{L}_Y^u = -Y_{ij}^u\bar{Q}_{L,i}\tilde{\Phi}u_{R,j}$$

Diagonalizing both $Y^u$ and $Y^d$ gives different rotations on $Q_L = (u,d)_L$. The charged current:

$$W_\mu^+ \bar{u}_L\gamma^\mu d_L \to W_\mu^+ \bar{u}_L\gamma^\mu V_{\text{CKM}} d_L$$

where the **CKM matrix** $V_{\text{CKM}} = U_L^{u\dagger}U_L^d$ mixes quark flavors, enabling CP violation.

**Answer:** $m_e = y_e v/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.511$ MeV with $y_e \sim 10^{-6}$; quark masses from diagonalizing Yukawa matrices, CKM mixing from non-commuting $U^u$, $U^d$.

The neutral current interactions couple to $Z_\mu$ with characteristic structure. For a fermion $f$ with charge $Q_f$ and third component of weak isospin $T_3^f$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{NC} = \frac{g}{\cos\theta_W}Z_\mu\bar{f}\gamma^\mu\left[T_3^f - Q_f\sin^2\theta_W - T_3^f\gamma_5\right]f$$

The **weak neutral vector and axial couplings** are:

$$g_V^f = T_3^f - 2Q_f\sin^2\theta_W, \quad g_A^f = T_3^f$$

For electron: $T_3^{e_L} = -1/2$, $Q_e = -1$:

$$g_V^e = -\frac{1}{2} + 2\sin^2\theta_W \approx -\frac{1}{2} + 0.446 = -0.04$$

$$g_A^e = -\frac{1}{2}$$

The smallness of $g_V^e$ suppresses parity-conserving $Z$ couplings to electrons, while $g_A^e$ drives parity violation observed in atomic physics.

### Example 4: Neutral Current Couplings

Calculate the Z boson coupling to quarks and explain the $Z \to b\bar{b}$ branching ratio.

**Solution:**

For down-type quarks ($d, s, b$): $T_3^{d_L} = -1/2$, $Q_d = -1/3$:

$$g_V^d = -\frac{1}{2} + \frac{2}{3}\sin^2\theta_W \approx -0.35$$

$$g_A^d = -\frac{1}{2}$$

For up-type quarks ($u,c,t$): $T_3^{u_L} = +1/2$, $Q_u = +2/3$:

$$g_V^u = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{4}{3}\sin^2\theta_W \approx 0.20$$

$$g_A^u = \frac{1}{2}$$

The partial width:

$$\Gamma(Z \to f\bar{f}) = \frac{G_F m_Z^3}{6\pi\sqrt{2}}N_c[(g_V^f)^2 + (g_A^f)^2]$$

where $N_c = 3$ for quarks, $1$ for leptons. For $b$ quarks:

$$\Gamma(Z \to b\bar{b}) \propto 3[(0.35)^2 + (0.5)^2] = 3 \times 0.3725 = 1.12$$

For leptons (e.g., $e^+e^-$):

$$\Gamma(Z \to e^+e^-) \propto 1[(0.04)^2 + (0.5)^2] = 0.252$$

The ratio:

$$\frac{\Gamma(Z \to b\bar{b})}{\Gamma(Z \to e^+e^-)} \approx \frac{1.12}{0.252} \approx 4.4$$

Including QCD corrections $\sim 1 + \alpha_s/\pi$, the branching fraction:

$$\text{BR}(Z \to b\bar{b}) \approx 15\%$$

Measured: $15.12 \pm 0.05\%$ (excellent agreement!).

**Answer:** $g_V^d \approx -0.35$, $g_A^d = -0.5$ for down quarks; $Z \to b\bar{b}$ enhanced by color factor $N_c = 3$, BR $\approx 15\%$.

The custodial SU(2) symmetry protects the relation $\rho = 1$ from large radiative corrections. Even though the Higgs doublet breaks SU(2)$_L$ × U(1)$_Y$, it preserves an accidental global SU(2)$_{\text{custodial}}$ symmetry that relates W and Z masses. This explains why precision electroweak measurements find $\rho$ so close to unity despite potentially large loop corrections. New physics respecting custodial symmetry (e.g., composite Higgs models) preserves this relation, while custodial-breaking sectors (Higgs triplets, large $Y$ representations) would push $\rho$ away from 1.

The electroweak scale $v = 246$ GeV sets all masses through dimensionless couplings. For the Higgs: $m_h^2 = 2\lambda v^2$ with $\lambda \approx 0.13$ giving $m_h = 125$ GeV. For W/Z: masses set by gauge couplings $g, g'$. For fermions: Yukawa couplings span $10^{-12}$ (neutrinos if massive) to $\sim 1$ (top quark), an unexplained hierarchy. The top Yukawa $y_t \approx 1$ means the top quark mass $m_t = y_t v/\sqrt{2} \approx 173$ GeV is entirely natural—it's the light fermions requiring explanation.

The gauge coupling unification provides tantalizing hints of grand unification. In the minimal SM, the three gauge couplings $g_1$ (U(1)$_Y$), $g_2$ (SU(2)$_L$), $g_3$ (SU(3)$_C$) run with different beta functions and nearly meet around $10^{16}$ GeV—close but not quite. In supersymmetric extensions, the running is modified by additional particles (squarks, sleptons, gauginos), and the couplings unify precisely at the GUT scale $M_{\text{GUT}} \sim 2 \times 10^{16}$ GeV. This unification, combined with proton decay predictions and neutrino mass generation through the seesaw mechanism, suggests the SM is a low-energy effective theory of a more fundamental unified theory.

### Example 5: Higgs Decay to W Bosons

Calculate the partial width $\Gamma(h \to WW^*)$ for Higgs decay to off-shell W pairs.

**Solution:**

The Higgs-W coupling from $|D_\mu\Phi|^2$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{hWW} = g_{hWW}hW_\mu^+ W^{-\mu} + \frac{1}{2}g_{hhWW}h^2 W_\mu^+ W^{-\mu}$$

where $g_{hWW} = g^2v/2 = 2m_W^2/v$ and $g_{hhWW} = g^2/2 = 2m_W^2/v^2$.

For $m_h = 125$ GeV $< 2m_W = 160$ GeV, decay is to one on-shell and one off-shell W: $h \to WW^*$.

The partial width (approximate):

$$\Gamma(h \to WW^*) \approx \frac{G_F m_h^3}{16\pi\sqrt{2}}\left(1 - \frac{4m_W^2}{m_h^2}\right)^{1/2}g(x)$$

where $x = m_W^2/m_h^2$ and $g(x)$ is a phase space function. For $m_h = 125$ GeV, since $m_h < 2m_W$, use the off-shell formula:

$$\Gamma(h \to WW^*) \approx \frac{3G_F m_h^3}{16\pi\sqrt{2}} \approx \frac{3 \times 1.166 \times 10^{-5}\text{ GeV}^{-2} \times (125\text{ GeV})^3}{16\pi\sqrt{2}}$$

$$\approx \frac{3 \times 1.166 \times 10^{-5} \times 1.95 \times 10^6}{71} \approx 0.97\text{ MeV}$$

With proper phase space integration, the accurate result is $\Gamma(h \to WW^*) \approx 0.88$ MeV, giving branching ratio:

$$\text{BR}(h \to WW^*) = \frac{0.88\text{ MeV}}{4.1\text{ MeV}} \approx 21\%$$

This is the second-largest Higgs decay mode after $b\bar{b}$, crucial for discovery and measurement.

**Answer:** $\Gamma(h \to WW^*) \approx 0.88$ MeV for $m_h = 125$ GeV; BR $\approx 21\%$, major discovery channel.

The unification of electromagnetic and weak forces becomes manifest in the neutral current sector. The Z boson mediates weak neutral currents with strength comparable to charged currents, while the photon mediates electromagnetic interactions with the same coupling constant $e$ appearing in QED. The Weinberg angle relates these through $e = g\sin\theta_W$, with $\sin^2\theta_W \approx 0.223$ measured precisely from $Z$ pole observables at LEP.

The discovery of W and Z bosons at CERN in 1983 by UA1 and UA2 collaborations confirmed the electroweak theory's predictions. The measured masses agreed with theoretical expectations from precision low-energy measurements (muon decay, neutral currents), and subsequent precision measurements at LEP and SLC validated the theory to extraordinary accuracy—deviations at the sub-percent level testing loop corrections and constraining new physics.

### Example 6: Muon Decay and Fermi Constant

Relate the Fermi constant $G_F$ to electroweak parameters and compute $g$ from muon lifetime.

**Solution:**

Muon decay $\mu^- \to e^-\bar{\nu}_e\nu_\mu$ proceeds via W exchange. At low energies $q^2 \ll m_W^2$, the W propagator gives effective four-fermion interaction:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}} = \frac{g^2}{8m_W^2}(\bar{\nu}_\mu\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma_5)\mu)(\bar{e}\gamma_\mu(1-\gamma_5)\nu_e)$$

Comparing to Fermi theory $\mathcal{L}_F = \frac{G_F}{\sqrt{2}}(\bar{\nu}_\mu\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma_5)\mu)(\bar{e}\gamma_\mu(1-\gamma_5)\nu_e)$:

$$\frac{G_F}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{g^2}{8m_W^2}$$

Using $m_W = gv/2$:

$$G_F = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}v^2}$$

Measured from muon lifetime: $G_F = 1.1663787 \times 10^{-5}$ GeV$^{-2}$, giving:

$$v = \left(\sqrt{2}G_F\right)^{-1/2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1.166 \times 10^{-5}\text{ GeV}^{-2} \times \sqrt{2}}} \approx 246\text{ GeV}$$

Then from $m_W = 80.4$ GeV:

$$g = \frac{2m_W}{v} = \frac{2 \times 80.4}{246} \approx 0.653$$

And $\sin^2\theta_W = 1 - m_W^2/m_Z^2 \approx 0.223$, so:

$$e = g\sin\theta_W \approx 0.653 \times 0.472 \approx 0.308$$

consistent with $e^2/(4\pi) = \alpha \approx 1/137$ giving $e \approx 0.303$.

**Answer:** $G_F = 1/(\sqrt{2}v^2)$ relates to EW scale $v = 246$ GeV; muon lifetime determines $g \approx 0.65$.

The Higgs discovery in 2012 completed the Standard Model, confirming the electroweak symmetry breaking mechanism proposed in the 1960s. The measured mass $m_h = 125.09 \pm 0.24$ GeV, combined with top mass $m_t = 172.76 \pm 0.30$ GeV, places the Standard Model in a metastable vacuum regime—stable for cosmological timescales but potentially unstable at very high energies. This "living on the edge" might be coincidental or could hint at new physics stabilizing the hierarchy between electroweak and Planck scales.

### Example 7: Custodial Symmetry and $\rho$ Parameter

Explain custodial SU(2) symmetry and compute one-loop correction to $\rho$ from top-bottom mass splitting.

**Solution:**

The Higgs doublet $\Phi = (\phi^+, \phi^0)$ can be reorganized as a bidoublet under SU(2)$_L$ × SU(2)$_R$ custodial:

$$\Phi \to (2,2)_{\text{custodial}}$$

After SSB, the vacuum preserves SU(2)$_{\text{custodial}}$ (diagonal subgroup), relating W and Z:

$$m_W^2 = m_Z^2\cos^2\theta_W \quad \Rightarrow \quad \rho = \frac{m_W^2}{m_Z^2\cos^2\theta_W} = 1$$

Yukawa couplings break custodial if $m_t \neq m_b$ (SU(2)$_L$ doublet split). The one-loop correction:

$$\Delta\rho = \frac{3G_F}{8\pi^2\sqrt{2}}(m_t^2 - m_b^2)$$

For $m_t = 173$ GeV, $m_b = 4.2$ GeV:

$$\Delta\rho \approx \frac{3 \times 1.166 \times 10^{-5}}{8\pi^2\sqrt{2}}(173^2 - 4.2^2)\text{ GeV}^2$$

$$\approx \frac{3 \times 1.166 \times 10^{-5}}{111}(29929 - 18) \approx 0.0094$$

So $\rho = 1.0094$ at one loop from top-bottom splitting. Measured: $\rho = 1.00038 \pm 0.00020$, consistent including higher corrections.

The large top mass drives the main correction, showing why precision electroweak measurements were sensitive to top mass before direct discovery. The constraint $\Delta\rho \sim G_F(m_t^2 - m_b^2)$ from LEP data predicted $m_t \sim 170$ GeV before top's 1995 discovery at Tevatron.

**Answer:** Custodial SU(2) gives $\rho = 1$ at tree level; one-loop $\Delta\rho \approx G_F(m_t^2-m_b^2)/(8\pi^2\sqrt{2}) \approx 0.009$ from top-bottom splitting.

The electroweak theory's predictive power extends beyond tree-level masses to loop-induced processes, precision observables, and constraints on new physics. Modern measurements at the LHC test the Higgs couplings to fermions and bosons, probing the proportionality $g_{hff} \propto m_f$ and searching for deviations that could signal composite Higgs, supersymmetry, or extra dimensions. The agreement between theory and experiment across energies from MeV (muon decay) to TeV (LHC) validates the SU(2)$_L$ × U(1)$_Y$ structure and spontaneous symmetry breaking as the correct description of electroweak unification.

**Problem 3: B** — From $|D_\mu\Phi|^2 \supset (g^2v^2/4)W_\mu^+ W^{-\mu}$, mass $m_W = gv/2 \approx 80.377$ GeV. SU(2)$_L$ coupling $g \approx 0.653$. Relation to Fermi constant: $G_F = 1/(\sqrt{2}v^2)$. Discovered 1983 at CERN, mass confirmed electroweak predictions.

**Problem 4: B** — Weinberg angle: $\cos\theta_W = m_W/m_Z = g/\sqrt{g^2+g'^2} \approx 0.8819$. Or $\sin^2\theta_W \approx 0.223$. Mixing angle for neutral bosons: $Z = \cos\theta_W W^3 - \sin\theta_W B$, $A = \sin\theta_W W^3 + \cos\theta_W B$. EM coupling $e = g\sin\theta_W = g'\cos\theta_W$.
