# Quantum Field Theory Topic 7.6: Precision Electroweak Tests

Quantum loop corrections to electroweak observables provide extraordinary sensitivity to the Standard Model's consistency and new physics contributions through virtual effects. The **S, T, U parameters** (oblique corrections) quantify deviations from SM predictions in gauge boson self-energies, with $S$ measuring weak isospin violation, $T$ encoding custodial symmetry breaking, and $U$ representing W-photon mixing. Precision measurements at the **Z pole** at LEP (Large Electron-Positron collider) determined the Z mass $m_Z = 91.1876 \pm 0.0021$ GeV, total width $\Gamma_Z = 2.4952 \pm 0.0023$ GeV, hadronic peak cross section, and asymmetries like the forward-backward asymmetry $A_{FB}^{0,b}$ for bottom quarks, all agreeing with SM predictions at per-mille precision. The W boson mass measurement $m_W = 80.379 \pm 0.012$ GeV from Tevatron and LHC combines with Z mass to extract $\sin^2\theta_W$, while the top quark mass $m_t \approx 172.76$ GeV enters precision fits through large loop corrections proportional to $m_t^2$.

Radiative corrections encoded in $\Delta r$ connect the Fermi constant $G_F$ from muon decay to gauge couplings and boson masses. At tree level, $m_W^2 = (\pi\alpha)/(\sqrt{2}G_F\sin^2\theta_W)$, but one-loop corrections modify this to $m_W^2(1 + \Delta r) = \pi\alpha/(\sqrt{2}G_F\sin^2\theta_W)$ where $\Delta r$ depends logarithmically on $m_h$ and quadratically on $m_t$, providing indirect constraints. Before the Higgs discovery, global electroweak fits predicted $m_h = 94^{+29}_{-24}$ GeV from precision data alone—remarkably close to the observed 125 GeV. This triumph demonstrates how virtual Higgs and top loops affect low-energy observables, and how precision measurements constrain new physics through similar virtual contributions from heavy particles beyond the Standard Model.

The precision program tests quantum field theory at the loop level with exquisite accuracy. Measuring the Z mass to 2 parts per million, the W mass to 15 parts per million, and various asymmetries and branching ratios to sub-percent precision has verified the electroweak theory including one-loop and leading two-loop corrections. Any deviation from SM predictions—even at the permille level—could signal new physics such as supersymmetric particles in loops, extra dimensions modifying Kaluza-Klein sum rules, or composite Higgs structure affecting coupling constants. The current agreement between theory and experiment across all observables severely constrains beyond-SM scenarios, requiring new particles to be either very heavy ($\gg$ TeV) or to have precisely arranged couplings that preserve the SM predictions.

### Example 1: Z Boson Mass and Width Measurement

Explain how LEP measured $m_Z$ and $\Gamma_Z$ at the Z pole and extract the number of light neutrino species.

**Solution:**

LEP operated at $\sqrt{s} \approx m_Z$, scanning the Z resonance. The cross section for $e^+e^- \to Z \to f\bar{f}$:

$$\sigma(s) = \frac{12\pi}{m_Z^2}\frac{\Gamma_{ee}\Gamma_{ff}}{(s-m_Z^2)^2 + m_Z^2\Gamma_Z^2}$$

Near resonance $s \approx m_Z^2$, this becomes a Breit-Wigner:

$$\sigma_{\text{peak}} = \frac{12\pi\Gamma_{ee}\Gamma_{ff}}{m_Z^2\Gamma_Z^2}$$

Measuring the peak position gives $m_Z$, the width gives $\Gamma_Z$, and the height gives $\Gamma_{ee}\Gamma_{ff}/\Gamma_Z^2$.

LEP results:
- $m_Z = 91.1876 \pm 0.0021$ GeV
- $\Gamma_Z = 2.4952 \pm 0.0023$ GeV
- $\Gamma_{had} = 1.7444 \pm 0.0020$ GeV
- $\Gamma_{\ell\ell} = 83.984 \pm 0.086$ MeV (per lepton species)

The total width decomposes:

$$\Gamma_Z = \Gamma_{had} + 3\Gamma_{\ell\ell} + N_\nu\Gamma_{\nu\nu}$$

where $N_\nu$ counts light neutrinos ($m_\nu < m_Z/2$). The invisible width:

$$\Gamma_{\text{inv}} = \Gamma_Z - \Gamma_{had} - 3\Gamma_{\ell\ell}$$

SM predicts $\Gamma_{\nu\nu} = 167.2$ MeV per neutrino species. Measuring:

$$\Gamma_{\text{inv}} = 499.0 \pm 1.5\text{ MeV}$$

Extracting:

$$N_\nu = \frac{\Gamma_{\text{inv}}}{\Gamma_{\nu\nu}} = \frac{499.0}{167.2} = 2.984 \pm 0.008$$

Precisely three light neutrino species! This excludes fourth-generation neutrinos lighter than $m_Z/2$ and constrains exotic scenarios.

**Answer:** LEP Z-pole scan gives $m_Z = 91.1876$ GeV, $\Gamma_Z = 2.4952$ GeV; invisible width determines $N_\nu = 2.984 \pm 0.008$ (exactly 3 light neutrinos).

The S, T, U parameters provide a model-independent framework for characterizing new physics contributions to gauge boson propagators. Defined through vacuum polarization:

$$\Pi_{WW}(q^2) - \Pi_{WW}(0) \equiv \frac{\alpha T}{4\sin^2\theta_W}q^2 + \cdots$$

$$\frac{\Pi_{ZZ}(0) - \Pi_{\gamma\gamma}(0)}{m_Z^2} \equiv \frac{\alpha S}{4\sin^2\theta_W}$$

$$\frac{\Pi_{WW}(0)}{m_W^2} - \frac{\Pi_{ZZ}(0)}{m_Z^2} \equiv -\frac{\alpha U}{4\sin^2\theta_W}$$

The T parameter measures custodial SU(2) breaking, vanishing for doublets at tree level. Heavy new physics with mass $M$ contributes roughly $T \sim N/(16\pi^2)(m_t^2/m_W^2)$ where $N$ counts new states, allowing constraints $M \gtrsim$ few hundred GeV from precision data.

### Example 2: S, T, U Parameters and New Physics Constraints

Calculate the contribution to the T parameter from a heavy fourth-generation quark doublet.

**Solution:**

A fourth-generation quark doublet $(t', b')$ with large mass splitting contributes to T through custodial SU(2) violation:

$$\Delta\rho = \frac{G_F}{8\pi^2\sqrt{2}}(m_{t'}^2 - m_{b'}^2)$$

The T parameter relates:

$$T = \frac{\Delta\rho}{\alpha(m_Z)}$$

For $m_{t'} = 600$ GeV, $m_{b'} = 500$ GeV:

$$\Delta\rho = \frac{1.166 \times 10^{-5}}{8\pi^2\sqrt{2}}[(600)^2 - (500)^2]\text{ GeV}^2$$

$$= \frac{1.166 \times 10^{-5}}{111}(110000) \approx 0.116$$

$$T = \frac{0.116}{1/128} = 14.8$$

Experimental constraint from global fits: $T = 0.03 \pm 0.12$ (SM reference point). This fourth generation contribution $T \sim 15$ is excluded at $\sim 100\sigma$!

The constraint arises because measured $m_W/m_Z$ ratio is very close to SM prediction. Heavy chiral fermions in doublets would shift this ratio through $T$ parameter, contradicting precision data. This is why sequential fourth generations are ruled out—they'd produce too large corrections to electroweak observables.

Conversely, for new physics respecting custodial symmetry (fermions in vector-like representations, singlets, or degenerate doublets), the T parameter contribution vanishes, allowing heavier masses before precision constraints bind.

**Answer:** Heavy fourth generation with $m_{t'} - m_{b'} \sim 100$ GeV gives $T \sim 15$; excluded by precision data requiring $|T| < 0.3$, ruling out sequential fourth generations.

The forward-backward asymmetry for fermions at the Z pole tests the vector and axial-vector couplings. Defining the asymmetry:

$$A_{FB}^f = \frac{\sigma_F - \sigma_B}{\sigma_F + \sigma_B}$$

where $\sigma_F$ is the cross section for forward scattering ($\cos\theta > 0$) and $\sigma_B$ backward, the SM predicts:

$$A_{FB}^{0,f} = \frac{3}{4}A_e A_f$$

where the fermion asymmetry parameter $A_f = 2g_V^f g_A^f/[(g_V^f)^2 + (g_A^f)^2]$ depends on couplings. For the bottom quark, $A_b \approx 0.935$ (large due to $T_3 = -1/2$), making $A_{FB}^{0,b}$ very sensitive to $\sin^2\theta_W$.

### Example 3: Forward-Backward Asymmetry and Weak Mixing Angle

Calculate the bottom quark forward-backward asymmetry and extract $\sin^2\theta_W^{\text{eff}}$.

**Solution:**

The Z couplings to bottom quarks:

$$g_V^b = T_3^b - 2Q_b\sin^2\theta_W = -\frac{1}{2} + \frac{2}{3}\sin^2\theta_W$$

$$g_A^b = T_3^b = -\frac{1}{2}$$

For $\sin^2\theta_W^{\text{eff}} = 0.23153$ (LEP average including radiative corrections):

$$g_V^b = -0.5 + 0.667 \times 0.23153 = -0.345$$

$$A_b = \frac{2g_V^b g_A^b}{(g_V^b)^2 + (g_A^b)^2} = \frac{2 \times (-0.345) \times (-0.5)}{(0.345)^2 + (0.5)^2}$$

$$= \frac{0.345}{0.119 + 0.25} = \frac{0.345}{0.369} = 0.935$$

For electrons (unpolarized):

$$A_e = \frac{2g_V^e g_A^e}{(g_V^e)^2 + (g_A^e)^2}$$

With $g_V^e \approx -0.037$ (small!), $g_A^e = -0.5$:

$$A_e \approx \frac{2 \times 0.037 \times 0.5}{0.0014 + 0.25} \approx 0.147$$

The forward-backward asymmetry:

$$A_{FB}^{0,b} = \frac{3}{4}A_e A_b = \frac{3}{4} \times 0.147 \times 0.935 = 0.103$$

LEP measured: $A_{FB}^{0,b} = 0.0992 \pm 0.0016$

Excellent agreement! Inverting the relation determines $\sin^2\theta_W^{\text{eff}}$ to high precision. Different observables (asymmetries, branching ratios) provide independent determinations, and global fits combine them.

**Answer:** $A_{FB}^{0,b} = \frac{3}{4}A_e A_b \approx 0.103$ for $\sin^2\theta_W = 0.2315$; measured $0.0992 \pm 0.0016$ determines weak mixing angle precisely.

The top quark mass enters electroweak precision through loop corrections, particularly in $\Delta\rho$ which goes as $m_t^2$. Before the top's direct discovery in 1995, precision data predicted $m_t = 177 \pm 11$ GeV from fits to Z-pole observables combined with W mass measurements. The actual measured value $m_t = 172.76 \pm 0.30$ GeV confirmed this prediction spectacularly, demonstrating how virtual top loops affect low-energy physics.

### Example 4: Top and Higgs Loop Corrections to $\Delta r$

Derive the leading $m_t^2$ and $\log m_h$ dependence of radiative corrections $\Delta r$.

**Solution:**

The quantity $\Delta r$ parametrizes radiative corrections in the relation:

$$m_W^2\left(1 - \frac{m_W^2}{m_Z^2}\right) = \frac{\pi\alpha(m_Z)}{\sqrt{2}G_F}(1 + \Delta r)$$

At one loop, the dominant contributions:

**Top quark** (custodial breaking from $m_t^2 - m_b^2$):

$$\Delta r_{\text{top}} \approx -\frac{3G_F m_t^2}{8\pi^2\sqrt{2}} = -\frac{3 \times 1.166 \times 10^{-5} \times (173)^2}{8\pi^2\sqrt{2}}$$

$$\approx -\frac{3 \times 1.166 \times 10^{-5} \times 29929}{111} \approx -0.0094$$

The negative sign means top loops reduce $m_W$ (or equivalently shift the predicted relation).

**Higgs boson** (logarithmic):

$$\Delta r_{\text{Higgs}} \approx \frac{G_F m_W^2}{8\pi^2\sqrt{2}}\left[\frac{11}{3}\ln\frac{m_h}{m_W} - \frac{5}{6}\right]$$

For $m_h = 125$ GeV:

$$\Delta r_{\text{Higgs}} \approx \frac{1.166 \times 10^{-5} \times (80.4)^2}{111}\left[\frac{11}{3}\ln\frac{125}{80.4} - \frac{5}{6}\right]$$

$$\approx 0.068 \times [3.67 \times 0.442 - 0.833] \approx 0.068 \times 0.79 \approx 0.0054$$

The Higgs contribution is logarithmic (much weaker than quadratic top dependence), explaining why precision fits could constrain $m_h$ only to factor-of-two precision while $m_t$ was predicted to $\sim 6\%$ accuracy.

Including other contributions (gauge boson loops, QCD corrections), the full $\Delta r \approx 0.03$, which is a few-percent correction but measurable at LEP precision.

**Answer:** Leading corrections: $\Delta r_{\text{top}} \sim -G_F m_t^2/(8\pi^2\sqrt{2}) \approx -0.009$ (quadratic), $\Delta r_{\text{Higgs}} \sim G_F m_W^2 \ln(m_h/m_W)/(8\pi^2\sqrt{2}) \approx +0.005$ (logarithmic).

Global fits combine all precision observables—Z-pole measurements (mass, width, asymmetries, branching ratios), W mass, top mass, low-energy neutral current data (atomic parity violation, neutrino scattering)—to constrain SM parameters and probe new physics. The fits extract fundamental parameters like $\alpha_s(m_Z)$, $m_t$, $m_h$ (before discovery) and test consistency. Before 2012, fits predicted $m_h = 94^{+29}_{-24}$ GeV (68% CL), with upper bound $m_h < 152$ GeV (95% CL). The discovered mass $m_h = 125$ GeV sits comfortably within this range, validating the precision program.

The atomic parity violation experiments complement collider measurements by probing electroweak interactions at very low energies. The weak charge of nuclei $Q_W$ depends on Z couplings through neutral current interactions with electrons. Precise measurements in cesium-133 give $Q_W^{\text{Cs}} = -73.16 \pm 0.29$, agreeing with SM prediction including radiative corrections. These low-energy tests provide independent determination of $\sin^2\theta_W$ and constrain new physics that might couple differently at atomic versus collider energy scales.

### Example 5: Higgs Mass Prediction from Precision Data

Explain how Higgs mass was constrained before discovery through loop corrections to $m_W$.

**Solution:**

The W mass depends on Higgs mass through loop corrections:

$$m_W^2 = \frac{\pi\alpha}{\sqrt{2}G_F\sin^2\theta_W}\frac{1}{1 + \Delta r(m_h, m_t)}$$

where $\Delta r$ depends logarithmically on $m_h$. Isolating the Higgs dependence:

$$\frac{\partial m_W}{\partial \ln m_h} \approx -\frac{m_W}{2}\frac{\partial \Delta r}{\partial \ln m_h} \approx -\frac{m_W G_F m_W^2}{16\pi^2\sqrt{2}}\frac{11}{3}$$

$$\approx -\frac{80.4 \times 1.166 \times 10^{-5} \times (80.4)^2 \times 11}{16\pi^2\sqrt{2} \times 3}$$

$$\approx -\frac{6600}{670} \approx -10\text{ MeV per unit }\ln m_h$$

Measuring $m_W$ to $\pm 15$ MeV precision constrains $\ln(m_h/100\text{ GeV})$ to $\sim 1.5$, giving:

$$100\text{ GeV} \times e^{-1.5} < m_h < 100\text{ GeV} \times e^{+1.5}$$

$$22\text{ GeV} < m_h < 450\text{ GeV}$$

Combined with direct search exclusions (LEP: $m_h > 114$ GeV; Tevatron: $m_h \notin [147, 180]$ GeV), the window narrowed. Global fits including all observables (not just $m_W$) gave central value $m_h \approx 94$ GeV with allowed range roughly $60$-$150$ GeV.

When LHC discovered the Higgs at $125$ GeV, this confirmed the precision data consistency—the virtual Higgs loops had been detected indirectly through their effects on $m_W$, $A_{FB}$, etc.

**Answer:** Higgs mass constrained by loop corrections $\Delta r \sim \ln m_h$; precision $m_W$ measurement gave $m_h = 94^{+29}_{-24}$ GeV (68% CL), confirmed by discovery at $125$ GeV.

New physics contributions to precision observables are constrained severely. Heavy particles at mass $M$ contribute to oblique parameters roughly as:

$$\Delta S \sim \frac{g^2}{16\pi^2}\ln\frac{M^2}{m_Z^2}, \quad \Delta T \sim \frac{g^2}{16\pi^2}\frac{M^2}{m_Z^2}$$

Current constraints $|S|, |T| < 0.1$-$0.2$ imply $M \gtrsim$ few hundred GeV for generic new physics. Specific models face tighter constraints: composite Higgs (technicolor) overproduces S; extra dimensions with KK modes contribute to T; Z' bosons mix with SM Z affecting couplings. The agreement between precision data and SM predictions is so good that it severely limits the allowed parameter space for beyond-SM scenarios.

The running of electroweak parameters provides additional tests. The electromagnetic coupling runs from $\alpha(0) = 1/137$ to $\alpha(m_Z) = 1/128$ due to fermion loops, while $\sin^2\theta_W$ runs from $\sin^2\theta_W^{\overline{\text{MS}}}(m_Z) \approx 0.2312$ at the Z pole to different values at other scales. Consistency between running determined from QED/QCD and the electroweak structure tests the gauge theory framework. Grand unification scenarios predict the three gauge couplings $g_1, g_2, g_3$ meet at $M_{\text{GUT}} \sim 10^{16}$ GeV in SUSY extensions, providing a striking prediction testable through precision measurements combined with RG evolution.

### Example 6: Constraints on Extra Dimensions from Precision Data

Estimate how Kaluza-Klein excitations in extra dimensions contribute to T parameter.

**Solution:**

In models with one extra dimension of radius $R$, each SM particle has a tower of KK modes with masses:

$$M_n = \sqrt{m_0^2 + \frac{n^2}{R^2}}$$

For compactification scale $M_{\text{KK}} = 1/R$, the lightest KK modes appear at mass $\sim M_{\text{KK}}$. These contribute to precision observables through loops.

The T parameter receives contributions from KK fermions. For a single generation:

$$\Delta T \approx \frac{G_F}{8\pi^2\sqrt{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\text{max}}}(m_{t,n}^2 - m_{b,n}^2)$$

The sum over KK tower grows as:

$$\Delta T \sim \frac{G_F M_{\text{KK}}^2}{8\pi^2\sqrt{2}}\times N_{\text{KK}}$$

where $N_{\text{KK}}$ is the number of KK modes below cutoff (typically $N_{\text{KK}} \sim 10$-$100$ before UV completion).

For $M_{\text{KK}} = 500$ GeV, $N_{\text{KK}} = 10$:

$$\Delta T \sim \frac{1.166 \times 10^{-5} \times (500)^2 \times 10}{111} \approx 0.26$$

Experimental constraint $|T| < 0.15$ (95% CL) requires:

$$M_{\text{KK}} > 1\text{ TeV}$$

for generic extra dimensions. Models with custodial symmetry in the bulk (warped extra dimensions like Randall-Sundrum) can evade this constraint by protecting the T parameter, allowing $M_{\text{KK}} \sim 3$-$5$ TeV.

Direct LHC searches for KK gluons/gravitons also constrain $M_{\text{KK}} \gtrsim 2$-$4$ TeV depending on model, so precision and collider constraints are now comparable.

**Answer:** KK modes contribute $\Delta T \sim G_F M_{\text{KK}}^2 N_{\text{KK}}/(8\pi^2\sqrt{2})$; precision data requires $M_{\text{KK}} \gtrsim 1$ TeV for generic models.

The W mass measurement has recently attracted attention due to a tension between CDF's 2022 measurement $m_W = 80.433 \pm 0.009$ GeV and the SM prediction $m_W^{\text{SM}} = 80.357 \pm 0.006$ GeV, differing by $7\sigma$. If confirmed, this could indicate new physics such as additional Higgs doublets, supersymmetric particles, leptoquarks, or Z' bosons contributing to $\Delta r$. However, other W mass measurements from ATLAS, D0, and LEP are consistent with SM, creating an unresolved puzzle. Future measurements at LHC and proposed lepton colliders aim to resolve this discrepancy with sub-10 MeV precision.

### Example 7: W Mass Anomaly and New Physics Interpretation

Discuss how a shift in W mass from SM prediction could arise from new physics in loops.

**Solution:**

The W mass relation:

$$m_W^2 = \frac{\pi\alpha}{\sqrt{2}G_F\sin^2\theta_W}(1 + \Delta r)$$

where $\Delta r$ includes all loop corrections. New physics at scale $\Lambda_{\text{NP}}$ modifies:

$$\Delta r_{\text{NP}} \approx c \frac{v^2}{\Lambda_{\text{NP}}^2}$$

with coefficient $c \sim \mathcal{O}(1)$ depending on model. The W mass shift:

$$\delta m_W \approx -\frac{m_W}{2}\Delta r_{\text{NP}} \approx -40\text{ GeV} \times c \frac{(246\text{ GeV})^2}{\Lambda_{\text{NP}}^2}$$

CDF's shift $\delta m_W \approx +76$ MeV from SM central value would require:

$$\frac{(246)^2}{\Lambda_{\text{NP}}^2} \approx \frac{0.076}{40c} \approx \frac{0.002}{c}$$

$$\Lambda_{\text{NP}} \approx \frac{246}{\sqrt{0.002/c}} \approx 5500\sqrt{c}\text{ GeV}$$

For $c \sim 1$, this gives $\Lambda_{\text{NP}} \sim 5$-$10$ TeV, accessible to HL-LHC indirectly.

Specific models:
- **SUSY**: Chargino/neutralino loops contribute $\Delta r \sim \alpha M_{\text{SUSY}}^2/(4\pi m_W^2) \sin^2 2\beta$. For $\tan\beta \sim 10$, $M_{\text{SUSY}} \sim 200$ GeV could explain the shift.
- **Two Higgs doublets**: Additional scalars modify $\Delta r$ through charged Higgs loops
- **Leptoquarks**: Contribute if they couple to leptons and quarks differently

However, the tension with ATLAS ($m_W = 80.370 \pm 0.019$ GeV) and LEP makes interpretation uncertain. More measurements needed!

**Answer:** CDF $m_W$ excess could indicate new physics at $\sim 5$-$10$ TeV in loops; SUSY, 2HDM, leptoquarks candidates. Tension with other measurements unresolved—more data needed.

The precision electroweak program continues at the LHC and will extend to future colliders. HL-LHC aims for $m_W$ precision below 10 MeV, $m_t$ to $\sim 300$ MeV, and improved Higgs coupling measurements to few-percent level. Proposed lepton colliders (ILC, FCC-ee, CLIC, CEPC) could measure $m_W$, $m_t$, $\sin^2\theta_W$ at the MeV and sub-permille level, testing radiative corrections to two-loop and beyond, probing new physics indirectly to scales $\sim 100$ TeV through virtual effects. The combination of direct searches for new particles and indirect precision constraints provides complementary windows into physics beyond the Standard Model.
