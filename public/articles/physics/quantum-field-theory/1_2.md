# Quantum Field Theory Topic 1.2: Klein-Gordon, Dirac, and EM Fields (Classical)

Before quantizing fields, we must understand their classical behavior thoroughly. Three fundamental fields underpin particle physics: the **Klein-Gordon field** describing spin-0 scalar particles like the Higgs boson and pions, the **Dirac field** describing spin-1/2 fermions like electrons and quarks, and the **electromagnetic field** describing the photon. Each field has its own equation of motion, symmetries, and physical interpretation. The Klein-Gordon equation extends Schrödinger's equation to special relativity, treating space and time symmetrically. The Dirac equation brilliantly incorporates spin and predicts antimatter—one of the greatest theoretical triumphs of the 20th century. The Maxwell field, familiar from classical electromagnetism, requires gauge redundancy that becomes crucial in quantum theory.

These classical field theories share common mathematical structure—they're all solutions to relativistic wave equations derived from Lagrangians. But they differ profoundly in their properties. Scalar fields have no internal indices and obey commutation relations when quantized. Spinor fields carry half-integer spin, require anticommutation to avoid negative energy states, and describe fermions obeying the Pauli exclusion principle. Vector fields have polarization degrees of freedom and gauge invariance, describing massless spin-1 particles. Understanding these classical structures is essential before confronting the conceptual challenges of quantization.

This article explores the Klein-Gordon, Dirac, and electromagnetic fields classically. We'll solve their equations of motion, construct plane wave solutions, count degrees of freedom, understand spinor algebra, examine gauge transformations, and prepare the mathematical machinery needed for canonical quantization.

## Klein-Gordon Field and Plane Waves

The Klein-Gordon equation $(\Box + m^2)\phi = 0$ describes relativistic scalar particles. In momentum space, it becomes an algebraic dispersion relation.

### Example 1: Klein-Gordon Plane Waves

Find the general plane wave solution and interpret the frequency.

**Solution:**

Plane wave ansatz: $\phi(\mathbf{x}, t) = e^{i(\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} - \omega t)}$

Substituting into $(\partial_t^2 - \nabla^2 + m^2)\phi = 0$:

$$(-\omega^2 + |\mathbf{k}|^2 + m^2)e^{i(\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} - \omega t)} = 0$$

Dispersion relation:
$$\omega^2 = |\mathbf{k}|^2 + m^2$$

Taking the square root: $\omega = \pm\sqrt{|\mathbf{k}|^2 + m^2} = \pm\omega_k$

General solution (real field):
$$\phi(\mathbf{x}, t) = \int \frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\left[a(\mathbf{k})e^{i(\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} - \omega_k t)} + a^*(\mathbf{k})e^{-i(\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} - \omega_k t)}\right]$$

where $\omega_k = \sqrt{|\mathbf{k}|^2 + m^2}$ (positive energy only).

The negative frequency solution corresponds to **antiparticles** in the quantum theory—particles propagating backward in time classically.

**Answer:** Klein-Gordon plane waves $\phi \sim e^{i(\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} - \omega_k t)}$ with $\omega_k = \sqrt{k^2 + m^2}$ (relativistic dispersion).

### Example 2: Mode Expansion and Reality Condition

Write the mode expansion satisfying the reality condition for a real scalar field.

**Solution:**

For a real field $\phi^* = \phi$, define mode expansion:

$$\phi(\mathbf{x}, t) = \int \frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3\sqrt{2\omega_k}}\left[a(\mathbf{k})e^{i(\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} - \omega_k t)} + a^*(\mathbf{k})e^{-i(\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} - \omega_k t)}\right]$$

Reality requires: $a^*(\mathbf{k})$ coefficient for negative frequency.

For discrete modes (box normalization $V = L^3$):

$$\phi(\mathbf{x}, t) = \sum_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\omega_k V}}\left[a_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i(\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} - \omega_k t)} + a_{\mathbf{k}}^*e^{-i(\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} - \omega_k t)}\right]$$

where $\mathbf{k} = \frac{2\pi}{L}(n_x, n_y, n_z)$ with integers $n_i$.

Normalization factor $1/\sqrt{2\omega_k}$ is chosen for convenience in quantum theory—it makes creation/annihilation operators dimensionless.

**Answer:** Mode expansion $\phi = \sum_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\omega_k V}}[a_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x}}e^{-i\omega_k t} + \text{c.c.}]$ with reality condition.

## Dirac Equation and Spinors

The Dirac equation $(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - m)\psi = 0$ describes spin-1/2 particles. The Dirac matrices $\gamma^\mu$ satisfy the Clifford algebra $\{\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu\} = 2g^{\mu\nu}$.

### Example 3: Dirac Matrices and Clifford Algebra

Verify the properties of Dirac matrices in the chiral representation.

**Solution:**

Chiral (Weyl) representation:

$$\gamma^0 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \mathbb{1} \\ \mathbb{1} & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \gamma^i = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \sigma^i \\ -\sigma^i & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

where $\sigma^i$ are Pauli matrices and $\mathbb{1}$ is $2 \times 2$ identity.

Check anticommutation:
$$\{\gamma^0, \gamma^0\} = 2(\gamma^0)^2 = 2\begin{pmatrix} \mathbb{1} & 0 \\ 0 & \mathbb{1} \end{pmatrix} = 2g^{00} = 2 \mathbb{1}_4$$ ✓

$$\{\gamma^i, \gamma^j\} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \sigma^i \\ -\sigma^i & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & \sigma^j \\ -\sigma^j & 0 \end{pmatrix} + (i \leftrightarrow j)$$

$$= \begin{pmatrix} -\sigma^i\sigma^j & 0 \\ 0 & -\sigma^i\sigma^j \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} -\sigma^j\sigma^i & 0 \\ 0 & -\sigma^j\sigma^i \end{pmatrix}$$

$$= -\begin{pmatrix} \{\sigma^i, \sigma^j\} & 0 \\ 0 & \{\sigma^i, \sigma^j\} \end{pmatrix} = -2\delta^{ij}\mathbb{1}_4 = 2g^{ij}\mathbb{1}_4$$ ✓

Dirac adjoint: $\bar{\psi} = \psi^\dagger\gamma^0$

Fifth gamma: $\gamma^5 = i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3 = \begin{pmatrix} -\mathbb{1} & 0 \\ 0 & \mathbb{1} \end{pmatrix}$

**Answer:** Dirac matrices satisfy $\{\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu\} = 2g^{\mu\nu}\mathbb{1}_4$ (Clifford algebra in 4D).

### Example 4: Positive and Negative Energy Solutions

Construct the plane wave solutions to the Dirac equation.

**Solution:**

Plane wave: $\psi(\mathbf{x}, t) = u(\mathbf{p})e^{-ip \cdot x}$ where $p \cdot x = Et - \mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{x}$

Dirac equation becomes:
$$(\gamma^\mu p_\mu - m)u(\mathbf{p}) = 0$$

or $(\gamma \cdot p - m)u = 0$ where $\gamma \cdot p = \gamma^0 E - \boldsymbol{\gamma} \cdot \mathbf{p}$.

For positive energy $E = +\sqrt{|\mathbf{p}|^2 + m^2}$:
Two solutions $u^{(s)}(\mathbf{p})$ with $s = 1, 2$ (spin up/down)

For negative energy $E = -\sqrt{|\mathbf{p}|^2 + m^2}$:
Two solutions $v^{(s)}(\mathbf{p})$ with $s = 1, 2$

Complete mode expansion:
$$\psi(\mathbf{x}, t) = \sum_{\mathbf{p}, s}\left[\frac{u^{(s)}(\mathbf{p})}{\sqrt{V}}e^{-ip \cdot x} + \frac{v^{(s)}(\mathbf{p})}{\sqrt{V}}e^{+ip \cdot x}\right]$$

Negative energy solutions are reinterpreted as **antiparticles** (positrons) with positive energy propagating backward in time.

**Answer:** Dirac solutions: $u^{(s)}(\mathbf{p})e^{-ip \cdot x}$ (particles), $v^{(s)}(\mathbf{p})e^{+ip \cdot x}$ (antiparticles).

### Example 5: Spinor Normalization and Completeness

Derive the normalization and completeness relations for Dirac spinors.

**Solution:**

Normalization chosen to be Lorentz invariant:

$$\bar{u}^{(s)}(\mathbf{p})u^{(r)}(\mathbf{p}) = 2m\delta^{sr}$$
$$\bar{v}^{(s)}(\mathbf{p})v^{(r)}(\mathbf{p}) = -2m\delta^{sr}$$
$$\bar{u}^{(s)}(\mathbf{p})v^{(r)}(\mathbf{p}) = 0$$

Completeness (sum over spin states):

$$\sum_{s=1,2} u^{(s)}(\mathbf{p})\bar{u}^{(s)}(\mathbf{p}) = \gamma \cdot p + m$$

$$\sum_{s=1,2} v^{(s)}(\mathbf{p})\bar{v}^{(s)}(\mathbf{p}) = \gamma \cdot p - m$$

These projection operators $\Lambda_\pm(p) = \frac{\gamma \cdot p \pm m}{2m}$ project onto positive/negative energy subspaces.

Orthogonality and completeness are essential for calculating Feynman diagrams—they allow us to sum over intermediate spin states.

**Answer:** Normalization $\bar{u}^{(s)}u^{(r)} = 2m\delta^{sr}$; completeness $\sum_s u^{(s)}\bar{u}^{(s)} = \gamma \cdot p + m$.

## Electromagnetic Field and Gauge Freedom

Maxwell's equations $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = 0$ (vacuum) follow from the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$ where $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$.

### Example 6: Gauge Transformations

Show that gauge transformations leave physics unchanged.

**Solution:**

Under gauge transformation:
$$A_\mu(x) \to A_\mu(x) + \partial_\mu\chi(x)$$

for arbitrary function $\chi(x)$.

Field strength:
$$F_{\mu\nu} \to \partial_\mu(A_\nu + \partial_\nu\chi) - \partial_\nu(A_\mu + \partial_\mu\chi)$$
$$= F_{\mu\nu} + \partial_\mu\partial_\nu\chi - \partial_\nu\partial_\mu\chi = F_{\mu\nu}$$

Since $F_{\mu\nu}$ is gauge invariant, and all observables (electric and magnetic fields) are built from $F_{\mu\nu}$, physics is unchanged.

**Gauge freedom**: Redundancy in description—infinitely many $A_\mu$ describe the same physical state.

Common gauge choices:
1. **Lorenz gauge**: $\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0$ (Lorentz covariant)
2. **Coulomb gauge**: $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{A} = 0$ (useful for quantization)
3. **Temporal gauge**: $A^0 = 0$ (manifest time-translation invariance)

**Answer:** Gauge transformation $A_\mu \to A_\mu + \partial_\mu\chi$ leaves $F_{\mu\nu}$ and all physics invariant.

### Example 7: Coulomb Gauge and Photon Polarizations

Count the physical degrees of freedom in the electromagnetic field.

**Solution:**

Vector potential $A_\mu$ has four components $(A^0, \mathbf{A})$.

**Coulomb gauge** $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{A} = 0$:
- Eliminates one degree of freedom (longitudinal)
- Remaining: $A^0$ and two transverse components of $\mathbf{A}$

Equation of motion in Coulomb gauge: $\Box A^0 = 0$ and $\Box \mathbf{A} = 0$ with $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{A} = 0$

For free field (no sources), $A^0 = 0$ can be chosen (another gauge freedom).

Result: **Two physical polarizations** (transverse modes)

For plane wave $A_\mu \sim \epsilon_\mu e^{ik \cdot x}$:
- $k \cdot \epsilon = 0$ (transversality)
- $\epsilon \cdot \epsilon^* = -1$ (normalization)
- Two independent choices: linear polarizations $\hat{x}, \hat{y}$ or circular $\epsilon_\pm = (\hat{x} \pm i\hat{y})/\sqrt{2}$

These correspond to the two helicity states $h = \pm 1$ of the massless spin-1 photon.

**Answer:** EM field: 4 components − 1 (Coulomb) − 1 (residual) = 2 physical polarizations (massless spin-1).

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
Klein-Gordon: $(\Box + m^2)\phi = 0$
Plane wave: $(-\omega^2 + k^2 + m^2) = 0$
Dispersion: $\omega = \sqrt{k^2 + m^2}$ (relativistic)
Non-relativistic limit: $\omega \approx m + k^2/(2m)$ (Schrödinger)
Massless: $\omega = |k|$ (photon-like)

**Problem 2: B**
Clifford algebra: $\{\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu\} = 2g^{\mu\nu}\mathbb{1}$
Anticommutator, not commutator
Different representations (Dirac, Weyl, Majorana) related by similarity transformations
Essential for Lorentz covariance of Dirac equation
$(\gamma^0)^2 = \mathbb{1}$, $(\gamma^i)^2 = -\mathbb{1}$ (signature $+---$)

**Problem 3: B**
Dirac sea interpretation: Filled negative energy states
Holes in sea = antiparticles (positrons)
Modern: Second quantization automatically gives antiparticles
$v^{(s)}(\mathbf{p})$ spinors describe positrons
Predicted by Dirac (1931), discovered by Anderson (1932)

**Problem 4: C**
Gauge transformation: $A_\mu \to A_\mu + \partial_\mu\chi$
Field strength: $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$ gauge invariant
Observables ($\mathbf{E}$, $\mathbf{B}$) built from $F_{\mu\nu}$
Redundancy: Infinitely many $A_\mu$ describe same physics
Requires gauge fixing for quantization

**Problem 5: C**
Vector potential: 4 components $A_\mu$
Gauge freedom: $A_\mu \to A_\mu + \partial_\mu\chi$ eliminates 1
Equation of motion: 1 constraint
Physical degrees of freedom: 4 − 2 = 2
Two transverse polarizations (helicity $\pm 1$)
Massless spin-1 particle
