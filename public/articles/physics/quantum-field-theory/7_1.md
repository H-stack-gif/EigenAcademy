# Quantum Field Theory Topic 7.1: Spontaneous Symmetry Breaking in Scalar Theories

When a system's ground state fails to respect the symmetries of its governing equations, **spontaneous symmetry breaking** (SSB) occurs, fundamentally altering the spectrum of excitations and vacuum structure. Consider a complex scalar field $\phi$ with potential $V(\phi) = -\mu^2|\phi|^2 + \lambda|\phi|^4$ where $\mu^2 > 0$ and $\lambda > 0$. The Lagrangian respects global U(1) symmetry $\phi \to e^{i\alpha}\phi$, yet the minimum energy configuration sits not at $\phi = 0$ but on a circle of degenerate vacua satisfying $|\phi_0|^2 = \mu^2/(2\lambda) \equiv v^2/2$. Choosing any particular vacuum—say $\phi_0 = v/\sqrt{2}$—spontaneously breaks the symmetry, and expanding fluctuations $\phi = (v+h+i\chi)/\sqrt{2}$ around this **vacuum expectation value** (VEV) reveals two real fields: the **Higgs mode** $h$ with mass $m_h^2 = 2\lambda v^2$ from oscillations in the radial direction, and the **Goldstone mode** $\chi$ with $m_\chi^2 = 0$ from massless fluctuations along the angular direction around the vacuum manifold.

**Goldstone's theorem** makes this structure universal: for every spontaneously broken continuous global symmetry generator, one massless Goldstone boson emerges, representing the zero-energy cost of infinitesimal transformations connecting degenerate vacua. The classic visualization is the **Mexican hat potential** where the bottom forms a circular trough—any point on the trough is equally valid as vacuum, but once chosen, rotations around the trough cost no energy (Goldstone), while climbing the walls costs energy (Higgs). This mechanism appears throughout physics: pions as approximate Goldstones of chiral symmetry in QCD, phonons in crystals, spin waves in magnets, and crucially for particle physics, in the electroweak theory where local gauge symmetry transforms Goldstones into longitudinal polarizations of massive gauge bosons.

The potential's shape determines everything. For $\mu^2 > 0$ (wrong sign mass term), the origin is a local maximum rather than minimum:

$$V(\phi) = -\mu^2|\phi|^2 + \lambda|\phi|^4 = -\mu^2\rho^2 + \lambda\rho^4$$

where $\rho^2 = |\phi|^2$. Finding the minimum by $\partial V/\partial\rho = 0$ gives:

$$-2\mu^2\rho + 4\lambda\rho^3 = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \rho^2 = \frac{\mu^2}{2\lambda} = \frac{v^2}{2}$$

The second derivative confirms this is a minimum: $\partial^2 V/\partial\rho^2 = -2\mu^2 + 12\lambda\rho^2 = -2\mu^2 + 6\mu^2 = 4\mu^2 > 0$. The vacuum energy is:

$$V(\phi_0) = -\frac{\mu^4}{4\lambda}$$

which we can shift to zero by adding a cosmological constant term. The entire circle $|\phi| = v/\sqrt{2}$ consists of degenerate minima related by U(1) transformations.

### Example 1: Vacuum Manifold and Goldstone Mode

For the potential $V(\phi) = -\mu^2|\phi|^2 + \lambda|\phi|^4$, identify the vacuum manifold and show the Goldstone mode is massless.

**Solution:**

Writing $\phi = \rho e^{i\theta}$ with $\rho \geq 0$ real, the potential becomes:

$$V(\rho, \theta) = -\mu^2\rho^2 + \lambda\rho^4$$

Independent of $\theta$! The angular direction is flat—no potential energy change—signaling the U(1) symmetry.

Minimum at $\rho_0 = v/\sqrt{2}$ where $v^2 = \mu^2/\lambda$. The vacuum manifold is:

$$\mathcal{M}_{\text{vac}} = \{\phi = \frac{v}{\sqrt{2}}e^{i\theta} : \theta \in [0, 2\pi)\} \cong S^1$$

A circle in the complex $\phi$ plane.

Choose vacuum $\phi_0 = v/\sqrt{2}$ (setting $\theta = 0$). Expand around this:

$$\phi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(v + h + i\chi)$$

where $h$ is the radial fluctuation, $\chi$ the angular. Substituting into $V(\phi)$:

$$|\phi|^2 = \frac{1}{2}[(v+h)^2 + \chi^2] = \frac{1}{2}[v^2 + 2vh + h^2 + \chi^2]$$

$$V(\phi) = -\mu^2 \cdot \frac{1}{2}[v^2 + 2vh + h^2 + \chi^2] + \lambda \cdot \frac{1}{4}[v^2 + 2vh + h^2 + \chi^2]^2$$

Using $v^2 = \mu^2/\lambda$:

$$V = -\frac{\mu^4}{4\lambda} + \frac{1}{2}(2\lambda v^2)h^2 + \lambda v h^3 + \frac{\lambda}{4}(h^4 + h^2\chi^2 + \cdots)$$

The mass terms are:
- Higgs: $m_h^2 = 2\lambda v^2 = 2\mu^2$ (massive)
- Goldstone: $m_\chi^2 = 0$ (no $\chi^2$ term, massless!)

The Goldstone $\chi$ corresponds to angular fluctuations around the vacuum circle, costing zero energy at long wavelengths.

**Answer:** Vacuum manifold is $S^1$ circle with $|\phi| = v/\sqrt{2}$; Goldstone mode $\chi$ from angular fluctuations is massless.

Physically interpreting the fields requires understanding their origin. The Higgs field $h$ measures how far the field magnitude deviates from the VEV—it's the "amplitude" mode. Its mass $m_h^2 = 2\lambda v^2$ connects to the curvature of the potential at the minimum. The Goldstone $\chi$ is the "phase" mode, representing which point on the vacuum circle we're near. Because all points are equivalent, shifting phase costs no energy, hence masslessness. In terms of the original complex field:

$$\phi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(v+h)e^{i\chi/v}$$

to leading order. The factor $1/v$ in the exponent ensures canonical normalization of $\chi$.

### Example 2: Deriving Field Masses from Potential Expansion

Expand the potential to second order and extract mass terms for $h$ and $\chi$.

**Solution:**

For the quadratic terms:

$$\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial h^2}\bigg|_{h=\chi=0} = -\mu^2 + 3\lambda v^2$$

$v^2 = \mu^2/\lambda$, so:

$$m_h^2 = -\mu^2 + 3\lambda \cdot \frac{\mu^2}{\lambda} = -\mu^2 + 3\mu^2 = 2\mu^2 = 2\lambda v^2$$

For the Goldstone:

$$\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial \chi^2}\bigg|_{h=\chi=0} = -\mu^2 + \lambda v^2 = -\mu^2 + \mu^2 = 0$$

So $m_\chi^2 = 0$ (massless, as required by Goldstone's theorem).

**Answer:** Mass terms: $m_h^2 = 2\lambda v^2 = 2\mu^2$ (Higgs), $m_\chi^2 = 0$ (Goldstone), from second derivatives at VEV.

The counting of degrees of freedom remains constant before and after SSB. Initially, a complex scalar has two real components—two degrees of freedom. After SSB, we still have two: one massive Higgs and one massless Goldstone. The symmetry wasn't lost; it's just hidden in the vacuum structure. Transforming by the broken symmetry shifts which point on the vacuum circle we expand around, but physics remains invariant—we just get a different Goldstone field.

For multiple scalar fields, the vacuum manifold can be higher-dimensional. Consider SU(2) global symmetry with a complex doublet $\Phi = (\phi_1, \phi_2)^T$, potential $V(\Phi) = -\mu^2\Phi^\dagger\Phi + \lambda(\Phi^\dagger\Phi)^2$. The vacuum manifold is $\mathcal{M}_{\text{vac}} = \{\Phi : \Phi^\dagger\Phi = v^2/2\} \cong S^3$. Breaking SU(2) completely gives three Goldstone bosons (dimension of $S^3$), or breaking to U(1) gives two Goldstones (dimension of coset space SU(2)/U(1) $\cong S^2$).

### Example 3: Real Scalar Field with Z₂ Symmetry

Analyze SSB for a real scalar with $\phi \to -\phi$ symmetry and potential $V(\phi) = -\mu^2\phi^2/2 + \lambda\phi^4/4$.

**Solution:**

The Lagrangian has discrete $\mathbb{Z}_2$ symmetry $\phi \to -\phi$. The potential:

$$V(\phi) = -\frac{\mu^2}{2}\phi^2 + \frac{\lambda}{4}\phi^4$$

Extrema from $\frac{dV}{d\phi} = -\mu^2\phi + \lambda\phi^3 = \phi(-\mu^2 + \lambda\phi^2) = 0$:

Solutions: $\phi = 0$ or $\phi^2 = \mu^2/\lambda = v^2$.

The origin $\phi = 0$ is a local maximum (unstable). The two minima are $\phi = \pm v$ where $v = \mu/\sqrt{\lambda}$, both at:

$$V(\pm v) = -\frac{\mu^4}{4\lambda}$$

These are **degenerate** but **isolated**—the vacuum manifold consists of two discrete points. Choose $\phi_0 = +v$ (spontaneously breaks $\mathbb{Z}_2$). Expand $\phi = v + h$:

$$V(v+h) = -\frac{\mu^2}{2}(v+h)^2 + \frac{\lambda}{4}(v+h)^4$$

$$= -\frac{\mu^2v^2}{2} - \mu^2 vh + \frac{\lambda v^4}{4} + \lambda v^3 h + \cdots$$

Using $v^2 = \mu^2/\lambda$, the linear term $-\mu^2 v + \lambda v^3 = 0$ vanishes. The quadratic term:

$$\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial h^2}\bigg|_{h=0} = -\mu^2 + 3\lambda v^2 = -\mu^2 + 3\mu^2 = 2\mu^2$$

giving $m_h^2 = 2\mu^2$. But there's **no Goldstone boson** because the symmetry is discrete, not continuous! The vacuum $\phi = +v$ is isolated—small fluctuations cannot reach $\phi = -v$ continuously. Goldstone's theorem applies only to continuous symmetries.

This model describes the Ising model's continuum limit, the Landau-Ginzburg theory of phase transitions, and the Higgs field in the Standard Model (after projecting to one component).

**Answer:** Real scalar with $\mathbb{Z}_2$: Two isolated vacua $\phi = \pm v$, one massive mode $m_h^2 = 2\mu^2$, no Goldstone (discrete symmetry).

The kinetic term structure changes subtly under SSB. For complex $\phi = (v+h+i\chi)/\sqrt{2}$:

$$|\partial_\mu\phi|^2 = \frac{1}{2}[(\partial_\mu h)^2 + (\partial_\mu\chi)^2]$$

Both fields have canonical kinetic terms. The Goldstone derivative coupling can be written:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\chi} = \frac{1}{2}(\partial_\mu\chi)^2$$

showing manifest shift symmetry $\chi \to \chi + c$ (constant shifts leave kinetic term invariant), directly reflecting the broken U(1).

### Example 4: Shift Symmetry and Goldstone Coupling

Show that the Goldstone field has derivative-only couplings at low energies.

**Solution:**

After SSB, the field $\phi = (v+h)e^{i\chi/v}/\sqrt{2}$ has expansion:

$$\phi \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(v+h)\left(1 + \frac{i\chi}{v} - \frac{\chi^2}{2v^2} + \cdots\right)$$

The kinetic term:

$$|\partial_\mu\phi|^2 = \frac{1}{2}\left|\partial_\mu(v+h)e^{i\chi/v}\right|^2 = \frac{1}{2}\left|e^{i\chi/v}\left[\partial_\mu h + i\frac{v+h}{v}\partial_\mu\chi\right]\right|^2$$

$$= \frac{1}{2}\left[(\partial_\mu h)^2 + \frac{(v+h)^2}{v^2}(\partial_\mu\chi)^2\right]$$

Expanding:

$$|\partial_\mu\phi|^2 = \frac{1}{2}(\partial_\mu h)^2 + \frac{1}{2}(\partial_\mu\chi)^2 + \frac{h}{v}(\partial_\mu\chi)^2 + \frac{h^2}{2v^2}(\partial_\mu\chi)^2$$

The Goldstone appears only with derivatives! Couplings like $h(\partial\chi)^2$ involve derivatives of $\chi$, suppressed by $1/v$ at low energies. This is the **Goldstone boson equivalence theorem**: at energies $E \ll v$, Goldstone interactions are soft.

The shift symmetry $\chi \to \chi + \alpha v$ (residual of broken U(1)) forbids $\chi$ potential terms. Only $\partial_\mu\chi$ appears, consistent with $m_\chi = 0$.

**Answer:** Goldstone has derivative couplings $\sim h(\partial\chi)^2/v$, shift symmetry $\chi \to \chi + c$ forbids potential terms, ensuring masslessness.

Higher-order terms in the potential produce self-interactions. The $\lambda\phi^4$ term generates:

$$V_{\text{int}} = \lambda v h^3 + \frac{\lambda}{4}(h^4 + h^2\chi^2 + \chi^4)$$

giving trilinear coupling $m_h^2 h/(2v)$ and quartic couplings $\sim \lambda$. These govern Higgs self-interactions crucial for vacuum stability and naturalness questions in the Standard Model.

### Example 5: Trilinear Higgs Self-Coupling

Compute the Higgs trilinear coupling from the potential.

**Solution:**

The potential after SSB contains:

$$V \supset \lambda v h^3 + \frac{\lambda h^4}{4}$$

The trilinear vertex is:

$$\lambda_{hhh} = 3\lambda v = 3 \cdot \frac{m_h^2}{2v^2} \cdot v = \frac{3m_h^2}{2v}$$

using $m_h^2 = 2\lambda v^2$. For Standard Model with $m_h = 125$ GeV, $v = 246$ GeV:

$$\lambda_{hhh} = \frac{3 \times (125)^2}{2 \times 246} \approx \frac{46875}{492} \approx 95\text{ GeV}$$

This coupling appears in double Higgs production $gg \to hh$ at the LHC, proceeding through top quark loops with trilinear vertex. The cross section $\sigma(pp \to hh) \sim 30$ fb at $\sqrt{s} = 13$ TeV is small but measurable with high luminosity.

Deviations from $\lambda_{hhh} = 3m_h^2/(2v)$ would signal new physics modifying the Higgs potential (e.g., composite Higgs, extra dimensions). Precision measurements constrain $\lambda_{hhh}$ to be within $\sim 50\%$ of SM value currently.

**Answer:** Trilinear coupling $\lambda_{hhh} = 3m_h^2/(2v) \approx 95$ GeV for SM; tested via $hh$ production at LHC.

The vacuum choice seems arbitrary—why $\phi_0 = v/\sqrt{2}$ instead of $\phi_0 = ve^{i\theta}/\sqrt{2}$ for some other $\theta$? Physical observables cannot depend on this choice (gauge freedom). Different choices correspond to different field parametrizations related by U(1) transformations, but S-matrix elements are identical. This is the essence of spontaneous breaking: the symmetry exists, but vacuum picks a direction.

### Example 6: Vacuum Choice and Field Redefinition

Show that different vacuum choices are related by field redefinition.

**Solution:**

Suppose we choose vacuum $\phi_0 = ve^{i\alpha}/\sqrt{2}$ for some phase $\alpha$. Expand around this:

$$\phi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(v+h')e^{i(\alpha + \chi'/v)}$$

where $h'$, $\chi'$ are new fluctuation fields. The potential becomes:

$$V = -\mu^2|\phi|^2 + \lambda|\phi|^4 = -\mu^2 \cdot \frac{(v+h')^2}{2} + \lambda \cdot \frac{(v+h')^4}{4}$$

Independent of $\alpha$! The masses are:

$$m_{h'}^2 = 2\lambda v^2, \quad m_{\chi'}^2 = 0$$

identical to before. The fields are related by:

$$\phi = e^{i\alpha}\phi', \quad \text{where } \phi' = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(v+h')e^{i\chi'/v}$$

So $h' = h$, $\chi' = \chi - \alpha v$ (shift in Goldstone). Observables like scattering amplitudes $\langle h h | h h\rangle$ are the same in both parametrizations—the vacuum choice is pure convention.

This generalizes to non-Abelian symmetries where the vacuum manifold is higher-dimensional. Any point on $\mathcal{M}_{\text{vac}}$ can be chosen, related by symmetry transformations.

**Answer:** Different vacuum choices related by $\phi \to e^{i\alpha}\phi$; Goldstone shifts $\chi \to \chi - \alpha v$, physics unchanged.

Classical field configurations with non-trivial vacuum structure include **domain walls** separating regions with $\phi = +v$ and $\phi = -v$ in the real scalar case, or **vortices** in the complex case where the phase winds $\theta \to \theta + 2\pi$ around a core. These topological defects store energy proportional to their size and appear in cosmological phase transitions, condensed matter systems, and early universe scenarios.

### Example 7: Energy Density in SSB Vacuum

Calculate the vacuum energy density and discuss cosmological implications.

**Solution:**

The vacuum sits at $\phi = v/\sqrt{2}$ with potential energy:

$$V_{\text{vac}} = V(\phi_0) = -\mu^2 \cdot \frac{v^2}{2} + \lambda \cdot \frac{v^4}{4} = -\frac{\mu^4}{4\lambda} + \frac{\mu^4}{4\lambda} = 0$$

after using $v^2 = \mu^2/\lambda$. Cancels. Add a constant to make $V(\phi_0) = 0$ exactly:

$$V(\phi) = \lambda\left(|\phi|^2 - \frac{v^2}{2}\right)^2$$

This form manifestly has $V(\phi_0) = 0$ at the minimum.

However, in cosmology, the vacuum energy density $\rho_{\text{vac}} = \langle V \rangle$ contributes to the cosmological constant $\Lambda$. For the Higgs field with $v = 246$ GeV:

$$\rho_{\text{vac}} \sim v^4 \sim (246\text{ GeV})^4 \sim 10^{48}\text{ eV}^4$$

Converting to critical density units: $\rho_c \sim 10^{-47}\text{ GeV}^4$, the ratio is:

$$\frac{\rho_{\text{vac}}}{\rho_c} \sim 10^{95}$$

vastly exceeding observed dark energy $\rho_{\Lambda} \sim \rho_c$! This is the **cosmological constant problem**—why doesn't SSB in particle physics dominate cosmology? Quantum corrections to vacuum energy are even worse, requiring fine-tuning to $\sim 10^{-120}$ precision.

The resolution remains unknown. Possible explanations: supersymmetry cancellations (broken), anthropic principle (multiverse), modified gravity, or as-yet-undiscovered symmetry.

**Answer:** Vacuum energy $V(\phi_0) = 0$ (after constant shift), but scale $v^4 \sim 10^{48}$ eV$^4$ vastly exceeds observed $\rho_\Lambda$ (cosmological constant problem).

The vacuum structure's richness extends beyond simple field theory. In quantum mechanics, SSB manifests in ground state degeneracy—the Hamiltonian has a symmetry, but individual eigenstates don't. The infinite volume limit is crucial: in finite systems, quantum tunneling connects different classical vacua through instantons, lifting the degeneracy. Only in the thermodynamic limit does true SSB occur, with the vacuum permanently "stuck" in one choice. This connects to the measurement problem in quantum mechanics—why do we observe definite outcomes when the Hamiltonian is symmetric?

The potential's analytic properties determine stability beyond perturbation theory. For $\lambda \phi^4$ theory, quantum corrections generate higher-order terms $\sim \phi^6/\Lambda^2$, $\phi^8/\Lambda^4$ from loop diagrams. If these destabilize the vacuum at large field values, the theory is only an effective description valid below cutoff $\Lambda$. The Standard Model faces this issue: including gravity, the Higgs potential must be UV-completed at the Planck scale $M_{\text{Pl}} \sim 10^{19}$ GeV. Why is the electroweak scale $v = 246$ GeV so much smaller? This **hierarchy problem** suggests new physics (supersymmetry, technicolor, extra dimensions) near the TeV scale to stabilize the hierarchy.

The phase transition aspect connects field theory to statistical mechanics and cosmology. At high temperatures $T \gg v$, thermal fluctuations restore symmetry: $\langle\phi\rangle_T = 0$. As the universe cools through critical temperature $T_c \sim v$, the system undergoes a phase transition with $\langle\phi\rangle$ developing spontaneously. The order of transition (first-order, second-order, crossover) depends on parameters. First-order transitions produce bubbles of true vacuum nucleating in false vacuum, bubble collisions generating gravitational waves potentially observable in LIGO/LISA. The electroweak phase transition in the early universe occurred around $T_c \sim 100$ GeV at $t \sim 10^{-11}$ seconds, though in the Standard Model it's a smooth crossover rather than first-order.

The formalism extends straightforwardly to gauge theories, but with a crucial twist: when SSB combines with local gauge symmetry, Goldstone bosons aren't physical—they're absorbed as longitudinal polarizations of gauge bosons (Higgs mechanism), providing masses without explicit mass terms that would break gauge invariance. This resolves the apparent conflict between gauge symmetry and massive vector bosons, foundational to electroweak theory.

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B** — Minimize $V(\rho) = -\mu^2\rho^2 + \lambda\rho^4$ gives $\rho_0^2 = \mu^2/(2\lambda) = v^2/2$ where $v = \mu/\sqrt{\lambda}$. The VEV $\langle\phi\rangle = v/\sqrt{2}$ breaks U(1) spontaneously. Origin $\phi = 0$ is unstable maximum.

**Problem 2: C** — Expanding $V(\phi)$ around VEV to quadratic order: $m_h^2 = \partial^2 V/\partial h^2|_{\text{min}} = 2\lambda v^2 = 2\mu^2$ using $v^2 = \mu^2/\lambda$. Higgs mode from radial oscillations in potential. At tree level, $m_h = \sqrt{2}\mu = \sqrt{2\lambda}v$.

**Problem 3: C** — Goldstone's theorem: Broken continuous global symmetry → massless Goldstone boson. The $\chi$ field represents fluctuations along vacuum manifold (angular mode), costing zero energy. Shift symmetry $\chi \to \chi + c$ forbids mass term. Valid for U(1), SU(N), etc.

**Problem 4: C** — Discrete $\mathbb{Z}_2$ ($\phi \to -\phi$): Two isolated vacua $\phi = \pm v$, one massive scalar $m_h^2 = 2\mu^2$, **no Goldstone**. Goldstone's theorem requires continuous symmetry. Cannot interpolate continuously between $\pm v$ vacua, so no massless mode.

**Problem 5: B** — Vacuum manifold $\mathcal{M}_{\text{vac}} = \{|\phi| = v/\sqrt{2}\}$ is circle in complex $\phi$ plane. U(1) symmetry $\phi \to e^{i\theta}\phi$ rotates around circle—all degenerate minima. Dimension = 1 = number of Goldstones. Choosing one point breaks symmetry spontaneously.
