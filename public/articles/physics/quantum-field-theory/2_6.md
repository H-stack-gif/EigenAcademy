# Quantum Field Theory Topic 2.6: Causality and Vacuum Structure

The vacuum—the lowest energy state $|0\rangle$—might seem trivial, but in quantum field theory it's remarkably rich. Even with no particles present, fields have zero-point energy from each oscillator mode contributing $\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega_k$. This **vacuum energy** is infinite (sum over all modes), yet finite energy differences like the Casimir force are measurable. Virtual particles constantly pop in and out of existence—**vacuum fluctuations** responsible for spontaneous emission, the Lamb shift, and quantum corrections to scattering amplitudes. The vacuum isn't empty; it's a seething sea of quantum activity. But not all vacua are created equal. Some field theories have multiple possible vacuum states (degenerate minima of the potential), and quantum tunneling between them leads to **instantons** and non-perturbative effects.

Understanding vacuum structure requires understanding propagators beyond the Feynman propagator. The **retarded propagator** $D_R$ describes causal response—signals propagating forward in time only. The **advanced propagator** $D_A$ propagates backward. **Wightman functions** encode time-ordered correlations without vacuum projections. Each has distinct analytic properties in the complex energy plane. The **Källén-Lehmann representation** reveals that the full propagator encodes the entire particle spectrum, with spectral density $\rho(\mu^2) \geq 0$ guaranteed by unitarity. Single-particle states appear as poles, multi-particle continua as branch cuts. This spectral structure connects deeply to causality through analytic continuation.

This article explores vacuum structure, propagator zoo, causality constraints, and spectral representations. We'll examine vacuum energy and fluctuations, define various propagators, understand their causal properties, derive the Källén-Lehmann representation, discuss stable vs. metastable vacua, introduce cluster decomposition, and see how virtual particles emerge from quantum fluctuations.

## Vacuum Energy and Fluctuations

### Example 1: Vacuum Energy Calculation

Calculate the vacuum energy for a free scalar field.

**Solution:**

Hamiltonian (normal-ordered):
$$:\hat{H}: = \sum_{\mathbf{k}}\omega_k\hat{a}^\dagger_{\mathbf{k}}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}}$$

gives $:\hat{H}:|0\rangle = 0$ (vacuum has zero energy).

But **before normal ordering**:
$$\hat{H} = \sum_{\mathbf{k}}\omega_k\left(\hat{a}^\dagger_{\mathbf{k}}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}} + \frac{1}{2}\right)$$

**Vacuum energy**:
$$E_0 = \langle 0|\hat{H}|0\rangle = \sum_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{\omega_k}{2}$$

Converting to integral (continuum limit):
$$E_0 = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{|\mathbf{k}|^2 + m^2}$$

This **diverges** as $|\mathbf{k}| \to \infty$ (UV divergence)!

**Physical interpretation**:
- Each mode: Zero-point energy $\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega_k$
- Infinitely many modes → infinite total
- Observable: Energy **differences** (Casimir effect)

**Regularization**: Introduce cutoff $\Lambda$, or use dimensional regularization.

Cosmological constant problem: Why is observed vacuum energy so small?

**Answer:** Vacuum energy $E_0 = \sum_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{\omega_k}{2} \to \infty$ (UV divergent, needs regularization).

### Example 2: Vacuum Fluctuations of Fields

Calculate field fluctuations in the vacuum state.

**Solution:**

**Mean field**: $\langle 0|\hat{\phi}(x)|0\rangle = 0$ (vacuum expectation vanishes).

**Variance**:
$$\langle 0|\hat{\phi}^2(x)|0\rangle = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{2\omega_k}$$

This is **non-zero** and diverges (UV).

Regularized version:
$$\langle\phi^2\rangle_{\text{reg}} \sim \int_0^\Lambda\frac{k^2 dk}{2\sqrt{k^2 + m^2}} \sim \Lambda^2$$

(Quadratically divergent.)

**Physical consequences**:
- Spontaneous emission: Atoms decay due to vacuum field fluctuations
- Lamb shift: Energy levels shifted by virtual photon exchange
- Casimir force: Boundary conditions modify $\sum \omega_k$

**Virtual particles**: Temporary particle-antiparticle pairs created from vacuum.

Uncertainty principle: $\Delta E \Delta t \sim \hbar$ allows temporary energy violation.

**Answer:** Vacuum fluctuations: $\langle 0|\phi^2|0\rangle \neq 0$ (non-zero, UV divergent), cause observable effects.

## Propagator Zoo

### Example 3: Retarded Propagator

Define the retarded propagator and its causal properties.

**Solution:**

**Retarded (causal) propagator**:
$$D_R(x - y) = \theta(t_x - t_y)\langle 0|[\hat{\phi}(x), \hat{\phi}(y)]|0\rangle$$

Step function $\theta(t_x - t_y)$ ensures **causality**: propagation only forward in time.

Properties:
- Vanishes for $t_x < t_y$ (no backward propagation)
- Non-zero for $t_x > t_y$ and $(x-y)^2 > 0$ (timelike separation)
- Vanishes for $(x-y)^2 < 0$ (spacelike separation, microcausality)

**Momentum space**:
$$\tilde{D}_R(p) = \frac{i}{p^2 - m^2 + i\epsilon\text{sgn}(p^0)}$$

Pole prescription: Both poles below real axis for $p^0 > 0$.

**Physical use**:
- Classical response functions (Green's function for wave equation)
- Real-time evolution with sources
- Not used for S-matrix (Feynman propagator better)

**Answer:** Retarded propagator $D_R$: Forward time only ($\theta(t_x-t_y)$), describes causal response.

### Example 4: Advanced Propagator

Compare advanced and retarded propagators.

**Solution:**

**Advanced propagator**:
$$D_A(x - y) = -\theta(t_y - t_x)\langle 0|[\hat{\phi}(x), \hat{\phi}(y)]|0\rangle$$

Propagates **backward** in time (negative step function).

**Momentum space**:
$$\tilde{D}_A(p) = \frac{i}{p^2 - m^2 - i\epsilon\text{sgn}(p^0)}$$

Poles: Both above real axis.

**Relationship**:
$$D_F(x - y) = \theta(t_x - t_y)D_+(x - y) + \theta(t_y - t_x)D_-(x - y)$$

where $D_\pm$ are positive/negative frequency Wightman functions.

**Physical meaning**:
- Advanced: Acausal (response before source)
- Not used for physical scattering
- Appears in some theoretical constructions

**Combination**:
$$D_R(x - y) - D_A(x - y) = \langle 0|[\hat{\phi}(x), \hat{\phi}(y)]|0\rangle$$

(Commutator of fields, independent of time ordering.)

**Answer:** Advanced $D_A$: Backward time ($-\theta(t_y-t_x)$), acausal, mainly theoretical tool.

### Example 5: Wightman Functions

Define Wightman functions and their role.

**Solution:**

**Positive-frequency Wightman function**:
$$D_+(x - y) = \langle 0|\hat{\phi}(x)\hat{\phi}(y)|0\rangle$$

(No time ordering, operators in fixed order.)

**Negative-frequency**:
$$D_-(x - y) = \langle 0|\hat{\phi}(y)\hat{\phi}(x)|0\rangle$$

**Properties**:
- $D_+(x) \neq D_-(x)$ (order matters)
- Both contain only positive energy in their Fourier transform
- Analytic in certain regions of complex time

**Relationship to Feynman propagator**:
$$D_F(x - y) = \theta(t_x - t_y)D_+(x - y) + \theta(t_y - t_x)D_-(x - y)$$

**Use**:
- Axiomatic QFT (Wightman axioms)
- Thermal field theory
- Algebraic QFT

**Spectral properties**: Encode vacuum state structure.

**Answer:** Wightman $D_\pm$: No time ordering, fixed operator order, encode vacuum correlations.

## Källén-Lehmann Representation

### Example 6: Spectral Representation of Propagator

Derive the Källén-Lehmann form of the full propagator.

**Solution:**

Insert complete set of energy eigenstates between field operators:

$$D(x - y) = \langle 0|\hat{\phi}(x)\hat{\phi}(y)|0\rangle = \sum_n\langle 0|\hat{\phi}(0)|n\rangle\langle n|\hat{\phi}(0)|0\rangle e^{-ip_n \cdot (x-y)}$$

Summing over all intermediate states $|n\rangle$ with momentum $p_n$.

Define **spectral density**:
$$\rho(\mu^2) = \sum_n(2\pi)\delta^4(p - p_n)|\langle 0|\hat{\phi}(0)|n\rangle|^2$$

**Källén-Lehmann representation**:
$$\tilde{D}_F(p) = \int_0^\infty\frac{d\mu^2}{2\pi}\frac{\rho(\mu^2)}{p^2 - \mu^2 + i\epsilon}$$

**Key properties**:
1. $\rho(\mu^2) \geq 0$ (positivity from unitarity)
2. Single-particle: $\rho(\mu^2) \approx Z\delta(\mu^2 - m^2) + \text{continuum}$
3. Continuum: Multi-particle states ($\mu^2 \geq (2m)^2$)
4. Spectral density encodes **entire particle spectrum**

**Physical interpretation**:
- Poles of $\tilde{D}_F$ at $p^2 = m_n^2$ → stable particles
- Branch cuts → multi-particle thresholds
- $\rho(\mu^2)$ measures "coupling" of vacuum to states of mass $\mu$

**Answer:** Källén-Lehmann: $\tilde{D}_F(p) = \int d\mu^2 \frac{\rho(\mu^2)}{p^2 - \mu^2 + i\epsilon}$ with $\rho \geq 0$ (spectral representation).

### Example 7: Field Strength and Spectral Density

Relate the field strength $Z$ to the spectral density.

**Solution:**

For interacting theory near single-particle pole:

$$\tilde{D}_F(p) \approx \frac{iZ}{p^2 - m^2 + i\epsilon} + \text{regular}$$

where $Z$ is the **field strength renormalization** ($Z < 1$ typically).

Spectral density near $\mu^2 = m^2$:
$$\rho(\mu^2) = Z\delta(\mu^2 - m^2) + \rho_{\text{cont}}(\mu^2)$$

**Physical meaning**:
- $Z$: Overlap between bare field and physical single-particle state
- $Z < 1$: Field creates multi-particle states with some probability
- $1 - Z$: Weight in continuum (virtual particle clouds)

**LSZ formula**: External legs contribute $\sqrt{Z}$ each.

For free field: $Z = 1$, $\rho(\mu^2) = \delta(\mu^2 - m^2)$ (pure single-particle).

For interacting: $Z < 1$, continuum present.

**Answer:** Field strength $Z$: Residue of propagator at single-particle pole, $Z < 1$ in interacting theory.

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
Vacuum energy: $E_0 = \sum_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{\omega_k}{2}$ (infinite)
UV divergent (sum over all modes)
Zero-point energy per mode: $\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega$
Observable: Energy differences (Casimir)
Removed by normal ordering: $:\hat{H}:$

**Problem 2: B**
Retarded: $D_R = \theta(t_x - t_y)[\phi(x), \phi(y)]$
Forward time only (causal)
$D_R(x-y) = 0$ for $t_x < t_y$
Used for classical response
Different from Feynman (both directions)

**Problem 3: B**
Wightman: $D_\pm = \langle 0|\phi(x)\phi(y)|0\rangle$
Fixed operator order (no $T$)
Encode vacuum structure
Axiomatic QFT foundation
Different from Feynman (has $T$)

**Problem 4: C**
Källén-Lehmann: $\rho(\mu^2) \geq 0$ (positivity)
From unitarity (probability interpretation)
Single-particle: $\rho \sim Z\delta(\mu^2 - m^2)$
Continuum: $\mu^2 \geq (2m)^2$ (multi-particle)
Encodes particle spectrum

**Problem 5: B**
Field strength: $Z < 1$ in interacting theory
Free field: $Z = 1$
Interactions: Field creates virtual particle clouds
$1 - Z$ = weight in continuum
Calculated perturbatively: $Z = 1 + O(\lambda)$
