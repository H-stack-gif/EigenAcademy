# Quantum Field Theory Topic 7.5: Higgs Phenomenology

The 2012 discovery of the Higgs boson at the Large Hadron Collider represented the culmination of a fifty-year search for the last missing piece of the Standard Model. With mass $m_h = 125.09 \pm 0.24$ GeV, the Higgs is produced primarily through **gluon fusion** $gg \to h$ via top quark loops (cross section $\sigma \approx 50$ pb at $\sqrt{s} = 13$ TeV), with subleading contributions from **vector boson fusion** $qq \to qqh$ ($\sigma \approx 4$ pb), **associated production** with W/Z bosons ($Wh$, $Zh$, $\sigma \approx 1.5$ pb), and associated production with top pairs ($t\bar{t}h$, $\sigma \approx 0.5$ pb). The Higgs decays predominantly to bottom quarks $h \to b\bar{b}$ (BR $\approx 58\%$) followed by $W$ pairs $h \to WW^*$ (21%), tau leptons $h \to \tau^+\tau^-$ (6%), $Z$ pairs $h \to ZZ^*$ (3%), and photons $h \to \gamma\gamma$ (0.2%)—the latter being the **golden channel** for discovery despite tiny branching ratio due to clean experimental signature. The total width $\Gamma_h \approx 4.1$ MeV corresponds to lifetime $\tau_h \sim 1.6 \times 10^{-22}$ seconds, making the Higgs extremely short-lived and observable only through decay products.

The Higgs coupling structure reveals its fundamental role in mass generation: couplings to fermions are $g_{hff} = -y_f = -m_f/v$ (proportional to fermion mass), to gauge bosons $g_{hVV} \propto m_V^2/v$ (proportional to boson mass squared), and Higgs self-coupling $\lambda_{hhh} = 3m_h^2/(2v)$ determining the potential shape. This proportionality to mass—predicted by the Higgs mechanism—has been confirmed experimentally across two orders of magnitude in fermion masses from $\tau$ leptons (1.8 GeV) to top quarks (173 GeV), providing the most direct evidence that the observed particle is indeed the Standard Model Higgs. Precision measurements of these couplings test for deviations that could indicate composite Higgs models, supersymmetry, or other beyond-SM physics, with current agreement at the 10-20% level gradually improving toward percent precision.

The production mechanism diversity enables comprehensive Higgs studies. Gluon fusion dominates at hadron colliders, proceeding through a top quark triangle loop since the Higgs couples most strongly to the heaviest particle kinematically accessible. The amplitude has interesting properties:

$$\mathcal{M}(gg \to h) \sim \frac{\alpha_s y_t}{m_t}F_{\triangle}(\tau)$$

where $\tau = 4m_t^2/m_h^2$ and $F_{\triangle}(\tau)$ is a loop function. In the heavy top limit $m_t \gg m_h$, the loop decouples and $F_\triangle \to 1$, giving the effective operator:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}} = -\frac{\alpha_s}{12\pi v}h F^a_{\mu\nu}F^{a\mu\nu}$$

This effective field theory description works well for $m_h = 125$ GeV since $m_t/m_h \approx 1.4$ is moderately heavy.

### Example 1: Gluon Fusion Production Cross Section

Estimate the $gg \to h$ cross section at LHC using the effective operator and parton luminosity.

**Solution:**

The effective Lagrangian gives:

$$\mathcal{M}(gg \to h) = \frac{\alpha_s}{3\pi v}(\epsilon_1 \cdot \epsilon_2)(k_1 \cdot k_2)A_{1/2}(\tau)$$

where $A_{1/2}(\tau)$ is the fermion loop function. For $m_t \gg m_h$: $A_{1/2} \to 1$.

The partonic cross section:

$$\hat{\sigma}(gg \to h) = \frac{\pi^2}{8m_h}\Gamma(h \to gg)\delta(s - m_h^2)$$

where the partial width:

$$\Gamma(h \to gg) = \frac{\alpha_s^2 m_h^3}{72\pi^3 v^2}\left|\sum_q A_{1/2}(\tau_q)\right|^2$$

For top dominance: $A_{1/2} \approx 4/3$ (exact), giving:

$$\Gamma(h \to gg) \approx \frac{\alpha_s^2(m_h) m_h^3}{72\pi^3 v^2}\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)^2$$

With $\alpha_s(m_h) \approx 0.113$, $m_h = 125$ GeV, $v = 246$ GeV:

$$\Gamma(h \to gg) \approx \frac{(0.113)^2 \times (125)^3}{72\pi^3 \times (246)^2}\times \frac{16}{9}$$

$$\approx \frac{0.0128 \times 1.95 \times 10^6}{72 \times 31 \times 60516}\times 1.78 \approx 0.33\text{ MeV}$$

The hadronic cross section at LHC ($\sqrt{s} = 13$ TeV):

$$\sigma(pp \to h) = \int dx_1 dx_2\, g(x_1, m_h^2)g(x_2, m_h^2)\hat{\sigma}(gg \to h)$$

Gluon PDFs at $x \sim m_h/\sqrt{s} \sim 0.01$ give $xg(x) \sim 5$. The luminosity factor $\mathcal{L}_{gg} \sim 10^3$ pb$^{-1}$ GeV$^{-2}$ gives:

$$\sigma \approx 50\text{ pb}$$

Including NLO and NNLO QCD corrections (K-factor $\sim 2.5$), the cross section is well-predicted.

**Answer:** $gg \to h$ proceeds via top loop, $\sigma \approx 50$ pb at LHC 13 TeV; gluon PDFs at small $x$ drive production.

Vector boson fusion (VBF) provides a distinctive signature with two forward jets and central Higgs. The topology arises from quarks radiating W or Z bosons that fuse into Higgs:

$$qq \to qq(WW/ZZ) \to qqh$$

The cross section is smaller than gluon fusion but has cleaner final state—two high-rapidity jets with large separation and small central hadronic activity. This allows better background rejection for $h \to \tau^+\tau^-$ and $h \to WW^*$ channels where multijet backgrounds would otherwise dominate.

### Example 2: Higgs Decay to Bottom Quarks

Calculate $\Gamma(h \to b\bar{b})$ and explain why it's difficult to observe at hadron colliders.

**Solution:**

The Higgs-bottom Yukawa coupling:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\frac{m_b}{v}h\bar{b}b$$

The partial width with color factor:

$$\Gamma(h \to b\bar{b}) = \frac{3m_b^2 m_h}{8\pi v^2}\left(1 - \frac{4m_b^2}{m_h^2}\right)^{3/2}$$

Using running bottom mass $m_b(m_h) \approx 2.8$ GeV at scale $\mu = m_h$:

$$\Gamma_0(h \to b\bar{b}) = \frac{G_F m_b^2 m_h}{4\pi\sqrt{2}} N_c$$

where $N_c = 3$:

$$\Gamma_0 \approx \frac{1.166 \times 10^{-5} \times (2.8)^2 \times 125 \times 3}{4\pi\sqrt{2}}$$

$$\approx \frac{1.166 \times 10^{-5} \times 2940}{17.77} \approx 1.93\text{ MeV}$$

Including QCD corrections $(1 + 5.67\alpha_s/\pi)$:

$$\Gamma(h \to b\bar{b}) \approx 1.93\text{ MeV} \times 1.2 \approx 2.4\text{ MeV}$$

Branching ratio: $\text{BR}(h \to b\bar{b}) = 2.4/4.1 \approx 58\%$.

**Challenge at hadron colliders**: Enormous QCD $b\bar{b}$ background from $gg \to b\bar{b}$ swamps signal. Signal/background $\sim 10^{-9}$! Observation requires associated production ($Zh$, $Wh$, $t\bar{t}h$) where leptonic W/Z decay provides trigger. ATLAS/CMS observed $h \to b\bar{b}$ in 2018 using VH production.

**Answer:** $\Gamma(h \to b\bar{b}) \approx 2.4$ MeV (BR $\approx 58\%$); difficult at hadron colliders due to huge QCD background, requires associated production.

The discovery channels exploited rare but clean decay modes. The diphoton channel $h \to \gamma\gamma$ proceeds through W boson and top quark loops, giving branching ratio only $0.2\%$ but providing excellent mass resolution ($\Delta m/m \sim 1\%$) and manageable backgrounds. The four-lepton channel $h \to ZZ^* \to 4\ell$ ($\ell = e, \mu$) has even tinier branching ratio ($0.01\%$) but is essentially background-free, providing the cleanest Higgs mass measurement.

### Example 3: Higgs to Diphoton Decay

Calculate $\Gamma(h \to \gamma\gamma)$ including W and top loops, and explain the discovery significance.

**Solution:**

The $h\gamma\gamma$ coupling is loop-induced since Higgs doesn't couple directly to massless photons. Contributing loops:

W boson: $A_W = -\frac{m_W^2}{v}F_1(\tau_W)$ where $\tau_W = 4m_W^2/m_h^2$

Top quark: $A_t = \frac{m_t}{v}N_c Q_t^2 F_{1/2}(\tau_t)$ where $Q_t = 2/3$, $N_c = 3$

The loop functions: $F_1(\tau) \to 7$ (W, heavy limit), $F_{1/2}(\tau) \to -4/3$ (fermion, heavy limit).

The partial width:

$$\Gamma(h \to \gamma\gamma) = \frac{\alpha^2 m_h^3}{256\pi^3 v^2}\left|\sum_{i}A_i\right|^2$$

Numerically: $A_W \approx -8.3$, $A_t \approx +1.8$, giving $|A_W + A_t|^2 \approx 43$.

$$\Gamma(h \to \gamma\gamma) \approx \frac{(1/137)^2 \times (125)^3 \times 43}{256\pi^3 \times (246)^2}$$

$$\approx \frac{5.33 \times 10^{-5} \times 1.95 \times 10^6 \times 43}{256\pi^3 \times 60516} \approx 0.0094\text{ MeV}$$

Branching ratio: $\text{BR}(h \to \gamma\gamma) = 0.0094/4.1 \approx 0.23\%$.

**Discovery significance**: Despite tiny BR, the diphoton channel provided the first evidence for Higgs at LHC. The narrow diphoton mass peak at 125 GeV stood out clearly above smooth continuum background from $gg \to \gamma\gamma$, $q\bar{q} \to \gamma\gamma$. ATLAS and CMS both observed $\sim 5\sigma$ excess in $\gamma\gamma$ in July 2012 announcement. The excellent mass resolution ($\pm 1.5$ GeV) and large cross section times branching ratio ($\sigma \times \text{BR} \approx 0.1$ pb) made this the golden channel.

**Answer:** $\Gamma(h \to \gamma\gamma) \approx 9.4$ keV (BR $\approx 0.23\%$) from W and top loops; golden discovery channel with clean signature and good mass resolution.

The Higgs width $\Gamma_h \approx 4.1$ MeV is too narrow to measure directly (experimental resolution $\sim$ GeV). Instead, it's inferred from cross sections times branching ratios combined with theoretical predictions. Off-shell Higgs production $gg \to h^* \to ZZ$ at $m_{ZZ} > 2m_Z$ constrains the width, with current limits $\Gamma_h < 13$ MeV (95% CL), consistent with SM prediction.

### Example 4: Higgs Lifetime and Decay Length

Calculate the Higgs lifetime and typical decay length, explaining why direct observation is impossible.

**Solution:**

The lifetime-width relation:

$$\tau_h = \frac{\hbar}{\Gamma_h}$$

With $\Gamma_h = 4.1$ MeV $= 4.1 \times 10^6$ eV:

$$\tau_h = \frac{6.582 \times 10^{-16}\text{ eV·s}}{4.1 \times 10^6\text{ eV}} = 1.6 \times 10^{-22}\text{ s}$$

Decay length (typical boost $\gamma \sim 1$ for Higgs at rest in lab):

$$c\tau_h = 3 \times 10^{10}\text{ cm/s} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-22}\text{ s} \approx 5 \times 10^{-12}\text{ cm} = 50\text{ fm}$$

For comparison:
- W boson: $\Gamma_W \approx 2$ GeV, $c\tau_W \sim 0.1$ fm
- Top quark: $\Gamma_t \approx 1.5$ GeV, $c\tau_t \sim 0.1$ fm (decays before hadronizing)
- Z boson: $\Gamma_Z \approx 2.5$ GeV, $c\tau_Z \sim 0.08$ fm
- B meson: $\Gamma_B \sim 10^{-12}$ eV, $c\tau_B \sim 500$ μm (observable secondary vertex!)

The Higgs decay length $50$ fm is far below detector resolution (typically $\sim$ 100 μm for vertex detectors). The Higgs is produced and decays within a single point in spacetime from experimental perspective. We observe only decay products, never the Higgs itself—it's a **resonance** in the cross section, not a directly detected particle.

**Boost effects**: At LHC with $p_T(h) \sim 50$ GeV, $\gamma \sim p_T/m_h \sim 0.4$ (typical), so $\gamma c\tau_h \sim 20$ fm (still unobservable).

**Answer:** $\tau_h \approx 1.6 \times 10^{-22}$ s, decay length $c\tau \sim 50$ fm (far below detector resolution); Higgs observed only via decay products.

The coupling measurements test the mass-proportionality prediction. Defining signal strength $\mu = \sigma \cdot \text{BR}/(\sigma \cdot \text{BR})_{\text{SM}}$, measurements find:

$\mu(\gamma\gamma) = 1.10 \pm 0.07$  
$\mu(ZZ^*) = 1.01 \pm 0.08$  
$\mu(WW^*) = 1.09 \pm 0.08$  
$\mu(b\bar{b}) = 1.04 \pm 0.13$  
$\mu(\tau\tau) = 1.09 \pm 0.11$

All consistent with SM at 10% level. The coupling to top quarks measured through $t\bar{t}h$ production gives $\mu(t\bar{t}h) = 1.12 \pm 0.23$, confirming the Higgs couples to the heaviest fermion as expected.

The associated production channels provide crucial tests. The $Vh$ production ($V = W, Z$) allows tagging through leptonic decay of the vector boson, providing clean triggers. The $t\bar{t}h$ channel, though rarest among major production modes, directly probes the top Yukawa coupling—the largest coupling in the SM. Combined measurements in multiple decay channels ($\gamma\gamma$, $b\bar{b}$, $WW^*$, $\tau\tau$) extract coupling modifiers $\kappa_V$ (vector bosons) and $\kappa_F$ (fermions), testing for deviations from the SM prediction $\kappa = 1$.

### Example 5: Higgs Self-Coupling Measurement

Explain double Higgs production and its role in measuring the trilinear coupling $\lambda_{hhh}$.

**Solution:**

The Higgs potential:

$$V(h) = -\frac{m_h^2}{2}h^2 + \lambda_{hhh}h^3 + \lambda_{hhhh}h^4$$

where trilinear coupling:

$$\lambda_{hhh} = \frac{3m_h^2}{2v} = \frac{3 \times (125)^2}{2 \times 246} \approx 96\text{ GeV}$$

Double Higgs production $gg \to hh$ at LHC proceeds through:

**Box diagram**: Top quark box with two Higgs emissions (no $hhh$ vertex)

**Triangle diagram**: Top loop producing single Higgs which splits via $hhh$ vertex

These interfere destructively! The triangle contribution is proportional to $\lambda_{hhh}$, making $\sigma(gg \to hh)$ sensitive to trilinear coupling.

Cross section at $\sqrt{s} = 13$ TeV:

$$\sigma(pp \to hh) \approx 30\text{ fb}$$

Very small! With $\mathcal{L} = 3000$ fb$^{-1}$ (HL-LHC), expect $\sim 90,000$ $hh$ events, but backgrounds from $t\bar{t}b\bar{b}$ are formidable.

Decay channels:
- $hh \to b\bar{b}b\bar{b}$: Largest BR $\sim 33\%$ but huge QCD background
- $hh \to b\bar{b}\gamma\gamma$: Clean signature, BR $\sim 0.2\%$
- $hh \to b\bar{b}\tau\tau$: Moderate BR $\sim 7\%$, manageable background

Current limit: $|\lambda_{hhh}/\lambda_{hhh}^{\text{SM}}| < 6.6$ (95% CL). HL-LHC expects $\sim 50\%$ precision, directly testing Higgs potential shape.

If $\lambda_{hhh} \neq 3m_h^2/(2v)$, it would indicate modified Higgs sector (composite Higgs, SUSY corrections, dimension-6 operators from new physics).

**Answer:** $\lambda_{hhh} = 3m_h^2/(2v) \approx 96$ GeV determines $hhh$ vertex; measured via $gg \to hh$ ($\sigma \sim 30$ fb), current limits weak, HL-LHC will measure to $\sim 50\%$.

The hierarchy problem looms over the Higgs. Quantum corrections to $m_h^2$ from loops are quadratically divergent:

$$\delta m_h^2 \sim \frac{\lambda_t^2}{16\pi^2}\Lambda_{\text{UV}}^2$$

If the cutoff $\Lambda_{\text{UV}}$ is the Planck scale $M_{\text{Pl}} \sim 10^{19}$ GeV, then $\delta m_h^2 \sim 10^{34}$ GeV$^2$, requiring cancellations to 32 decimal places to give $m_h = 125$ GeV. This fine-tuning problem motivates:

Supersymmetry: Fermion-boson loops cancel via superpartners

Composite Higgs: Higgs is composite bound state, cutoff $\sim$ TeV

Extra dimensions: Planck scale lowered to TeV via large extra dimensions

These scenarios predict new particles at TeV scale, searched for intensively at LHC with no discoveries yet, increasing tension.

The rare Higgs decays probe new physics indirectly. The decay $h \to \mu\mu$ has tiny SM branching ratio $\text{BR} \approx 0.02\%$ (muon Yukawa $y_\mu \sim 10^{-3}$) but provides a direct test of lepton universality and sensitivity to lepton-flavor-violating new physics. Flavor-changing decays like $h \to Z\gamma$ occur at one-loop in the SM with $\text{BR} \sim 0.15\%$, sensitive to charged Higgs bosons in 2HDM or stop squarks in SUSY. Invisible decays $h \to \chi\chi$ to dark matter particles are constrained to $\text{BR}(h \to \text{inv}) < 11\%$ from searches in $Zh$ production with $Z \to \ell\ell$.

### Example 6: Quantum Corrections to Higgs Mass

Calculate the one-loop top quark correction to Higgs mass and estimate the fine-tuning.

**Solution:**

The top Yukawa coupling generates a quadratically divergent correction:

$$\delta m_h^2 = -\frac{3y_t^2}{8\pi^2}\Lambda^2 + \text{log terms}$$

where $y_t = \sqrt{2}m_t/v \approx 1$ (largest coupling).

For cutoff $\Lambda = M_{\text{Pl}} = 1.22 \times 10^{19}$ GeV:

$$\delta m_h^2 \approx -\frac{3 \times 1}{8\pi^2}(1.22 \times 10^{19}\text{ GeV})^2 \approx -6 \times 10^{34}\text{ GeV}^2$$

The bare mass must be tuned:

$$m_{h,\text{bare}}^2 = m_h^2 - \delta m_h^2 \approx 6 \times 10^{34}\text{ GeV}^2$$

to cancel and give physical $m_h^2 = (125\text{ GeV})^2 = 1.56 \times 10^4$ GeV$^2$.

Fine-tuning:

$$\Delta = \frac{\delta m_h^2}{m_h^2} \approx \frac{6 \times 10^{34}}{1.56 \times 10^4} \approx 4 \times 10^{30}$$

Cancellation to 30 decimal places! This is the **naturalness problem**.

If new physics appears at scale $\Lambda_{\text{NP}} \sim 1$ TeV, the fine-tuning is only:

$$\Delta \sim \frac{(1000)^2}{(125)^2} \approx 64$$

More palatable but still requires $\sim 2\%$ level tuning. SUSY with stop masses $\sim 1$ TeV would naturally explain this, but LHC hasn't found stops up to $\sim 2$ TeV, pushing required fine-tuning to $\sim 1\%$ or worse.

**Answer:** Top loop gives $\delta m_h^2 \sim -\Lambda^2/(8\pi^2)$; for $\Lambda = M_{\text{Pl}}$, requires tuning to $\sim 10^{-30}$ (hierarchy problem).

Vacuum stability depends on whether the Higgs potential remains bounded from below at all field values. The quartic coupling $\lambda(\mu)$ runs according to:

$$\mu\frac{d\lambda}{d\mu} = \frac{1}{16\pi^2}\left[12\lambda^2 - 6y_t^2\lambda + \frac{3}{8}(2g^4 + (g^2+g'^2)^2) + 6y_t^4\right]$$

The large negative contribution $-6y_t^2\lambda$ from top Yukawa drives $\lambda$ toward negative values at high scales. For $m_h = 125.09$ GeV and $m_t = 172.76$ GeV, $\lambda$ becomes negative around $\mu \sim 10^{10}$ GeV, making the potential unbounded. However, the barrier is high enough that vacuum decay rate is negligible—we live in a metastable vacuum.

### Example 7: Vacuum Stability and Metastability

Calculate the scale where $\lambda$ goes negative and estimate vacuum lifetime.

**Solution:**

Running the Higgs quartic coupling from $m_h = 125$ GeV with two-loop RG equations:

At $\mu = m_h$: $\lambda(m_h) \approx 0.126$

At $\mu = m_t$: $\lambda(m_t) \approx 0.094$ (top threshold)

At $\mu = 10^{10}$ GeV: $\lambda \approx 0$ (crosses zero)

At $\mu > 10^{10}$ GeV: $\lambda < 0$ (unstable!)

The instability scale depends sensitively on $m_h$ and $m_t$:
- $m_h$ increased by 1 GeV → instability scale rises by factor $\sim 3$
- $m_t$ decreased by 1 GeV → instability scale rises by factor $\sim 2$

Current central values give crossover at $\Lambda_{\text{instab}} \sim 10^{10}$ GeV.

Vacuum decay rate via tunneling (bounce solution):

$$\Gamma/V \sim e^{-S_B}$$

where $S_B$ is the Euclidean action of the bounce configuration. Estimates give:

$$S_B \sim 10^{4}$$

$$\Gamma/V \sim e^{-10^4} \sim 10^{-4343}$$

Decay rate per Hubble volume per Hubble time:

$$\Gamma \cdot V_H \cdot t_H \sim 10^{-600}$$

Negligible! The vacuum is metastable but extraordinarily long-lived—lifetime exceeds $10^{600}$ times the age of the universe.

**Interpretation**: We're living on the edge between stability and metastability. If $m_h$ were 2 GeV heavier or $m_t$ were 1 GeV lighter, we'd be in the absolutely stable regime. The near-criticality might be coincidence or could hint at new physics (e.g., inflation fixing initial conditions, multiverse anthropics).

**Answer:** $\lambda$ crosses zero at $\mu \sim 10^{10}$ GeV for $m_h = 125$ GeV, $m_t = 173$ GeV; vacuum metastable with lifetime $\gg 10^{600}$ years.

The Higgs boson's discovery completed the Standard Model particle content but opened new questions. Why is the Higgs mass light compared to Planck scale (hierarchy problem)? Why is the vacuum metastable rather than stable or unstable? Are there additional Higgs bosons (two-Higgs-doublet models, SUSY)? Is the Higgs composite or elementary? Does it participate in dark matter or baryogenesis? The precision Higgs program at HL-LHC and future colliders (ILC, FCC, CLIC) aims to measure couplings to percent-level precision, probe rare decays like $h \to \mu\mu$, measure self-coupling through double Higgs production, and search for deviations from SM predictions that could reveal the path to physics beyond.
