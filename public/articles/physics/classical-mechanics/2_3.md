# Article 2.3: Applications of Newton's Second Law

Newton's second law $\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$ appears deceptively simple, but its power emerges in application. From projectile motion to banked curves, from pulley systems to coupled oscillators, this single equation—properly applied—predicts the behavior of mechanical systems. The art lies in identifying all forces, choosing appropriate coordinates, decomposing vectors, and solving the resulting differential equations. Complex systems may involve multiple objects, constraints, and time-dependent forces, requiring systematic analysis and mathematical sophistication.

The applications in this article progress from one-dimensional problems to three-dimensional motion, from single particles to coupled systems. We emphasize problem-solving strategy: drawing free-body diagrams, writing component equations, recognizing constraints, and checking limiting cases. Many problems involve circular motion, where the acceleration always points toward the center with magnitude $a_c = v^2/r = r\omega^2$. Understanding when to use Cartesian versus polar coordinates, when to apply energy methods versus force analysis, represents a crucial skill in classical mechanics.

These applications bridge the gap between abstract principles and real-world physics. The techniques developed here—constraint equations, normal force calculations, handling friction—form the foundation for more advanced topics including Lagrangian mechanics, where constraints are built into the formalism itself. Every problem solved deepens intuition about forces, motion, and the mathematical structure underlying classical mechanics.

## One-Dimensional Motion with Various Forces

For motion along a line (say, $x$-axis), Newton's second law reduces to:
$$\sum F_x = m\ddot{x}$$

This is a second-order ordinary differential equation. The form depends on how forces depend on position, velocity, and time.

**Cases:**
1. **Constant force:** $F = F_0$ → $\ddot{x} = F_0/m$ → constant acceleration
2. **Position-dependent:** $F(x)$ → $m\ddot{x} = F(x)$ → energy methods useful
3. **Velocity-dependent:** $F(v)$ → $m\ddot{x} = F(\dot{x})$ → separate variables
4. **Time-dependent:** $F(t)$ → $m\ddot{x} = F(t)$ → integrate twice

### Example 1: Linear Drag (Velocity-Dependent Force)

A particle of mass $m = 2$ kg experiences linear drag $F_{\text{drag}} = -bv$ with $b = 0.5$ kg/s. If released from rest, find $v(t)$.

**Solution:** Newton's second law:
$$m\frac{dv}{dt} = -bv$$

Separate variables:
$$\frac{dv}{v} = -\frac{b}{m}dt$$

Integrate:
$$\ln v = -\frac{b}{m}t + C$$

Initial condition: $v(0) = v_0$, so $C = \ln v_0$.

$$\ln\left(\frac{v}{v_0}\right) = -\frac{b}{m}t$$
$$v(t) = v_0 e^{-bt/m}$$

If $v_0 = 10$ m/s:
$$v(t) = 10e^{-0.5t/2} = 10e^{-0.25t} \text{ m/s}$$

At $t = 4$ s: $v = 10e^{-1} = 3.68$ m/s.

**Answer:** $v(t) = v_0e^{-bt/m}$ (exponential decay)

## Two and Three-Dimensional Motion

In multiple dimensions, apply Newton's second law component-wise:
$$\sum F_x = m\ddot{x}, \quad \sum F_y = m\ddot{y}, \quad \sum F_z = m\ddot{z}$$

If forces in different directions are independent, the motion decouples into separate one-dimensional problems.

**Projectile motion:** With only gravity acting:
- $x$-direction: $\ddot{x} = 0$ (constant velocity)
- $y$-direction: $\ddot{y} = -g$ (constant acceleration)

Result: parabolic trajectory.

### Example 2: Projectile with Air Resistance

A projectile experiences gravity and linear drag $\mathbf{F}_d = -b\mathbf{v}$. Write the equations of motion.

**Solution:** 

Forces:
- Gravity: $\mathbf{F}_g = -mg\hat{\mathbf{y}}$
- Drag: $\mathbf{F}_d = -b(v_x\hat{\mathbf{x}} + v_y\hat{\mathbf{y}})$

Newton's second law:
$$m\ddot{x} = -b\dot{x}$$
$$m\ddot{y} = -mg - b\dot{y}$$

These are coupled through initial conditions but mathematically independent.

**$x$-equation:**
$$\ddot{x} + \frac{b}{m}\dot{x} = 0 \implies v_x(t) = v_{x0}e^{-bt/m}$$

**$y$-equation:**
$$\ddot{y} + \frac{b}{m}\dot{y} = -g$$

This is an inhomogeneous first-order equation in $v_y$. Solution:
$$v_y(t) = \left(v_{y0} + \frac{mg}{b}\right)e^{-bt/m} - \frac{mg}{b}$$

As $t \to \infty$, $v_x \to 0$ and $v_y \to -mg/b$ (terminal velocity).

**Answer:** Equations shown; motion more complex than parabolic

## Constrained Motion: Inclined Planes

On an incline at angle $\theta$:
- Choose coordinates: $x$ along incline, $y$ perpendicular
- Gravity component along incline: $F_{\parallel} = mg\sin\theta$
- Gravity component perpendicular: $F_{\perp} = mg\cos\theta$
- Normal force: $N = mg\cos\theta$ (if no perpendicular acceleration)

With friction ($\mu_k$):
$$ma = mg\sin\theta - \mu_k mg\cos\theta = mg(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta)$$

### Example 3: Two Blocks on Double Incline

Block $m_1 = 3$ kg on incline at $\theta_1 = 30°$ is connected via pulley to block $m_2 = 5$ kg on incline at $\theta_2 = 45°$ (both on opposite sides). Assume frictionless. Find acceleration.

**Solution:**

For $m_1$ (up incline positive): $T - m_1g\sin\theta_1 = m_1a$

For $m_2$ (down incline positive): $m_2g\sin\theta_2 - T = m_2a$

Add equations:
$$m_2g\sin\theta_2 - m_1g\sin\theta_1 = (m_1 + m_2)a$$

$$a = \frac{g(m_2\sin\theta_2 - m_1\sin\theta_1)}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{9.8(5\sin 45° - 3\sin 30°)}{8}$$

$$= \frac{9.8(5 \cdot 0.707 - 3 \cdot 0.5)}{8} = \frac{9.8(3.535 - 1.5)}{8} = \frac{9.8(2.035)}{8} = 2.49 \text{ m/s}^2$$

**Answer:** $a = 2.49$ m/s² ($m_2$ accelerates down its incline)

## Circular Motion and Banked Curves

For circular motion at constant speed $v$ in a circle of radius $r$:
- Acceleration magnitude: $a_c = v^2/r = r\omega^2$
- Direction: toward center (centripetal)
- Required centripetal force: $F_c = mv^2/r$

This centripetal force must be provided by real forces: tension, gravity components, normal forces, friction, etc.

**Banked curves:** A road banked at angle $\theta$ allows cars to navigate curves without friction.

For no friction: $N\sin\theta = mv^2/r$ (horizontal) and $N\cos\theta = mg$ (vertical).

Dividing: $\tan\theta = v^2/(rg)$

### Example 4: Conical Pendulum

A mass $m = 0.5$ kg on a string of length $L = 1$ m swings in a horizontal circle at angle $\theta = 30°$ from vertical. Find the tension and angular velocity.

**Solution:**

Forces:
- Tension $T$ (along string)
- Weight $mg$ (downward)

Circular motion in horizontal plane with radius $r = L\sin\theta$:

**Vertical:** $T\cos\theta - mg = 0$ (no vertical acceleration)
$$T = \frac{mg}{\cos\theta} = \frac{0.5(9.8)}{\cos 30°} = \frac{4.9}{0.866} = 5.66 \text{ N}$$

**Horizontal:** $T\sin\theta = m\omega^2 r = m\omega^2 L\sin\theta$
$$T = m\omega^2 L$$
$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{T}{mL}} = \sqrt{\frac{5.66}{0.5(1)}} = \sqrt{11.32} = 3.36 \text{ rad/s}$$

Alternatively, from vertical equation: $T\cos\theta = mg$, so $T = mg/\cos\theta$.

From horizontal: $T\sin\theta = m\omega^2L\sin\theta$, giving $T = m\omega^2L$.

Equating: $\frac{mg}{\cos\theta} = m\omega^2L$
$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L\cos\theta}} = \sqrt{\frac{9.8}{1(0.866)}} = \sqrt{11.32} = 3.36 \text{ rad/s}$$

**Answer:** $T = 5.66$ N, $\omega = 3.36$ rad/s

### Example 5: Banked Curve

A car navigates a curve of radius $r = 50$ m banked at $\theta = 20°$. What speed allows navigation without friction?

**Solution:** For no friction, banking angle satisfies:
$$\tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{rg}$$

$$v = \sqrt{rg\tan\theta} = \sqrt{50(9.8)\tan 20°} = \sqrt{490(0.364)} = \sqrt{178.4} = 13.4 \text{ m/s}$$

Converting: $13.4$ m/s $= 48.2$ km/h $\approx 30$ mph.

**Answer:** $v = 13.4$ m/s (without friction)

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**

$F = -6x$ is a restoring force proportional to displacement: $m\ddot{x} = -6x$.

This is the equation for simple harmonic motion: $\ddot{x} + (6/2)x = 0$, with $\omega^2 = 3$.

---

**Problem 2: B**

With linear drag, horizontal component $v_x \to 0$ and vertical component $v_y \to -mg/b$ (terminal velocity downward).

---

**Problem 3: A**

$a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta) = 9.8(\sin 30° - 0.2\cos 30°)$
$= 9.8(0.5 - 0.2 \times 0.866) = 9.8(0.5 - 0.173) = 9.8(0.327) = 3.2$ m/s²

---

**Problem 4: D**

$F_c = m\omega^2r = 5(3)^2(2) = 5(9)(2) = 90$ N

---

**Problem 5: C**

$v = \sqrt{rg\tan\theta} = \sqrt{40(9.8)\tan 30°} = \sqrt{392(0.577)} = \sqrt{226} = 15$ m/s
