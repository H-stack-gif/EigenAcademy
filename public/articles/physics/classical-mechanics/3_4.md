# Article 3.4: Non-conservative Forces

Not all forces in nature are conservative. Friction, air resistance, and viscous drag dissipate mechanical energy into heat. Driving forces can add energy to systems. When non-conservative forces act, mechanical energy is no longer conserved, and the simple equation $K + U = $ constant fails. Yet energy itself remains conserved—it merely transforms into other forms like thermal energy, sound, or radiation. The work-energy theorem still applies, but now includes work by non-conservative forces explicitly.

The mathematical treatment requires separating forces into conservative and non-conservative components. Conservative forces contribute to potential energy; non-conservative forces appear explicitly in the work term. This leads to a modified energy equation: $\Delta E = W_{\text{nc}}$, where $W_{\text{nc}}$ is work by non-conservative forces. For dissipative forces, $W_{\text{nc}} < 0$, and mechanical energy decreases. For driving forces, $W_{\text{nc}} > 0$, adding energy to the system.

This article develops the framework for handling non-conservative forces in energy methods. We derive the modified work-energy theorem, analyze dissipation rates for various drag laws, and explore systems with both conservative and non-conservative forces. The techniques here—calculating energy loss, finding terminal velocities, solving differential equations with damping—are essential for realistic physics problems where friction and air resistance cannot be neglected.

## Work-Energy Theorem with Non-Conservative Forces

Separate total force into conservative and non-conservative parts:
$$\mathbf{F}_{\text{total}} = \mathbf{F}_c + \mathbf{F}_{nc}$$

where $\mathbf{F}_c = -\nabla U$.

Work-energy theorem:
$$W_{\text{total}} = \Delta K$$

$$\int \mathbf{F}_c \cdot d\mathbf{r} + \int \mathbf{F}_{nc} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = K_f - K_i$$

For conservative forces: $\int \mathbf{F}_c \cdot d\mathbf{r} = -\Delta U = U_i - U_f$

Therefore:
$$U_i - U_f + W_{nc} = K_f - K_i$$

$$K_i + U_i + W_{nc} = K_f + U_f$$

$$\Delta E = W_{nc}$$

where $E = K + U$ is mechanical energy.

**Interpretation:**
- If $W_{nc} < 0$ (friction): mechanical energy decreases
- If $W_{nc} > 0$ (driving force): mechanical energy increases
- If $W_{nc} = 0$: mechanical energy conserved

### Example 1: Block Sliding with Friction

A block of mass $m = 5$ kg slides down a 30° incline of length $L = 10$ m with kinetic friction $\mu_k = 0.2$. Starting from rest, find final speed.

**Solution:** 

**Conservative forces:** Gravity (potential energy)

**Non-conservative:** Friction

Initial state: $K_i = 0$, $U_i = mgL\sin\theta = 5(9.8)(10)(0.5) = 245$ J

Final state: $K_f = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2$, $U_f = 0$

Work by friction:
$$W_{nc} = -f_k \cdot L = -\mu_k N \cdot L = -\mu_k mg\cos\theta \cdot L$$
$$= -0.2(5)(9.8)\cos(30°)(10) = -0.2(5)(9.8)(0.866)(10) = -84.9 \text{ J}$$

Energy equation:
$$E_i + W_{nc} = E_f$$
$$245 + (-84.9) = \frac{1}{2}(5)v_f^2$$
$$160.1 = 2.5v_f^2$$
$$v_f = \sqrt{64.04} = 8.0 \text{ m/s}$$

**Answer:** $v_f = 8.0$ m/s

## Dissipative Forces and Energy Loss

For dissipative forces (friction, drag), the work done is always negative:
$$W_{nc} = -\int |\mathbf{F}_{dissipative}| \, ds < 0$$

where $s$ is distance traveled.

**Power dissipated:**
$$P_{dissipated} = \mathbf{F}_{dissipative} \cdot \mathbf{v} < 0$$

The magnitude $|P_{dissipated}|$ is the rate of energy conversion to heat.

### Linear Drag

For $\mathbf{F}_d = -b\mathbf{v}$ (low Reynolds number):
$$P_{dissipated} = -b\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v} = -bv^2$$

**Equation of motion (vertical fall):**
$$m\frac{dv}{dt} = mg - bv$$

Terminal velocity (when $dv/dt = 0$):
$$v_{term} = \frac{mg}{b}$$

### Example 2: Energy Dissipation by Linear Drag

A particle of mass $m = 0.5$ kg falls from rest with linear drag $b = 2$ kg/s. Calculate total energy dissipated in reaching 95% of terminal velocity.

**Solution:** Terminal velocity:
$$v_{term} = \frac{mg}{b} = \frac{0.5(9.8)}{2} = 2.45 \text{ m/s}$$

At 95%: $v_f = 0.95(2.45) = 2.33$ m/s.

Equation of motion:
$$m\frac{dv}{dt} = mg - bv$$

Solution (from separation of variables):
$$v(t) = v_{term}(1 - e^{-bt/m})$$

When $v = 0.95v_{term}$:
$$0.95 = 1 - e^{-bt/m} \implies e^{-bt/m} = 0.05$$
$$t = -\frac{m}{b}\ln(0.05) = \frac{0.5}{2}\ln(20) = 0.75 \text{ s}$$

Distance fallen:
$$y = \int_0^t v(t') dt' = v_{term}\left[t + \frac{m}{b}e^{-bt/m}\right]_0^t$$
$$= v_{term}\left[t + \frac{m}{b}(e^{-bt/m} - 1)\right]$$
$$= 2.45\left[0.75 + 0.25(0.05 - 1)\right] = 2.45[0.75 - 0.2375] = 1.26 \text{ m}$$

Energy lost:
$$\Delta E = (K_i + U_i) - (K_f + U_f)$$
$$= (0 + mgy) - \left(\frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 + 0\right)$$
$$= (0.5)(9.8)(1.26) - \frac{1}{2}(0.5)(2.33)^2$$
$$= 6.17 - 1.36 = 4.81 \text{ J}$$

**Answer:** $4.81$ J dissipated as heat

### Quadratic Drag

For $\mathbf{F}_d = -\frac{1}{2}\rho C_d A v^2\hat{\mathbf{v}}$ (high Reynolds number):
$$P_{dissipated} = -\frac{1}{2}\rho C_d A v^3$$

Terminal velocity:
$$v_{term} = \sqrt{\frac{2mg}{\rho C_d A}}$$

### Example 3: Terminal Velocity Comparison

Compare terminal velocities for linear vs. quadratic drag for a raindrop: $m = 0.001$ kg, $r = 2$ mm.

**Solution:** 

**Linear drag:** Estimate $b \sim 6\pi\eta r$ (Stokes' law for small sphere in viscous fluid)
For air: $\eta \approx 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ Pa·s
$$b = 6\pi(1.8 \times 10^{-5})(0.002) = 6.8 \times 10^{-7} \text{ kg/s}$$
$$v_{term,linear} = \frac{mg}{b} = \frac{0.001(9.8)}{6.8 \times 10^{-7}} = 14400 \text{ m/s}$$

This is absurdly high! Linear drag not appropriate for raindrop.

**Quadratic drag:** $\rho_{air} = 1.2$ kg/m³, $C_d \approx 0.5$ (sphere), $A = \pi r^2 = 1.26 \times 10^{-5}$ m²
$$v_{term,quad} = \sqrt{\frac{2mg}{\rho C_d A}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(0.001)(9.8)}{1.2(0.5)(1.26 \times 10^{-5})}}$$
$$= \sqrt{\frac{0.0196}{7.56 \times 10^{-6}}} = \sqrt{2593} = 50.9 \text{ m/s}$$

This is more reasonable (~180 km/h).

**Answer:** Quadratic drag gives $v_{term} \approx 50$ m/s; linear drag inapplicable

## Systems with Driving Forces

When external forces do positive work, mechanical energy increases:
$$\Delta E = W_{external} > 0$$

**Example:** Motor lifting a mass, person pumping a swing, engine accelerating a car.

### Example 4: Power Required for Constant-Speed Motion

A car of mass $m = 1200$ kg drives at constant speed $v = 25$ m/s up a 5° incline against rolling resistance $F_r = 500$ N. What power must the engine deliver?

**Solution:** At constant speed, $K = $ constant, so all engine power goes to:
1. Increasing gravitational potential energy
2. Overcoming rolling resistance

**Gravitational component:**
$$P_g = \frac{dU}{dt} = mg\sin\theta \cdot v = 1200(9.8)\sin(5°)(25)$$
$$= 1200(9.8)(0.087)(25) = 25600 \text{ W}$$

**Rolling resistance:**
$$P_r = F_r \cdot v = 500(25) = 12500 \text{ W}$$

**Total power:**
$$P_{total} = P_g + P_r = 25600 + 12500 = 38100 \text{ W} = 38.1 \text{ kW}$$

**Answer:** $P = 38.1$ kW $\approx 51$ hp

### Example 5: Work-Energy with Variable Force

A spring-loaded projectile launcher ($k = 1000$ N/m) is compressed $x_0 = 0.2$ m, then releases a $m = 0.5$ kg projectile. Including friction force $f = 20$ N over compression distance, find launch speed.

**Solution:** 

Initial energy (spring compressed):
$$E_i = \frac{1}{2}kx_0^2 = \frac{1}{2}(1000)(0.2)^2 = 20 \text{ J}$$

Work by friction during launch:
$$W_{nc} = -f \cdot x_0 = -20(0.2) = -4 \text{ J}$$

Final energy (projectile launched):
$$E_f = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2$$

Energy equation:
$$E_i + W_{nc} = E_f$$
$$20 - 4 = \frac{1}{2}(0.5)v_f^2$$
$$16 = 0.25v_f^2$$
$$v_f = 8 \text{ m/s}$$

**Answer:** $v_f = 8$ m/s
