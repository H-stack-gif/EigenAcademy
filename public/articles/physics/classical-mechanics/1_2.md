# Article 1.2: Coordinate Systems

The choice of coordinate system can transform an intractable problem into a straightforward calculation. While Cartesian coordinates $(x, y, z)$ are natural for describing motion in straight lines and rectangular geometries, many physical systems possess cylindrical or spherical symmetry. A planet orbiting a star demands spherical coordinates; a bead sliding on a circular wire requires polar coordinates. The art of classical mechanics lies partly in recognizing which coordinate system makes the physics transparent.

Coordinate transformations do more than simplify calculations—they reveal the geometric structure of physical laws. A vector equation like $\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$ holds in any coordinate system, but the components of $\mathbf{F}$ and $\mathbf{a}$ transform in specific ways. Understanding these transformations prepares us for the deeper principle that physical laws should be independent of our choice of coordinates—a principle that ultimately leads to the power of Lagrangian mechanics, where we work with generalized coordinates tailored to each problem.

This article develops the three most important curvilinear coordinate systems: plane polar, cylindrical, and spherical. We establish transformation formulas, compute scale factors and unit vector derivatives, and derive the gradient, divergence, and curl in each system. These formulas, though initially daunting, become second nature with practice and unlock entire classes of problems that would be nightmarish in Cartesian coordinates.

## Cartesian Coordinates

Cartesian coordinates $(x, y, z)$ use three mutually perpendicular axes with constant unit vectors $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$, $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$, $\hat{\mathbf{z}}$. Since these unit vectors don't change with position, differentiation is straightforward:
$$\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{x}}}{dt} = \frac{d\hat{\mathbf{y}}}{dt} = \frac{d\hat{\mathbf{z}}}{dt} = \mathbf{0}$$

Position: $\mathbf{r} = x\hat{\mathbf{x}} + y\hat{\mathbf{y}} + z\hat{\mathbf{z}}$

Velocity: $\mathbf{v} = \dot{x}\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \dot{y}\hat{\mathbf{y}} + \dot{z}\hat{\mathbf{z}}$

Acceleration: $\mathbf{a} = \ddot{x}\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \ddot{y}\hat{\mathbf{y}} + \ddot{z}\hat{\mathbf{z}}$

The line element is $d\mathbf{r} = dx\hat{\mathbf{x}} + dy\hat{\mathbf{y}} + dz\hat{\mathbf{z}}$, and $ds^2 = dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2$.

Cartesian coordinates are ideal for projectile motion, simple pendulums (in small angle approximation), and any system with rectangular symmetry.

## Plane Polar Coordinates

Plane polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ describe points in a plane using distance from origin $r$ and angle $\theta$ from the $x$-axis.

Transformation to Cartesian:
$$x = r\cos\theta, \quad y = r\sin\theta$$

Inverse transformation:
$$r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}, \quad \theta = \arctan(y/x)$$

The unit vectors are:
$$\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \cos\theta\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \sin\theta\hat{\mathbf{y}}$$
$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = -\sin\theta\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \cos\theta\hat{\mathbf{y}}$$

Critically, these unit vectors **rotate** with position. Their time derivatives are:
$$\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{dt} = \dot{\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}, \quad \frac{d\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{dt} = -\dot{\theta}\hat{\mathbf{r}}$$

Position: $\mathbf{r} = r\hat{\mathbf{r}}$

The line element is $d\mathbf{r} = dr\hat{\mathbf{r}} + r\,d\theta\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$, giving $ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2d\theta^2$.

### Example 1: Unit Vector Derivatives in Polar Coordinates

Verify that $d\hat{\mathbf{r}}/d\theta = \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ and $d\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}/d\theta = -\hat{\mathbf{r}}$.

**Solution:** From $\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \cos\theta\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \sin\theta\hat{\mathbf{y}}$:
$$\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{d\theta} = -\sin\theta\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \cos\theta\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$$

From $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = -\sin\theta\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \cos\theta\hat{\mathbf{y}}$:
$$\frac{d\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{d\theta} = -\cos\theta\hat{\mathbf{x}} - \sin\theta\hat{\mathbf{y}} = -(\cos\theta\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \sin\theta\hat{\mathbf{y}}) = -\hat{\mathbf{r}}$$

These relationships are crucial for deriving velocity and acceleration in polar coordinates.

**Answer:** Verified: $\partial\hat{\mathbf{r}}/\partial\theta = \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ and $\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}/\partial\theta = -\hat{\mathbf{r}}$

### Example 2: Transformation of a Vector

A velocity vector in Cartesian coordinates is $\mathbf{v} = 3\hat{\mathbf{x}} - 4\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ at point $(x,y) = (3, 4)$ meters. Express this velocity in polar coordinates.

**Solution:** First find $r$ and $\theta$ at this point:
$$r = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5, \quad \theta = \arctan(4/3)$$

We have $\cos\theta = 3/5$ and $\sin\theta = 4/5$.

The polar unit vectors at this point are:
$$\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \frac{3}{5}\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \frac{4}{5}\hat{\mathbf{y}}, \quad \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = -\frac{4}{5}\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \frac{3}{5}\hat{\mathbf{y}}$$

Express $\mathbf{v}$ in polar basis:
$$v_r = \mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}} = (3\hat{\mathbf{x}} - 4\hat{\mathbf{y}}) \cdot \left(\frac{3}{5}\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \frac{4}{5}\hat{\mathbf{y}}\right) = \frac{9}{5} - \frac{16}{5} = -\frac{7}{5}$$

$$v_\theta = \mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = (3\hat{\mathbf{x}} - 4\hat{\mathbf{y}}) \cdot \left(-\frac{4}{5}\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \frac{3}{5}\hat{\mathbf{y}}\right) = -\frac{12}{5} - \frac{12}{5} = -\frac{24}{5}$$

**Answer:** $\mathbf{v} = -\frac{7}{5}\hat{\mathbf{r}} - \frac{24}{5}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ m/s

## Cylindrical Coordinates

Cylindrical coordinates $(s, \phi, z)$ extend polar coordinates to three dimensions with an added height $z$.

Transformation:
$$x = s\cos\phi, \quad y = s\sin\phi, \quad z = z$$

Unit vectors:
$$\hat{\mathbf{s}} = \cos\phi\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \sin\phi\hat{\mathbf{y}}$$
$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} = -\sin\phi\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \cos\phi\hat{\mathbf{y}}$$
$$\hat{\mathbf{z}} = \hat{\mathbf{z}}$$

The $\hat{\mathbf{s}}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$ vectors rotate with $\phi$ but $\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ remains fixed:
$$\frac{\partial\hat{\mathbf{s}}}{\partial\phi} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}, \quad \frac{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}{\partial\phi} = -\hat{\mathbf{s}}, \quad \frac{\partial\hat{\mathbf{z}}}{\partial\phi} = \mathbf{0}$$

Line element: $d\mathbf{r} = ds\hat{\mathbf{s}} + s\,d\phi\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} + dz\hat{\mathbf{z}}$

Metric: $ds^2 = ds^2 + s^2d\phi^2 + dz^2$

Cylindrical coordinates are natural for problems with cylindrical symmetry: flow through pipes, motion of a particle around a vertical axis, torque problems.

## Spherical Coordinates

Spherical coordinates $(r, \theta, \phi)$ use:
- $r$: distance from origin
- $\theta$: polar angle from $z$-axis (colatitude), $0 \leq \theta \leq \pi$
- $\phi$: azimuthal angle in $xy$-plane, $0 \leq \phi < 2\pi$

Transformation:
$$x = r\sin\theta\cos\phi, \quad y = r\sin\theta\sin\phi, \quad z = r\cos\theta$$

Unit vectors:
$$\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \sin\theta\cos\phi\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \sin\theta\sin\phi\hat{\mathbf{y}} + \cos\theta\hat{\mathbf{z}}$$
$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = \cos\theta\cos\phi\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \cos\theta\sin\phi\hat{\mathbf{y}} - \sin\theta\hat{\mathbf{z}}$$
$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} = -\sin\phi\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \cos\phi\hat{\mathbf{y}}$$

All three unit vectors rotate with position. The derivatives are:
$$\frac{\partial\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{\partial\theta} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}, \quad \frac{\partial\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{\partial\phi} = \sin\theta\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$$
$$\frac{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\partial\theta} = -\hat{\mathbf{r}}, \quad \frac{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\partial\phi} = \cos\theta\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$$
$$\frac{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}{\partial\phi} = -\sin\theta\hat{\mathbf{r}} - \cos\theta\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$$

Line element: $d\mathbf{r} = dr\hat{\mathbf{r}} + r\,d\theta\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} + r\sin\theta\,d\phi\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$

Metric: $ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2d\theta^2 + r^2\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2$

### Example 3: Converting Cartesian Gradient to Spherical

The gradient in Cartesian coordinates is $\nabla = \hat{\mathbf{x}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \hat{\mathbf{y}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \hat{\mathbf{z}}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}$. Using the chain rule, show that in spherical coordinates:
$$\nabla = \hat{\mathbf{r}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}$$

**Solution:** This requires applying the chain rule:
$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial r}{\partial x}\frac{\partial}{\partial r} + \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}$$

From $r^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$:
$$\frac{\partial r}{\partial x} = \frac{x}{r} = \sin\theta\cos\phi$$

From $\cos\theta = z/r$:
$$\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x} = \frac{\cos\theta\cos\phi}{r}$$

From $\tan\phi = y/x$:
$$\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x} = -\frac{\sin\phi}{r\sin\theta}$$

Substituting these (and similar expressions for $\partial/\partial y$ and $\partial/\partial z$) and using the unit vector expressions, one can verify the spherical gradient formula. The full derivation is lengthy but follows systematically from the chain rule.

**Answer:** Gradient formula verified (full derivation uses chain rule on all three Cartesian derivatives)

### Example 4: Jacobian of Spherical Coordinates

Compute the Jacobian $J$ for the transformation from Cartesian to spherical coordinates.

**Solution:** The Jacobian is:
$$J = \left|\frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(r,\theta,\phi)}\right| = \begin{vmatrix} \partial x/\partial r & \partial x/\partial\theta & \partial x/\partial\phi \\ \partial y/\partial r & \partial y/\partial\theta & \partial y/\partial\phi \\ \partial z/\partial r & \partial z/\partial\theta & \partial z/\partial\phi \end{vmatrix}$$

From $x = r\sin\theta\cos\phi$, $y = r\sin\theta\sin\phi$, $z = r\cos\theta$:
$$J = \begin{vmatrix} \sin\theta\cos\phi & r\cos\theta\cos\phi & -r\sin\theta\sin\phi \\ \sin\theta\sin\phi & r\cos\theta\sin\phi & r\sin\theta\cos\phi \\ \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta & 0 \end{vmatrix}$$

Expanding along the third row:
$$J = \cos\theta(r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta(\cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi)) + r\sin\theta(r\sin^2\theta(\cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi))$$
$$= r^2\sin\theta\cos^2\theta + r^2\sin^3\theta = r^2\sin\theta(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = r^2\sin\theta$$

This appears in volume integrals: $dV = dx\,dy\,dz = J\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi = r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi$.

**Answer:** $J = r^2\sin\theta$

### Example 5: Central Force in Spherical Coordinates

A central force $\mathbf{F} = f(r)\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ (depending only on distance) acts on a particle. Show that such a force has zero curl.

**Solution:** In spherical coordinates, the curl of a vector field $\mathbf{A} = A_r\hat{\mathbf{r}} + A_\theta\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} + A_\phi\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$ is:
$$\nabla \times \mathbf{A} = \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\left[\frac{\partial(A_\phi\sin\theta)}{\partial\theta} - \frac{\partial A_\theta}{\partial\phi}\right]\hat{\mathbf{r}} + \ldots$$

For $\mathbf{F} = f(r)\hat{\mathbf{r}}$, we have $F_\theta = F_\phi = 0$, and $F_r = f(r)$ depends only on $r$.

The $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ component of curl involves derivatives with respect to $\theta$ and $\phi$ of $F_\theta$ and $F_\phi$, which are zero.

The $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ component involves:
$$\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial F_r}{\partial\phi} - \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(rF_\phi)}{\partial r} = 0 - 0 = 0$$

The $\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$ component involves:
$$\frac{1}{r}\left[\frac{\partial(rF_\theta)}{\partial r} - \frac{\partial F_r}{\partial\theta}\right] = 0 - 0 = 0$$

Therefore $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$, confirming that central forces are conservative.

**Answer:** $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$ (central forces are conservative)

**Problem 1: A**

$\cos\theta = z/r = 6/(6\sqrt{2}) = 1/\sqrt{2}$, so $\theta = \pi/4$

$\tan\phi = y/x = 3\sqrt{3}/3 = \sqrt{3}$, so $\phi = \pi/3$

$$J = \begin{vmatrix} \cos\phi & -s\sin\phi & 0 \\ \sin\phi & s\cos\phi & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = 1(s\cos^2\phi + s\sin^2\phi) = s$$
