# Article 5.1: Simple Harmonic Motion

Simple harmonic motion represents the most fundamental oscillatory behavior in physics. When a system displaced from equilibrium experiences a restoring force proportional to displacement—Hooke's law, $F = -kx$—the resulting motion is sinusoidal with constant amplitude and frequency. SHM appears throughout physics: pendulums, springs, LC circuits, atomic vibrations, and even as small-amplitude approximations to more complex oscillations. The mathematical structure of SHM, governed by a second-order linear differential equation, provides the foundation for understanding all oscillatory phenomena.

The differential equation $\ddot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = 0$ admits solutions in multiple forms: exponential ($e^{i\omega t}$), trigonometric ($\cos\omega t$, $\sin\omega t$), or phase-amplitude form ($A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$). Each representation offers different insights: exponentials connect to complex analysis, trigonometric forms reveal periodicity directly, and phase-amplitude form encodes initial conditions geometrically. Energy in SHM oscillates between kinetic and potential forms, with total energy proportional to amplitude squared.

This article develops SHM rigorously from the differential equation, explores all solution forms and their relationships, analyzes energy exchange, introduces phase space representation, and establishes connections to physical systems. The mathematics employs ordinary differential equations, complex exponentials, trigonometric identities, and parametric curves. Applications range from mass-spring systems to pendulums to molecular vibrations.

## The Differential Equation of SHM

For a mass $m$ subject to restoring force $F = -kx$, Newton's second law gives:
$$m\ddot{x} = -kx$$

Rearranging:
$$\ddot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = 0$$

where $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$ is the **natural angular frequency**.

This is a second-order, linear, homogeneous ODE with constant coefficients.

**General solution:** The characteristic equation is:
$$r^2 + \omega_0^2 = 0 \implies r = \pm i\omega_0$$

Using complex exponentials:
$$x(t) = Ae^{i\omega_0 t} + Be^{-i\omega_0 t}$$

Or equivalently in real form:
$$x(t) = C\cos(\omega_0 t) + D\sin(\omega_0 t)$$

Or in phase-amplitude form:
$$x(t) = A\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi)$$

where $A$ is amplitude and $\phi$ is phase constant.

**Period and frequency:**
$$T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$$
$$f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{\omega_0}{2\pi} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$$

### Example 1: SHM from Initial Conditions

A mass $m = 2$ kg on a spring with $k = 8$ N/m is released from rest at position $x_0 = 0.5$ m. Find $x(t)$ and the maximum speed.

**Solution:**

Natural frequency:
$$\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{8}{2}} = 2 \text{ rad/s}$$

General solution: $x(t) = A\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi)$

**Initial conditions:**
- At $t = 0$: $x(0) = 0.5$ m
- At $t = 0$: $v(0) = \dot{x}(0) = 0$

From $x(0) = A\cos\phi = 0.5$ and $v(0) = -A\omega_0\sin\phi = 0$:

Since $v(0) = 0$: $\sin\phi = 0$, so $\phi = 0$ or $\phi = \pi$.

If $\phi = 0$: $A\cos(0) = A = 0.5$ ✓

**Solution:**
$$x(t) = 0.5\cos(2t) \text{ m}$$

**Velocity:**
$$v(t) = \dot{x}(t) = -0.5(2)\sin(2t) = -\sin(2t) \text{ m/s}$$

**Maximum speed:** $v_{\max} = A\omega_0 = 0.5(2) = 1$ m/s

**Answer:** $x(t) = 0.5\cos(2t)$ m, $v_{\max} = 1$ m/s

## Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion

**Kinetic energy:**
$$K = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{x}^2 = \frac{1}{2}m\omega_0^2 A^2\sin^2(\omega_0 t + \phi)$$

**Potential energy:**
$$U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{1}{2}k A^2\cos^2(\omega_0 t + \phi)$$

**Total energy:**
$$E = K + U = \frac{1}{2}m\omega_0^2 A^2[\sin^2(\omega_0 t + \phi) + \cos^2(\omega_0 t + \phi)]$$

Using $\omega_0^2 = k/m$ and $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$:
$$E = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 = \text{constant}$$

**Key observations:**
- Total energy is proportional to amplitude squared: $E \propto A^2$
- Energy oscillates between kinetic and potential
- Maximum kinetic energy (at $x = 0$): $K_{\max} = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 = E$
- Maximum potential energy (at $x = \pm A$): $U_{\max} = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 = E$

### Example 2: Energy Analysis

For a mass-spring system with $k = 50$ N/m and amplitude $A = 0.2$ m, find: (a) total energy, (b) position where $K = U$.

**Solution:**

**(a) Total energy:**
$$E = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 = \frac{1}{2}(50)(0.2)^2 = \frac{1}{2}(50)(0.04) = 1 \text{ J}$$

**(b) Where $K = U$:**

$$\frac{1}{2}m\dot{x}^2 = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$$

Using $E = K + U = 2K = 2U$:
$$U = \frac{E}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\text{ J}$$

$$\frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{1}{2}$$
$$x^2 = \frac{1}{k} = \frac{1}{50} = 0.02$$
$$x = \pm\sqrt{0.02} = \pm 0.141 \text{ m}$$

This is at $x = \pm A/\sqrt{2} = \pm 0.2/\sqrt{2}$.

**Answer:** (a) $E = 1$ J, (b) $x = \pm 0.141$ m (where $K = U$)

## Phase Space Representation

In **phase space**, we plot $(x, p)$ or $(x, v)$. For SHM:
$$x(t) = A\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi)$$
$$v(t) = -A\omega_0\sin(\omega_0 t + \phi)$$

Eliminating $t$:
$$\frac{x^2}{A^2} + \frac{v^2}{(A\omega_0)^2} = \cos^2(\omega_0 t + \phi) + \sin^2(\omega_0 t + \phi) = 1$$

This is an **ellipse** in phase space with semi-axes $A$ (position) and $A\omega_0$ (velocity).

**Properties:**
- Each energy level corresponds to one ellipse
- Motion is clockwise (for standard conventions)
- Larger amplitude → larger ellipse
- Period is time to complete one loop

### Example 3: Phase Space Trajectory

For $x(t) = 0.3\cos(4t)$ m, sketch the phase space trajectory and find the area enclosed.

**Solution:**

From $x(t) = 0.3\cos(4t)$: $A = 0.3$ m, $\omega_0 = 4$ rad/s

Velocity: $v(t) = -0.3(4)\sin(4t) = -1.2\sin(4t)$ m/s

Phase space ellipse:
$$\frac{x^2}{(0.3)^2} + \frac{v^2}{(1.2)^2} = 1$$

Semi-axes: $a = 0.3$ m, $b = 1.2$ m/s

Area enclosed:
$$\mathcal{A} = \pi ab = \pi(0.3)(1.2) = 0.36\pi \text{ m}^2\text{/s}$$

**Relation to energy:** 
$$E = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 = \frac{1}{2}m\omega_0^2 A^2$$

The area in phase space $(x, mv)$ is:
$$\mathcal{A}_{xp} = \pi A \cdot (mA\omega_0) = \pi m A^2\omega_0 = 2\pi E/\omega_0$$

**Answer:** Ellipse with semi-axes $0.3$ m and $1.2$ m/s, area $= 0.36\pi$ m²/s

## Complex Exponential Representation

Using Euler's formula: $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$

Write solution as:
$$x(t) = \text{Re}[Ae^{i(\omega_0 t + \phi)}] = \text{Re}[\tilde{A}e^{i\omega_0 t}]$$

where $\tilde{A} = Ae^{i\phi}$ is a complex amplitude.

**Advantages:**
- Simplifies calculations with derivatives (just multiply by $i\omega_0$)
- Generalizes to driven/damped oscillators
- Connects to Fourier analysis

**Velocity and acceleration:**
$$\dot{x}(t) = \text{Re}[i\omega_0\tilde{A}e^{i\omega_0 t}]$$
$$\ddot{x}(t) = \text{Re}[-\omega_0^2\tilde{A}e^{i\omega_0 t}]$$

### Example 4: Using Complex Notation

Solve $\ddot{x} + 9x = 0$ with $x(0) = 2$ m, $\dot{x}(0) = 3$ m/s using complex exponentials.

**Solution:**

If $x = a\cos\omega t - b\sin\omega t$ with $a = 2$, $b = -1$:
$$x = 2\cos 3t - (-1)\sin 3t = 2\cos 3t + \sin 3t$$

To convert: $x = A\cos(3t + \phi)$ where
$$A\cos\phi = 2, \quad -A\sin\phi = 1$$

$$A = \sqrt{2^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{5}$$
$$\phi = -\arctan(1/2) \approx -26.6°$$

**Answer:** $x(t) = 2\cos 3t + \sin 3t = \sqrt{5}\cos(3t - 26.6°)$ m

## Applications to Physical Systems

### Simple Pendulum (Small Angles)

For pendulum of length $L$, angle $\theta$ from vertical:
$$\ddot{\theta} + \frac{g}{L}\theta = 0$$

(Small angle approximation: $\sin\theta \approx \theta$)

Angular frequency: $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/L}$

Period: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ (independent of mass!)

### LC Circuit

For inductor $L$ and capacitor $C$ in series:
$$L\ddot{Q} + \frac{Q}{C} = 0$$

where $Q$ is charge. This gives:
$$\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$$

### Example 5: Physical Pendulum

A uniform rod of length $L = 1$ m and mass $m = 2$ kg pivots about one end. Find the period of small oscillations.

**Solution:**

Moment of inertia about pivot: $I = \frac{1}{3}mL^2 = \frac{1}{3}(2)(1)^2 = \frac{2}{3}$ kg·m²

Torque (small angles): $\tau = -mg(L/2)\sin\theta \approx -mg(L/2)\theta$

Equation of motion:
$$I\ddot{\theta} = -mg\frac{L}{2}\theta$$
$$\ddot{\theta} + \frac{mgL}{2I}\theta = 0$$

$$\omega_0^2 = \frac{mgL}{2I} = \frac{2(9.8)(1)}{2(2/3)} = \frac{19.6}{4/3} = 14.7 \text{ rad}^2\text{/s}^2$$

$$\omega_0 = 3.83 \text{ rad/s}$$

$$T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0} = \frac{2\pi}{3.83} = 1.64 \text{ s}$$

Compare to simple pendulum: $T_{\text{simple}} = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g} = 2\pi\sqrt{1/9.8} = 2.01$ s

**Answer:** $T = 1.64$ s (faster than simple pendulum)
