# Article 1.1: Review of Vector Calculus

Classical mechanics reaches its full mathematical power when we express physical laws in the language of vectors and vector calculus. While introductory physics introduces vectors primarily as arrows with magnitude and direction, upper-division mechanics demands facility with vector derivatives, integrals, and differential operators. The position of a particle becomes not just a point in space, but a vector function $\mathbf{r}(t)$ whose derivatives give velocity and acceleration. Forces, momenta, and angular momenta all become vector fields that we manipulate with the full machinery of multivariable calculus.

The transition from scalar to vector thinking is essential because physical laws are fundamentally geometric—they describe relationships between quantities that exist independently of our choice of coordinate system. Newton's second law $\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$ holds in any coordinate system, but expressing the acceleration vector $\mathbf{a}$ in spherical coordinates, for instance, requires careful application of vector calculus. Similarly, determining whether a force is conservative requires checking if its curl vanishes, connecting the physics of energy conservation to the topology of vector fields.

This article establishes the vector calculus foundation for classical mechanics. We review vector operations, develop time derivatives of vector functions, introduce the gradient, divergence, and curl operators, and explore vector integration. Throughout, we emphasize physical interpretations and applications to mechanical systems. Mastery of these mathematical tools is essential for the analytical methods—Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics—that form the capstone of classical mechanics.

## Vector Operations and Notation

In classical mechanics, we use boldface notation $\mathbf{v}$ for vectors and unit vectors like $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$, $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$, $\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ (or sometimes $\hat{\mathbf{i}}$, $\hat{\mathbf{j}}$, $\hat{\mathbf{k}}$). A vector in three dimensions can be written as:
$$\mathbf{v} = v_x\hat{\mathbf{x}} + v_y\hat{\mathbf{y}} + v_z\hat{\mathbf{z}}$$

The dot product (scalar product) of two vectors is:
$$\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z = |\mathbf{A}||\mathbf{B}|\cos\theta$$

This relates directly to work: $W = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d}$.

The cross product (vector product) produces a vector perpendicular to both inputs:
$$\mathbf{A} \times \mathbf{B} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{x}} & \hat{\mathbf{y}} & \hat{\mathbf{z}} \\ A_x & A_y & A_z \\ B_x & B_y & B_z \end{vmatrix}$$

The magnitude is $|\mathbf{A} \times \mathbf{B}| = |\mathbf{A}||\mathbf{B}|\sin\theta$, and the direction follows the right-hand rule. This appears in torque $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}$ and angular momentum $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}$.

### Example 1: Computing Torque

A force $\mathbf{F} = 3\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 2\hat{\mathbf{y}} - \hat{\mathbf{z}}$ (in Newtons) acts at position $\mathbf{r} = 2\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \hat{\mathbf{y}} + 3\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ (in meters). Find the torque about the origin.

**Solution:** The torque is $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}$:
$$\boldsymbol{\tau} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{x}} & \hat{\mathbf{y}} & \hat{\mathbf{z}} \\ 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 & -1 \end{vmatrix}$$

$$= \hat{\mathbf{x}}[(1)(-1) - (3)(2)] - \hat{\mathbf{y}}[(2)(-1) - (3)(3)] + \hat{\mathbf{z}}[(2)(2) - (1)(3)]$$

$$= \hat{\mathbf{x}}(-1 - 6) - \hat{\mathbf{y}}(-2 - 9) + \hat{\mathbf{z}}(4 - 3)$$

$$= -7\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 11\hat{\mathbf{y}} + \hat{\mathbf{z}}$$

The magnitude is $|\boldsymbol{\tau}| = \sqrt{49 + 121 + 1} = \sqrt{171} \approx 13.1$ N·m.

**Answer:** $\boldsymbol{\tau} = -7\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 11\hat{\mathbf{y}} + \hat{\mathbf{z}}$ N·m

## Time Derivatives of Vectors

In mechanics, vectors often depend on time: position $\mathbf{r}(t)$, velocity $\mathbf{v}(t) = d\mathbf{r}/dt$, acceleration $\mathbf{a}(t) = d\mathbf{v}/dt$. When the basis vectors are fixed (as in Cartesian coordinates), differentiation is straightforward:
$$\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = \frac{dv_x}{dt}\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \frac{dv_y}{dt}\hat{\mathbf{y}} + \frac{dv_z}{dt}\hat{\mathbf{z}}$$

However, in curvilinear coordinates where basis vectors themselves rotate (like polar coordinates), we must account for $d\hat{\mathbf{r}}/dt \neq 0$. This leads to additional terms in velocity and acceleration.

Product rules apply:
$$\frac{d}{dt}(\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}) = \frac{d\mathbf{A}}{dt} \cdot \mathbf{B} + \mathbf{A} \cdot \frac{d\mathbf{B}}{dt}$$

$$\frac{d}{dt}(\mathbf{A} \times \mathbf{B}) = \frac{d\mathbf{A}}{dt} \times \mathbf{B} + \mathbf{A} \times \frac{d\mathbf{B}}{dt}$$

### Example 2: Angular Momentum of a Particle in Circular Motion

A particle of mass $m = 2$ kg moves in a circle of radius $R = 3$ m with constant angular velocity $\omega = 4$ rad/s. The position is $\mathbf{r}(t) = R\cos(\omega t)\hat{\mathbf{x}} + R\sin(\omega t)\hat{\mathbf{y}}$. Find the angular momentum $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}$ and verify it's constant.

**Solution:** First find velocity:
$$\mathbf{v} = \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} = -R\omega\sin(\omega t)\hat{\mathbf{x}} + R\omega\cos(\omega t)\hat{\mathbf{y}}$$

Momentum: $\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v} = -2R\omega\sin(\omega t)\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 2R\omega\cos(\omega t)\hat{\mathbf{y}}$

Angular momentum:
$$\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{x}} & \hat{\mathbf{y}} & \hat{\mathbf{z}} \\ R\cos(\omega t) & R\sin(\omega t) & 0 \\ -2R\omega\sin(\omega t) & 2R\omega\cos(\omega t) & 0 \end{vmatrix}$$

$$= \hat{\mathbf{z}}[R\cos(\omega t) \cdot 2R\omega\cos(\omega t) - R\sin(\omega t) \cdot (-2R\omega\sin(\omega t))]$$

$$= \hat{\mathbf{z}}[2R^2\omega\cos^2(\omega t) + 2R^2\omega\sin^2(\omega t)]$$

$$= 2R^2\omega\hat{\mathbf{z}} = 2(9)(4)\hat{\mathbf{z}} = 72\hat{\mathbf{z}}$$

Since $\mathbf{L}$ doesn't depend on $t$, it's constant—as expected for circular motion with no external torque.

**Answer:** $\mathbf{L} = 72\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ kg·m²/s, constant in time

## Gradient, Divergence, and Curl

The gradient of a scalar field $\phi(\mathbf{r})$ is a vector field:
$$\nabla\phi = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}\hat{\mathbf{y}} + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}\hat{\mathbf{z}}$$

In mechanics, $\nabla\phi$ points in the direction of maximum increase of $\phi$. For potential energy $U(\mathbf{r})$, the force is $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U$.

The divergence of a vector field $\mathbf{v}$ is a scalar:
$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = \frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z}$$

This measures the "source strength" at each point. For incompressible fluids, $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$.

The curl of a vector field is a vector:
$$\nabla \times \mathbf{v} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{x}} & \hat{\mathbf{y}} & \hat{\mathbf{z}} \\ \partial/\partial x & \partial/\partial y & \partial/\partial z \\ v_x & v_y & v_z \end{vmatrix}$$

For a force $\mathbf{F}$, if $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$, then $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative and can be written as $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U$.

### Example 3: Testing if a Force is Conservative

Consider the force $\mathbf{F} = (2xy + z^2)\hat{\mathbf{x}} + x^2\hat{\mathbf{y}} + 2xz\hat{\mathbf{z}}$. Is this force conservative? If so, find the potential energy $U$.

**Solution:** A force is conservative if $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$:
$$\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{x}} & \hat{\mathbf{y}} & \hat{\mathbf{z}} \\ \partial/\partial x & \partial/\partial y & \partial/\partial z \\ 2xy+z^2 & x^2 & 2xz \end{vmatrix}$$

$$= \hat{\mathbf{x}}\left[\frac{\partial(2xz)}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial(x^2)}{\partial z}\right] - \hat{\mathbf{y}}\left[\frac{\partial(2xz)}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial(2xy+z^2)}{\partial z}\right] + \hat{\mathbf{z}}\left[\frac{\partial(x^2)}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial(2xy+z^2)}{\partial y}\right]$$

$$= \hat{\mathbf{x}}[0 - 0] - \hat{\mathbf{y}}[2z - 2z] + \hat{\mathbf{z}}[2x - 2x] = \mathbf{0}$$

The force is conservative! To find $U$, we use $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U$:

From $F_x = -\partial U/\partial x = 2xy + z^2$, integrate: $U = -x^2y - xz^2 + g(y,z)$

From $F_y = -\partial U/\partial y = x^2$, we get $\partial U/\partial y = -x^2$, so $g(y,z) = h(z)$

From $F_z = -\partial U/\partial z = 2xz$, we get $\partial U/\partial z = -2xz$, consistent with $U = -x^2y - xz^2 + C$

**Answer:** Yes, conservative. $U(x,y,z) = -x^2y - xz^2 + C$

## Vector Integration

Line integrals compute work: $W = \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$. If $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative, this integral is path-independent and equals $U(\mathbf{r}_i) - U(\mathbf{r}_f)$.

Surface integrals compute flux: $\Phi = \int_S \mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} \, dA$.

Volume integrals appear in calculating center of mass, moments of inertia, etc.

The fundamental theorem for gradients states:
$$\int_C \nabla\phi \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \phi(\mathbf{r}_f) - \phi(\mathbf{r}_i)$$

This makes evaluating conservative force work trivial—just compute the potential difference.

### Example 4: Work Done by a Conservative Force

Using the potential $U = -x^2y - xz^2$ from Example 3, find the work done moving a particle from $(1, 1, 1)$ to $(2, 2, 0)$.

**Solution:** For conservative forces, $W = -\Delta U = U(\mathbf{r}_i) - U(\mathbf{r}_f)$:

At $(1, 1, 1)$: $U_i = -(1)^2(1) - (1)(1)^2 = -1 - 1 = -2$

At $(2, 2, 0)$: $U_f = -(2)^2(2) - (2)(0)^2 = -8 - 0 = -8$

Work done: $W = U_i - U_f = -2 - (-8) = 6$ J

The work is positive, meaning the force does positive work on the particle.

**Answer:** $W = 6$ J

### Example 5: Application of the Divergence Theorem

For a radial vector field $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{r}/r^3$ (where $r = |\mathbf{r}|$), compute the flux through a sphere of radius $R$ centered at the origin using both direct integration and the divergence theorem.

**Solution:** 

**Method 1 (Direct):** On the sphere, $\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \hat{\mathbf{r}}$ and $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{r}/R^3$, so:
$$\Phi = \int_S \mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} \, dA = \int_S \frac{\mathbf{r}}{R^3} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}} \, dA = \int_S \frac{R}{R^3} \, dA = \frac{1}{R^2} \cdot 4\pi R^2 = 4\pi$$

**Method 2 (Divergence theorem):** First compute $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}$. For $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{r}/r^3 = (x\hat{\mathbf{x}} + y\hat{\mathbf{y}} + z\hat{\mathbf{z}})/(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}$:

This calculation is tedious...

The resolution is that $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}$ has a delta function singularity at the origin: $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 4\pi\delta^3(\mathbf{r})$. The volume integral gives $4\pi$.

**Answer:** $\Phi = 4\pi$ (both methods agree when singularity is properly treated)
