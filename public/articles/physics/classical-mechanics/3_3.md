# Article 3.3: Conservation of Energy

Energy conservation stands as one of the most powerful principles in physics. When all forces are conservative, the total mechanical energy $E = K + U$ remains constant throughout motion. This single scalar equation often replaces complex vector differential equations, reducing dynamics problems to algebra. The conserved energy determines which regions of space are accessible and which are forbidden, constraining trajectories without solving for them explicitly.

The graphical representation of energy—plotting potential energy versus position—provides immediate physical insight. Turning points occur where $E = U$, separating accessible from forbidden regions. Equilibrium points appear as extrema of $U$, with stability determined by the second derivative. Near stable equilibria, particles undergo small oscillations with frequency determined by the curvature $U''(x_0)$. These graphical and analytical techniques extend to multi-dimensional systems through effective potentials and phase space representations.

This article develops energy conservation rigorously from conservative forces, explores energy diagrams and their physical interpretation, and establishes stability criteria for equilibrium. We introduce phase space as a geometric framework for visualizing dynamics, connecting trajectories in configuration space to curves in phase space. The mathematics—finding extrema, analyzing second derivatives, sketching phase portraits—translates directly into physical predictions about bound states, escape velocities, and oscillation frequencies.

## Conservation of Mechanical Energy

**Theorem:** If all forces acting on a particle are conservative, the total mechanical energy is conserved:
$$E = K + U = \text{constant}$$

**Proof:** For conservative force $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U$:
$$\frac{dK}{dt} = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v} = -\nabla U \cdot \mathbf{v} = -\frac{dU}{dt}$$

(using chain rule: $dU/dt = \nabla U \cdot d\mathbf{r}/dt = \nabla U \cdot \mathbf{v}$)

Therefore:
$$\frac{d}{dt}(K + U) = \frac{dK}{dt} + \frac{dU}{dt} = 0$$

Thus $K + U = E = $ constant ∎

**Alternative derivation from work-energy theorem:**

Work by conservative force:
$$W = \int \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = -\int \nabla U \cdot d\mathbf{r} = -\Delta U = U_i - U_f$$

By work-energy theorem: $W = \Delta K = K_f - K_i$

Therefore:
$$K_f - K_i = U_i - U_f$$
$$K_f + U_f = K_i + U_i$$

### Example 1: Projectile Energy Conservation

A ball of mass $m = 0.5$ kg is thrown upward with $v_0 = 15$ m/s from height $h_0 = 2$ m. Find maximum height using energy conservation.

**Solution:** At initial point:
$$E = K_0 + U_0 = \frac{1}{2}mv_0^2 + mgh_0 = \frac{1}{2}(0.5)(15)^2 + (0.5)(9.8)(2)$$
$$= 56.25 + 9.8 = 66.05 \text{ J}$$

At maximum height $h_{\max}$: $v = 0$, so $K = 0$
$$E = 0 + mgh_{\max} = (0.5)(9.8)h_{\max}$$

Conservation: $66.05 = 4.9h_{\max}$
$$h_{\max} = \frac{66.05}{4.9} = 13.5 \text{ m}$$

**Answer:** $h_{\max} = 13.5$ m

## Energy Diagrams and Turning Points

For one-dimensional motion with $E = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{x}^2 + U(x)$:

**Turning points:** Where $K = 0$, so $E = U(x)$. These are boundaries of accessible regions.

**Forbidden regions:** Where $U(x) > E$ (would require $K < 0$)

**Accessible regions:** Where $U(x) \leq E$

From energy equation:
$$\dot{x} = \pm\sqrt{\frac{2(E - U(x))}{m}}$$

Velocity is zero at turning points, maximum where $U$ is minimum.

### Example 2: Particle in Potential Well

A particle of mass $m = 1$ kg has potential energy $U(x) = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$ with $k = 4$ N/m. If total energy $E = 2$ J, find turning points and maximum speed.

**Solution:** Turning points where $E = U(x)$:
$$2 = \frac{1}{2}(4)x^2 = 2x^2$$
$$x^2 = 1 \implies x = \pm 1 \text{ m}$$

Maximum speed at $x = 0$ (minimum potential):
$$E = K_{\max} + U(0) = \frac{1}{2}mv_{\max}^2 + 0$$
$$2 = \frac{1}{2}(1)v_{\max}^2$$
$$v_{\max} = 2 \text{ m/s}$$

**Answer:** Turning points at $x = \pm 1$ m, $v_{\max} = 2$ m/s

## Equilibrium and Stability Analysis

**Equilibrium points** satisfy $F = -dU/dx = 0$, i.e., $dU/dx = 0$ (extrema of $U$).

**Stability analysis** uses the second derivative:
$$F = -\frac{dU}{dx}, \quad \frac{dF}{dx} = -\frac{d^2U}{dx^2}$$

At equilibrium $x_0$:
- **Stable equilibrium:** $d^2U/dx^2|_{x_0} > 0$ (local minimum of $U$)
  - Small displacement → restoring force
- **Unstable equilibrium:** $d^2U/dx^2|_{x_0} < 0$ (local maximum of $U$)
  - Small displacement → force amplifies displacement
- **Neutral equilibrium:** $d^2U/dx^2|_{x_0} = 0$ (inflection point)
  - Higher derivatives needed for classification

### Example 3: Stability of Equilibria

For potential $U(x) = x^4 - 4x^2 + 3$, find and classify all equilibrium points.

**Solution:** Equilibria where $dU/dx = 0$:
$$\frac{dU}{dx} = 4x^3 - 8x = 4x(x^2 - 2) = 0$$
$$x = 0, \quad x = \pm\sqrt{2}$$

Second derivative test:
$$\frac{d^2U}{dx^2} = 12x^2 - 8$$

At $x = 0$: $\frac{d^2U}{dx^2} = -8 < 0$ → **unstable** (local maximum)

At $x = \sqrt{2}$: $\frac{d^2U}{dx^2} = 12(2) - 8 = 16 > 0$ → **stable** (local minimum)

At $x = -\sqrt{2}$: $\frac{d^2U}{dx^2} = 16 > 0$ → **stable** (local minimum)

**Answer:** Unstable equilibrium at $x = 0$; stable equilibria at $x = \pm\sqrt{2}$

## Small Oscillations About Equilibrium

Near a stable equilibrium $x_0$, expand potential in Taylor series:
$$U(x) = U(x_0) + \frac{1}{2}U''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2 + \cdots$$

(First derivative vanishes at equilibrium)

Define $\xi = x - x_0$ (displacement from equilibrium). For small $\xi$:
$$U \approx U(x_0) + \frac{1}{2}k_{\text{eff}}\xi^2$$

where $k_{\text{eff}} = U''(x_0) > 0$ is the effective spring constant.

Equation of motion:
$$m\ddot{\xi} = -\frac{dU}{d\xi} = -k_{\text{eff}}\xi$$

This is simple harmonic motion with frequency:
$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k_{\text{eff}}}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{U''(x_0)}{m}}$$

### Example 4: Frequency of Small Oscillations

For potential $U(x) = x^4 - 4x^2$ and mass $m = 2$ kg, find oscillation frequency about stable equilibrium.

**Solution:** From Example 3, stable equilibria at $x_0 = \pm\sqrt{2}$.

At $x_0 = \sqrt{2}$:
$$U''(\sqrt{2}) = 12(\sqrt{2})^2 - 8 = 24 - 8 = 16 \text{ N/m}$$

Angular frequency:
$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{U''(x_0)}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{16}{2}} = \sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2} \text{ rad/s}$$

Frequency:
$$f = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{2\pi} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi} \approx 0.45 \text{ Hz}$$

Period:
$$T = \frac{1}{f} = \frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 2.22 \text{ s}$$

**Answer:** $\omega = 2\sqrt{2}$ rad/s, $f \approx 0.45$ Hz

## Phase Space Representation

**Phase space** is the $(x, p)$ plane where $p = m\dot{x}$ is momentum. Each point represents a state of the system.

For one-dimensional motion, energy conservation gives:
$$E = \frac{p^2}{2m} + U(x) = \text{constant}$$

Solving for $p$:
$$p = \pm\sqrt{2m(E - U(x))}$$

This defines curves in phase space called **phase trajectories** or **orbits**.

**Properties:**
- Constant energy → trajectory is closed curve (for bound motion)
- Higher energy → larger curve
- Trajectories never cross (uniqueness of solutions)
- Direction: clockwise for standard conventions

### Example 5: Phase Space for Harmonic Oscillator

Sketch phase space trajectories for $U(x) = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$.

**Solution:** Energy: $E = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}kx^2$

Rearranging:
$$\frac{p^2}{2mE} + \frac{kx^2}{2E} = 1$$

This is an ellipse:
$$\frac{x^2}{(2E/k)} + \frac{p^2}{(2mE)} = 1$$

Semi-axes:
- $x$-axis: $a = \sqrt{2E/k}$ (amplitude)
- $p$-axis: $b = \sqrt{2mE}$ (maximum momentum)

Different energies give concentric ellipses. Higher $E$ → larger ellipse.

The motion is periodic with period $T = 2\pi/\omega = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$.

**Answer:** Concentric ellipses centered at origin; higher energy = larger ellipse
