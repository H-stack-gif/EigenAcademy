# Article 3.5: Central Forces

Central forces—forces that point toward or away from a fixed center and depend only on distance—occupy a special place in physics. Gravity and electrostatic forces are central, making them fundamental to planetary motion, atomic structure, and countless other phenomena. The mathematical beauty of central force problems lies in their reducibility: three-dimensional motion separates into planar orbital motion plus trivial perpendicular motion. Angular momentum conservation further reduces the problem to one-dimensional radial motion in an effective potential.

The effective potential $U_{eff}(r) = U(r) + L^2/(2\mu r^2)$ combines the actual potential with a centrifugal barrier term. This single function determines all qualitative features of the orbit: bound vs. unbound motion, circular orbits and their stability, and the radial turning points. Energy diagrams in the effective potential reveal orbit types at a glance. The interplay between attractive force and angular momentum creates the rich phenomenology of orbital mechanics—ellipses, hyperbolas, precession, and scattering.

This article develops the central force problem with mathematical rigor. We derive the effective potential, classify orbits by energy and angular momentum, analyze stability of circular orbits, and establish the framework for Kepler's laws (to be proven in Unit 6). The techniques here—reducing dimensions through symmetry, analyzing effective potentials, finding stable configurations—exemplify the power of conservation laws and energy methods in classical mechanics.

## Definition and Properties of Central Forces

A **central force** has the form:
$$\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}) = f(r)\hat{\mathbf{r}}$$

where $f(r)$ depends only on distance $r = |\mathbf{r}|$ from a fixed center (origin), and $\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{r}/r$ is the radial unit vector.

**Properties:**
1. **Conservative:** Central forces have zero curl (shown in Unit 1), so $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U$ with potential $U(r)$ depending only on $r$.

2. **Angular momentum conservation:** Torque about origin: $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{r} \times (f(r)\hat{\mathbf{r}}) = f(r)\mathbf{r} \times \hat{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{0}$

   Since $\boldsymbol{\tau} = d\mathbf{L}/dt = 0$, angular momentum $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}$ is conserved.

3. **Planar motion:** Since $\mathbf{L} = $ constant, and $\mathbf{L} \perp \mathbf{r}$ always, motion is confined to plane perpendicular to $\mathbf{L}$.

### Example 1: Verifying Central Force Properties

For inverse-square force $\mathbf{F} = -k/r^2 \hat{\mathbf{r}}$, verify angular momentum conservation and find the potential.

**Solution:** 

**Angular momentum:**
$$\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times m\mathbf{v}$$

$$\frac{d\mathbf{L}}{dt} = \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} \times m\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{r} \times m\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}$$
$$= \mathbf{v} \times m\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0} + \mathbf{r} \times \left(-\frac{k}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}\right) = \mathbf{0}$$

Thus $\mathbf{L}$ is conserved.

**Potential:**
$$U(r) = -\int F_r dr = -\int \left(-\frac{k}{r^2}\right) dr = k\int \frac{dr}{r^2} = -\frac{k}{r} + C$$

Taking $U(\infty) = 0$: $U(r) = -k/r$.

**Answer:** $\mathbf{L}$ conserved; $U(r) = -k/r$

## Reduction to One Dimension: The Effective Potential

For motion in the plane perpendicular to $\mathbf{L}$, use polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$.

**Angular momentum:** $L = mr^2\dot{\theta}$ (constant)

**Energy:**
$$E = \frac{1}{2}m(\dot{r}^2 + r^2\dot{\theta}^2) + U(r)$$

Substitute $\dot{\theta} = L/(mr^2)$:
$$E = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{r}^2 + \frac{L^2}{2mr^2} + U(r)$$

Define **effective potential:**
$$U_{eff}(r) = U(r) + \frac{L^2}{2mr^2}$$

Then:
$$E = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{r}^2 + U_{eff}(r)$$

This is effectively one-dimensional radial motion in potential $U_{eff}(r)$.

The term $L^2/(2mr^2)$ is the **centrifugal barrier**—it represents the kinetic energy of angular motion and prevents the particle from reaching $r = 0$ (unless $L = 0$).

### Example 2: Effective Potential for Inverse-Square Force

For gravitational force with $U(r) = -k/r$ and angular momentum $L$, plot and analyze $U_{eff}(r)$.

**Solution:**
$$U_{eff}(r) = -\frac{k}{r} + \frac{L^2}{2mr^2}$$

**Limiting behavior:**
- As $r \to 0$: $U_{eff} \to +\infty$ (centrifugal barrier dominates)
- As $r \to \infty$: $U_{eff} \to 0$ (both terms vanish)

**Extrema:** Find $dU_{eff}/dr = 0$:
$$\frac{dU_{eff}}{dr} = \frac{k}{r^2} - \frac{L^2}{mr^3} = 0$$
$$\frac{k}{r^2} = \frac{L^2}{mr^3}$$
$$r_0 = \frac{L^2}{mk}$$

At this radius: $U_{eff}(r_0) = -\frac{k}{L^2/(mk)} + \frac{L^2}{2m(L^2/(mk))^2} = -\frac{mk^2}{L^2} + \frac{mk^2}{2L^2} = -\frac{mk^2}{2L^2}$

Second derivative:
$$\frac{d^2U_{eff}}{dr^2} = -\frac{2k}{r^3} + \frac{3L^2}{mr^4}$$

At $r = r_0$: 
$$\frac{d^2U_{eff}}{dr^2}\Big|_{r_0} = -\frac{2k}{r_0^3} + \frac{3L^2}{mr_0^4} = \frac{L^2}{mr_0^4}(3 - 2r_0) = \frac{mk^3}{L^4} > 0$$

So $r_0$ is a **minimum**—stable circular orbit possible.

**Answer:** Minimum at $r_0 = L^2/(mk)$ with $U_{eff,min} = -mk^2/(2L^2)$

## Orbit Classification by Energy

For effective potential $U_{eff}(r)$ with minimum at $r_0$:

1. **$E < U_{eff,min}$:** No motion possible (classically forbidden)

2. **$E = U_{eff,min}$:** Circular orbit at $r = r_0$

3. **$U_{eff,min} < E < 0$:** Bound orbit with $r_{min} \leq r \leq r_{max}$ (ellipse)

4. **$E = 0$:** Parabolic orbit (just barely unbound)

5. **$E > 0$:** Unbound orbit (hyperbola)

The turning points $r_{min}$ and $r_{max}$ satisfy $E = U_{eff}(r)$.

### Example 3: Orbit Classification

For $U = -k/r$ with $k = 10$ J} \cdot \text {m, $m = 1$ kg, and $L = 3$ kg} \cdot \text {m²/s, classify orbits for: (a) $E = -10$ J, (b) $E = -1$ J, (c) $E = +5$ J.

**Solution:** 

For attractive force ($U = -k/r$ with $k > 0$), $U < 0$ everywhere. The effective potential is:
$$U_{eff} = -\frac{k}{r} + \frac{L^2}{2mr^2}$$

As $r \to \infty$: $U_{eff} \to 0$ from below.
As $r \to 0$: $U_{eff} \to +\infty$ (centrifugal barrier).

So $U_{eff}$ has a minimum at some $r_0$ with $U_{eff,min} < 0$.

For $E < U_{eff,min}$: motion impossible.
For $E > 0$: unbound (since $U_{eff} \to 0$ as $r \to \infty$).
For $U_{eff,min} < E < 0$: bound orbit.

So:

**(a) $E = -10$ J $< -5.56$ J:** Motion impossible (need more energy)

**(b) $E = -1$ J:** $-5.56 < -1 < 0$ → Bound elliptical orbit

**(c) $E = +5$ J $> 0$:** Unbound hyperbolic orbit

**Answer:** (a) Forbidden, (b) Bound ellipse, (c) Unbound hyperbola

## Circular Orbits and Stability

**Circular orbit condition:** $r = r_0$ constant requires $\dot{r} = 0$ and $\ddot{r} = 0$.

From $E = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{r}^2 + U_{eff}(r)$ with $\dot{r} = 0$:
$$E = U_{eff}(r_0)$$

From equation of motion $m\ddot{r} = -dU_{eff}/dr$, equilibrium requires:
$$\frac{dU_{eff}}{dr}\Big|_{r_0} = 0$$

This determines $r_0$ for given $L$.

**Stability:** Small perturbation $r = r_0 + \xi$ with $|\xi| \ll r_0$:
$$U_{eff}(r_0 + \xi) \approx U_{eff}(r_0) + \frac{1}{2}U_{eff}''(r_0)\xi^2$$

Effective spring constant: $k_{eff} = U_{eff}''(r_0)$

If $k_{eff} > 0$: stable circular orbit (small oscillations about $r_0$)

If $k_{eff} < 0$: unstable (perturbations grow)

### Example 4: Stability of Circular Orbit

For $U = -k/r$, determine stability of circular orbit at $r_0 = L^2/(mk)$.

**Solution:** From Example 2:
$$\frac{d^2U_{eff}}{dr^2}\Big|_{r_0} = \frac{mk^3}{L^4} > 0$$

Since second derivative is positive, circular orbit is **stable**.

For inverse-square force, it can be shown that $\omega_r = \omega_\theta$ exactly, so orbits close (ellipses), not precessing rosettes.

**Answer:** Stable; $\omega_r = \omega_\theta$ (closed orbits)

### Example 5: Energy of Circular Orbit

For circular orbit at radius $r_0$ in potential $U = -k/r$, express total energy in terms of $r_0$.

**Solution:** At circular orbit:

Centripetal force equals gravitational force:
$$m\frac{v^2}{r_0} = \frac{k}{r_0^2}$$
$$v^2 = \frac{k}{mr_0}$$

Kinetic energy:
$$K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2}m \cdot \frac{k}{mr_0} = \frac{k}{2r_0}$$

Potential energy:
$$U = -\frac{k}{r_0}$$

Total energy:
$$E = K + U = \frac{k}{2r_0} - \frac{k}{r_0} = -\frac{k}{2r_0}$$

**Virial theorem relation:** $E = U/2 = -K$

**Answer:** $E = -k/(2r_0)$ (half the potential energy)
