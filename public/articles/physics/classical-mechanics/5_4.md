# Article 5.4: Coupled Oscillators

When two or more oscillators interact through coupling forces, the resulting system exhibits collective behavior more complex than independent oscillations. Coupled oscillators can exchange energy periodically, exhibit beats when weakly coupled, or synchronize in normal modes when strongly coupled. The mathematical description requires matrices: the equations of motion form a system of coupled differential equations naturally expressed in matrix form. Finding normal modes reduces to an eigenvalue problem—diagonalizing the system matrix transforms coupled equations into independent oscillator equations.

Normal modes represent collective oscillations where all parts of the system move with the same frequency and fixed phase relationships. Each normal mode has a characteristic frequency (eigenvalue) and spatial pattern (eigenvector). The modes are orthogonal, forming a complete basis for describing arbitrary motion. Any general motion decomposes into a superposition of normal modes, each evolving independently at its own frequency. This modal analysis powerfully extends from simple coupled pendulums to molecules, bridges, circuits, and lattice vibrations.

This article develops coupled oscillators with emphasis on linear algebra: writing equations in matrix form, finding eigenvalues and eigenvectors, diagonalizing the system, proving orthogonality, and interpreting normal modes physically. We work through two-oscillator and three-oscillator examples in detail, establishing the general formalism applicable to $N$ coupled oscillators. The mathematics employs matrix algebra, eigenvalue problems, orthogonality relations, and coordinate transformations.

## Two Coupled Oscillators

Consider two masses $m_1$, $m_2$ connected by springs with constants $k_1$, $k_2$, $k_3$:
- Mass $m_1$ connected to wall by spring $k_1$
- Coupling spring $k_2$ between masses
- Mass $m_2$ connected to wall by spring $k_3$

**Equations of motion:**
$$m_1\ddot{x}_1 = -k_1 x_1 - k_2(x_1 - x_2)$$
$$m_2\ddot{x}_2 = -k_3 x_2 - k_2(x_2 - x_1)$$

Rearranging:
$$m_1\ddot{x}_1 = -(k_1 + k_2)x_1 + k_2 x_2$$
$$m_2\ddot{x}_2 = k_2 x_1 - (k_2 + k_3)x_2$$

## Matrix Formulation

Define **position vector:** $\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix}$

Define **mass matrix:** $\mathbf{M} = \begin{pmatrix} m_1 & 0 \\ 0 & m_2 \end{pmatrix}$

Define **stiffness matrix:** $\mathbf{K} = \begin{pmatrix} k_1+k_2 & -k_2 \\ -k_2 & k_2+k_3 \end{pmatrix}$

**Matrix equation:**
$$\mathbf{M}\ddot{\mathbf{x}} = -\mathbf{K}\mathbf{x}$$

or equivalently:
$$\ddot{\mathbf{x}} = -\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{x} = -\mathbf{\Omega}^2\mathbf{x}$$

where $\mathbf{\Omega}^2 = \mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{K}$ is the **dynamical matrix**.

### Example 1: Setting Up Matrix Equation

Two identical masses $m$ with springs: $k_1 = k$, $k_2 = 2k$, $k_3 = k$. Write the matrix equation.

**Solution:**

**Mass matrix:**
$$\mathbf{M} = \begin{pmatrix} m & 0 \\ 0 & m \end{pmatrix} = m\mathbf{I}$$

**Stiffness matrix:**
$$\mathbf{K} = \begin{pmatrix} k+2k & -2k \\ -2k & 2k+k \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3k & -2k \\ -2k & 3k \end{pmatrix}$$

**Dynamical matrix:**
$$\mathbf{\Omega}^2 = \mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{K} = \frac{1}{m}\begin{pmatrix} 3k & -2k \\ -2k & 3k \end{pmatrix} = \frac{k}{m}\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ -2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Equation:**
$$\ddot{\mathbf{x}} = -\frac{k}{m}\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ -2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}$$

**Answer:** $\ddot{\mathbf{x}} = -\frac{k}{m}\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ -2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}$

## Normal Modes: Eigenvalue Problem

Seek solutions of form:
$$\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{a}e^{i\omega t}$$

where $\mathbf{a}$ is amplitude vector.

Substituting:
$$-\omega^2\mathbf{a}e^{i\omega t} = -\mathbf{\Omega}^2\mathbf{a}e^{i\omega t}$$

$$\mathbf{\Omega}^2\mathbf{a} = \omega^2\mathbf{a}$$

This is an **eigenvalue equation**: $\omega^2$ are eigenvalues, $\mathbf{a}$ are eigenvectors of $\mathbf{\Omega}^2$.

**Characteristic equation:**
$$\det(\mathbf{\Omega}^2 - \omega^2\mathbf{I}) = 0$$

### Example 2: Finding Normal Mode Frequencies

For the system in Example 1, find the normal mode frequencies.

**Solution:**

$$\mathbf{\Omega}^2 = \frac{k}{m}\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ -2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$$

Characteristic equation:
$$\det\begin{pmatrix} 3k/m - \omega^2 & -2k/m \\ -2k/m & 3k/m - \omega^2 \end{pmatrix} = 0$$

$$(3k/m - \omega^2)^2 - (2k/m)^2 = 0$$

$$(3k/m - \omega^2)^2 = 4k^2/m^2$$

$$3k/m - \omega^2 = \pm 2k/m$$

**Mode 1:** $\omega_1^2 = 3k/m - 2k/m = k/m$
$$\omega_1 = \sqrt{k/m}$$

**Mode 2:** $\omega_2^2 = 3k/m + 2k/m = 5k/m$
$$\omega_2 = \sqrt{5k/m}$$

**Answer:** $\omega_1 = \sqrt{k/m}$, $\omega_2 = \sqrt{5k/m}$

## Normal Mode Eigenvectors

For each eigenvalue $\omega_i^2$, find eigenvector $\mathbf{a}^{(i)}$ from:
$$(\mathbf{\Omega}^2 - \omega_i^2\mathbf{I})\mathbf{a}^{(i)} = \mathbf{0}$$

### Example 3: Finding Eigenvectors

Find the normal mode patterns for the system in Examples 1-2.

**Solution:**

**Mode 1** ($\omega_1^2 = k/m$):
$$\begin{pmatrix} 3k/m - k/m & -2k/m \\ -2k/m & 3k/m - k/m \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} a_1^{(1)} \\ a_2^{(1)} \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{0}$$

$$\begin{pmatrix} 2k/m & -2k/m \\ -2k/m & 2k/m \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} a_1^{(1)} \\ a_2^{(1)} \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{0}$$

First row: $2k a_1^{(1)}/m - 2k a_2^{(1)}/m = 0$, so $a_1^{(1)} = a_2^{(1)}$.

Normalized: $\mathbf{a}^{(1)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ (symmetric mode)

**Mode 2** ($\omega_2^2 = 5k/m$):
$$\begin{pmatrix} 3k/m - 5k/m & -2k/m \\ -2k/m & 3k/m - 5k/m \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} a_1^{(2)} \\ a_2^{(2)} \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{0}$$

$$\begin{pmatrix} -2k/m & -2k/m \\ -2k/m & -2k/m \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} a_1^{(2)} \\ a_2^{(2)} \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{0}$$

First row: $-2k a_1^{(2)}/m - 2k a_2^{(2)}/m = 0$, so $a_1^{(2)} = -a_2^{(2)}$.

Normalized: $\mathbf{a}^{(2)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$ (antisymmetric mode)

**Physical interpretation:**
- Mode 1: Both masses move in phase (symmetric)
- Mode 2: Masses move out of phase (antisymmetric)

**Answer:** $\mathbf{a}^{(1)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $\mathbf{a}^{(2)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$

## Orthogonality of Normal Modes

Normal mode eigenvectors are **orthogonal** with respect to the mass matrix:
$$(\mathbf{a}^{(i)})^T \mathbf{M} \mathbf{a}^{(j)} = 0 \quad \text{for } i \neq j$$

**Proof:** From eigenvalue equations:
$$\mathbf{M}\mathbf{\Omega}^2\mathbf{a}^{(i)} = \omega_i^2\mathbf{M}\mathbf{a}^{(i)}$$

which is equivalent to:
$$\mathbf{K}\mathbf{a}^{(i)} = \omega_i^2\mathbf{M}\mathbf{a}^{(i)}$$

Multiply by $(\mathbf{a}^{(j)})^T$:
$$(\mathbf{a}^{(j)})^T\mathbf{K}\mathbf{a}^{(i)} = \omega_i^2(\mathbf{a}^{(j)})^T\mathbf{M}\mathbf{a}^{(i)}$$

Similarly for mode $j$:
$$(\mathbf{a}^{(i)})^T\mathbf{K}\mathbf{a}^{(j)} = \omega_j^2(\mathbf{a}^{(i)})^T\mathbf{M}\mathbf{a}^{(j)}$$

Since $\mathbf{K}$ is symmetric: $(\mathbf{a}^{(j)})^T\mathbf{K}\mathbf{a}^{(i)} = (\mathbf{a}^{(i)})^T\mathbf{K}\mathbf{a}^{(j)}$

Subtracting:
$$0 = (\omega_i^2 - \omega_j^2)(\mathbf{a}^{(j)})^T\mathbf{M}\mathbf{a}^{(i)}$$

For $\omega_i \neq \omega_j$: $(\mathbf{a}^{(j)})^T\mathbf{M}\mathbf{a}^{(i)} = 0$ ∎

### Example 4: Verifying Orthogonality

Verify orthogonality for modes in Example 3 with $m_1 = m_2 = m$.

**Solution:**

$$\mathbf{M} = m\mathbf{I} = m\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

$$(\mathbf{a}^{(1)})^T\mathbf{M}\mathbf{a}^{(2)} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} m \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$$

$$= \frac{m}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{m}{2}(1 - 1) = 0$$ ✓

**Answer:** Modes are orthogonal

## Diagonalization and Normal Coordinates

Form **modal matrix** $\mathbf{A}$ with eigenvectors as columns:
$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix} | & | \\ \mathbf{a}^{(1)} & \mathbf{a}^{(2)} \\ | & | \end{pmatrix}$$

**Coordinate transformation:** $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{q}$

where $\mathbf{q} = \begin{pmatrix} q_1 \\ q_2 \end{pmatrix}$ are **normal coordinates**.

**Transformed equation:**
$$\mathbf{M}\mathbf{A}\ddot{\mathbf{q}} = -\mathbf{K}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{q}$$

Multiply by $\mathbf{A}^T$:
$$\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{M}\mathbf{A}\ddot{\mathbf{q}} = -\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{K}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{q}$$

Due to orthogonality, both $\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{M}\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{K}\mathbf{A}$ are **diagonal**:
$$\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{M}\mathbf{A} = \text{diag}(\mu_1, \mu_2)$$
$$\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{K}\mathbf{A} = \text{diag}(\mu_1\omega_1^2, \mu_2\omega_2^2)$$

**Decoupled equations:**
$$\mu_i\ddot{q}_i = -\mu_i\omega_i^2 q_i$$
$$\ddot{q}_i + \omega_i^2 q_i = 0$$

Each normal coordinate oscillates independently!

### Example 5: General Solution

For the system in previous examples, write general solution in terms of normal coordinates.

**Solution:**

Normal coordinates:
$$q_1(t) = A_1\cos(\omega_1 t + \phi_1) = A_1\cos(\sqrt{k/m}\,t + \phi_1)$$
$$q_2(t) = A_2\cos(\omega_2 t + \phi_2) = A_2\cos(\sqrt{5k/m}\,t + \phi_2)$$

**Transform back:**
$$\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{q} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} q_1 \\ q_2 \end{pmatrix}$$

$$x_1(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(q_1 + q_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[A_1\cos(\omega_1 t + \phi_1) + A_2\cos(\omega_2 t + \phi_2)]$$

$$x_2(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(q_1 - q_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[A_1\cos(\omega_1 t + \phi_1) - A_2\cos(\omega_2 t + \phi_2)]$$

Constants $A_1, A_2, \phi_1, \phi_2$ determined by initial conditions.

**Answer:** Motion is superposition of two normal modes oscillating at $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$

## Three Coupled Oscillators

For $N$ oscillators, the formalism generalizes:
- $\mathbf{x}$ is $N \times 1$ position vector
- $\mathbf{M}$ is $N \times N$ mass matrix
- $\mathbf{K}$ is $N \times N$ stiffness matrix
- Find $N$ eigenvalues $\omega_i^2$ and eigenvectors $\mathbf{a}^{(i)}$
- General motion is superposition of $N$ normal modes

### Example: Three Identical Masses

Three masses $m$ with four springs all with constant $k$. The dynamical matrix is:
$$\mathbf{\Omega}^2 = \frac{k}{m}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Characteristic equation:**
$$\det(\mathbf{\Omega}^2 - \omega^2\mathbf{I}) = 0$$

This yields (after calculation):
$$\omega_1^2 = \frac{k}{m}(2-\sqrt{2}), \quad \omega_2^2 = \frac{2k}{m}, \quad \omega_3^2 = \frac{k}{m}(2+\sqrt{2})$$

**Eigenvectors:**
$$\mathbf{a}^{(1)} \propto \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \sqrt{2} \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{a}^{(2)} \propto \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{a}^{(3)} \propto \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -\sqrt{2} \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

(After normalization)

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**

If $\omega_1 = \sqrt{k/m}$ (same as single oscillator), coupling must be zero.

---

**Problem 2: B**

In symmetric mode, both masses move together by same amount, so coupling spring doesn't compress or extend.

---

**Problem 3: B**

Orthogonality with respect to mass matrix: $(\mathbf{a}^{(i)})^T \mathbf{M} \mathbf{a}^{(j)} = 0$ for $i \neq j$

---

**Problem 4: C**

$N$ coupled oscillators have $N$ normal modes (one for each degree of freedom)

---

**Problem 5: B**

After diagonalization, each normal coordinate satisfies independent SHM equation: $\ddot{q}_i + \omega_i^2 q_i = 0$
