# Article 1.3: Kinematics in Different Frames

In introductory physics, velocity is simply the time derivative of position, and acceleration is the time derivative of velocity. This simplicity holds when working in Cartesian coordinates with constant unit vectors. But the real world rarely cooperates: planets orbit in planes, satellites trace curved paths, and particles spiral through magnetic fields. These motions demand curvilinear coordinates, and in curvilinear coordinates, the unit vectors themselves rotate and change with time.

The consequences are profound. A particle moving in a circle at constant speed has zero radial velocity ($\dot{r} = 0$) but nonzero acceleration—the famous centripetal acceleration. This acceleration arises not from changes in speed but from changes in the direction of the velocity vector, encoded in the time derivative of the rotating unit vector $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$. Similarly, particles moving radially outward while rotating—like water droplets flung from a spinning disk—have velocity components in both $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ directions, and acceleration terms mixing $\dot{r}$, $\ddot{r}$, $\dot{\theta}$, and $\ddot{\theta}$.

This article systematically derives velocity and acceleration expressions in polar, cylindrical, and spherical coordinates. We emphasize physical interpretation: the radial and tangential components of velocity, the centripetal and Coriolis-like terms in acceleration. Mastering these formulas is essential not just for solving problems but for developing intuition about curved motion—intuition that carries through to orbital mechanics, rigid body rotation, and eventually the tensor formulations of general relativity.

## Velocity in Polar Coordinates

For a particle at position $\mathbf{r} = r\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ in polar coordinates, velocity is:
$$\mathbf{v} = \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}(r\hat{\mathbf{r}}) = \dot{r}\hat{\mathbf{r}} + r\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{dt}$$

Since $d\hat{\mathbf{r}}/dt = \dot{\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ (from coordinate system rotation):
$$\mathbf{v} = \dot{r}\hat{\mathbf{r}} + r\dot{\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$$

The velocity has two components:
- **Radial velocity:** $v_r = \dot{r}$ (rate of change of distance from origin)
- **Tangential velocity:** $v_\theta = r\dot{\theta}$ (speed along circular arc)

For circular motion ($r =$ constant), $v_r = 0$ and $v = r\dot{\theta} = r\omega$ where $\omega = \dot{\theta}$ is angular velocity.

## Acceleration in Polar Coordinates

Differentiate the velocity:
$$\mathbf{a} = \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}(\dot{r}\hat{\mathbf{r}} + r\dot{\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})$$

$$= \ddot{r}\hat{\mathbf{r}} + \dot{r}\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{dt} + \dot{r}\dot{\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} + r\ddot{\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} + r\dot{\theta}\frac{d\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{dt}$$

Using $d\hat{\mathbf{r}}/dt = \dot{\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ and $d\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}/dt = -\dot{\theta}\hat{\mathbf{r}}$:
$$\mathbf{a} = \ddot{r}\hat{\mathbf{r}} + \dot{r}\dot{\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} + \dot{r}\dot{\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} + r\ddot{\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} - r\dot{\theta}^2\hat{\mathbf{r}}$$

$$= (\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2)\hat{\mathbf{r}} + (r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta})\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$$

The acceleration components are:
- **Radial acceleration:** $a_r = \ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2$
- **Tangential acceleration:** $a_\theta = r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$

The term $-r\dot{\theta}^2$ is **centripetal acceleration** (pointing inward). The term $2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$ is often called the **Coriolis-like term** (though true Coriolis force appears in rotating frames).

### Example 1: Circular Motion at Constant Speed

A particle moves in a circle of radius $R = 5$ m with constant angular velocity $\omega = 2$ rad/s. Find velocity and acceleration vectors.

**Solution:** For circular motion: $r = R = 5$ m (constant), $\theta = \omega t$, so $\dot{r} = 0$, $\ddot{r} = 0$, $\dot{\theta} = \omega = 2$ rad/s, $\ddot{\theta} = 0$.

Velocity:
$$\mathbf{v} = \dot{r}\hat{\mathbf{r}} + r\dot{\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = 0 + (5)(2)\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = 10\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \text{ m/s}$$

Acceleration:
$$\mathbf{a} = (\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2)\hat{\mathbf{r}} + (r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta})\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$$
$$= (0 - 5 \cdot 4)\hat{\mathbf{r}} + (0 + 0)\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = -20\hat{\mathbf{r}} \text{ m/s}^2$$

The acceleration is purely radial (centripetal), pointing toward the center with magnitude $a = r\omega^2 = 5(4) = 20$ m/s².

**Answer:** $\mathbf{v} = 10\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ m/s, $\mathbf{a} = -20\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ m/s²

### Example 2: Spiral Motion

A particle moves along spiral path $r = bt$ where $b = 2$ m/s, with constant angular velocity $\dot{\theta} = \omega = 3$ rad/s. Find velocity and acceleration at $t = 1$ s.

**Solution:** Given: $r = bt = 2t$, so $\dot{r} = b = 2$ m/s, $\ddot{r} = 0$, $\dot{\theta} = 3$ rad/s, $\ddot{\theta} = 0$.

At $t = 1$ s: $r = 2$ m.

Velocity:
$$\mathbf{v} = \dot{r}\hat{\mathbf{r}} + r\dot{\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = 2\hat{\mathbf{r}} + (2)(3)\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = 2\hat{\mathbf{r}} + 6\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \text{ m/s}$$

Speed: $|\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{4 + 36} = \sqrt{40} = 2\sqrt{10}$ m/s.

Acceleration:
$$\mathbf{a} = (\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2)\hat{\mathbf{r}} + (r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta})\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$$
$$= (0 - 2 \cdot 9)\hat{\mathbf{r}} + (0 + 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3)\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$$
$$= -18\hat{\mathbf{r}} + 12\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \text{ m/s}^2$$

The radial component $-18\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ is centripetal. The tangential component $12\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ arises from the coupling term $2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$.

**Answer:** $\mathbf{v} = 2\hat{\mathbf{r}} + 6\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ m/s, $\mathbf{a} = -18\hat{\mathbf{r}} + 12\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ m/s²

## Velocity and Acceleration in Cylindrical Coordinates

Cylindrical coordinates $(s, \phi, z)$ extend polar coordinates to 3D. Position: $\mathbf{r} = s\hat{\mathbf{s}} + z\hat{\mathbf{z}}$.

Velocity:
$$\mathbf{v} = \dot{s}\hat{\mathbf{s}} + s\dot{\phi}\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} + \dot{z}\hat{\mathbf{z}}$$

Acceleration:
$$\mathbf{a} = (\ddot{s} - s\dot{\phi}^2)\hat{\mathbf{s}} + (s\ddot{\phi} + 2\dot{s}\dot{\phi})\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} + \ddot{z}\hat{\mathbf{z}}$$

The radial ($\hat{\mathbf{s}}$) and azimuthal ($\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$) components are identical to polar coordinates; the vertical component ($\hat{\mathbf{z}}$) adds independently since $\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ doesn't rotate.

### Example 3: Helical Motion

A particle follows a helix: $s = R$ (constant), $\phi = \omega t$, $z = vt$ where $R = 3$ m, $\omega = 2$ rad/s, $v = 4$ m/s. Find velocity and acceleration.

**Solution:** We have $\dot{s} = 0$, $\ddot{s} = 0$, $\dot{\phi} = \omega = 2$ rad/s, $\ddot{\phi} = 0$, $\dot{z} = v = 4$ m/s, $\ddot{z} = 0$.

Velocity:
$$\mathbf{v} = 0 + (3)(2)\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} + 4\hat{\mathbf{z}} = 6\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} + 4\hat{\mathbf{z}} \text{ m/s}$$

Speed: $|\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{36 + 16} = \sqrt{52} = 2\sqrt{13}$ m/s.

Acceleration:
$$\mathbf{a} = (0 - 3 \cdot 4)\hat{\mathbf{s}} + (0 + 0)\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} + 0\hat{\mathbf{z}} = -12\hat{\mathbf{s}} \text{ m/s}^2$$

Pure centripetal acceleration toward the axis.

**Answer:** $\mathbf{v} = 6\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} + 4\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ m/s, $\mathbf{a} = -12\hat{\mathbf{s}}$ m/s²

## Velocity and Acceleration in Spherical Coordinates

In spherical coordinates $(r, \theta, \phi)$, position is $\mathbf{r} = r\hat{\mathbf{r}}$.

Velocity:
$$\mathbf{v} = \dot{r}\hat{\mathbf{r}} + r\dot{\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} + r\sin\theta\dot{\phi}\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$$

Acceleration (derivation is lengthy):
$$\mathbf{a} = (\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2 - r\sin^2\theta\dot{\phi}^2)\hat{\mathbf{r}} + (r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta} - r\sin\theta\cos\theta\dot{\phi}^2)\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$$
$$+ (r\sin\theta\ddot{\phi} + 2\dot{r}\sin\theta\dot{\phi} + 2r\cos\theta\dot{\theta}\dot{\phi})\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$$

These formulas are complex but systematic. Each term has physical meaning:
- Centripetal terms: $-r\dot{\theta}^2$, $-r\sin^2\theta\dot{\phi}^2$
- Coriolis-like terms: $2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$, $2\dot{r}\sin\theta\dot{\phi}$, $2r\cos\theta\dot{\theta}\dot{\phi}$
- Angular acceleration terms: $r\ddot{\theta}$, $r\sin\theta\ddot{\phi}$

### Example 4: Motion at Constant Distance with Varying Angles

A particle moves on a sphere of radius $R = 10$ m with $\theta = \pi/6$ (constant) and $\phi = \omega t$ where $\omega = 1$ rad/s. Find acceleration.

**Solution:** We have $r = R = 10$ m, $\dot{r} = 0$, $\ddot{r} = 0$, $\theta = \pi/6$, $\dot{\theta} = 0$, $\ddot{\theta} = 0$, $\dot{\phi} = 1$ rad/s, $\ddot{\phi} = 0$.

Note: $\sin(\pi/6) = 1/2$, $\cos(\pi/6) = \sqrt{3}/2$.

Radial component:
$$a_r = \ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2 - r\sin^2\theta\dot{\phi}^2 = 0 - 0 - 10(1/4)(1) = -2.5 \text{ m/s}^2$$

Polar component:
$$a_\theta = r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta} - r\sin\theta\cos\theta\dot{\phi}^2 = 0 + 0 - 10(1/2)(\sqrt{3}/2)(1) = -\frac{5\sqrt{3}}{2} \text{ m/s}^2$$

Azimuthal component:
$$a_\phi = r\sin\theta\ddot{\phi} + 2\dot{r}\sin\theta\dot{\phi} + 2r\cos\theta\dot{\theta}\dot{\phi} = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0$$

**Answer:** $\mathbf{a} = -2.5\hat{\mathbf{r}} - \frac{5\sqrt{3}}{2}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ m/s²

### Example 5: Projectile Motion in Spherical Coordinates

Though usually solved in Cartesian coordinates, projectile motion can be expressed in spherical coordinates for Earth-surface problems. A ball is thrown from the origin at angle $\alpha$ to the horizontal with initial speed $v_0$. Express initial velocity in spherical coordinates.

**Solution:** At $t = 0$, the particle is at origin with initial velocity at angle $\alpha$ from horizontal (angle $\theta_0 = \pi/2 - \alpha$ from vertical).

The initial velocity has magnitude $v_0$ directed at polar angle $\theta_0$. In spherical coordinates:
$$\mathbf{v}(0) = v_0\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = v_0(\cos\theta_0\cos\phi_0\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \cos\theta_0\sin\phi_0\hat{\mathbf{y}} - \sin\theta_0\hat{\mathbf{z}})$$

But... $r = 0$, spherical coordinates are singular. We need $r > 0$. If launched from Earth's surface at radius $R$:
$$\mathbf{v}(0) = 0\hat{\mathbf{r}} + v_0\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} + 0\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$$

where the $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ direction points "northward" along a meridian if we set up coordinates properly.

**Answer:** $\mathbf{v}(0) = v_0\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ (tangent to sphere at launch point)

A) $4$ m/s

B) $8$ m/s

D) $16$ m/s

E) $24$ m/s

**Problem 2:** For the same motion as Problem 1, what is $v_\theta$ at $t = 1$ s?

A) $12$ m/s

B) $24$ m/s

C) $36$ m/s

D) $48$ m/s

E) $60$ m/s

**Problem 3:** A particle in circular motion ($r =$ constant, $\dot{\theta} = \omega =$ constant) has centripetal acceleration magnitude:

A) $r\omega$

B) $r\omega^2$

C) $r^2\omega$

D) $\omega^2/r$

E) $r/\omega$

---

**Problem 4:** In cylindrical coordinates, a particle has $\dot{s} = 2$ m/s, $s = 3$ m, $\dot{\phi} = 4$ rad/s, $\ddot{\phi} = 0$. What is the magnitude of the tangential (Coriolis-like) acceleration component in the $\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$ direction?

A) $8$ m/s²

B) $12$ m/s²

C) $16$ m/s²

D) $24$ m/s²

E) $48$ m/s²

---

**Problem 5:** In spherical coordinates, for motion at constant $r$ and constant $\theta$, the acceleration is:

A) Zero

B) Purely radial

C) Purely azimuthal

D) Both radial and polar

E) All three components nonzero

---

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**

$r = 4t^2$, so $\dot{r} = 8t$. At $t = 1$ s: $v_r = \dot{r} = 8(1) = 8$ m/s.

---

**Problem 2: A**

$\theta = 3t$, so $\dot{\theta} = 3$ rad/s. At $t = 1$ s: $r = 4(1)^2 = 4$ m.

$v_\theta = r\dot{\theta} = (4)(3) = 12$ m/s.

---

**Problem 3: B**

Centripetal acceleration: $a_c = r\omega^2 = r\dot{\theta}^2$.

---

**Problem 4: C**

The tangential (Coriolis-like) term in $\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$ direction is: $a_\phi = 2\dot{s}\dot{\phi} = 2(2)(4) = 16$ m/s².

---

**Problem 5: B**

For $r =$ const, $\theta =$ const, $\phi = \omega t$ (constant angular velocity in azimuthal direction):

$\dot{r} = 0$, $\ddot{r} = 0$, $\dot{\theta} = 0$, $\ddot{\theta} = 0$, $\dot{\phi} = \omega$, $\ddot{\phi} = 0$.

Radial: $a_r = -r\sin^2\theta\dot{\phi}^2 \neq 0$
Polar: $a_\theta = 0$
Azimuthal: $a_\phi = 0$

Acceleration is purely radial (centripetal).
