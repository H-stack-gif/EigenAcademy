# Article 3.2: Conservative Forces and Potential Energy

The distinction between conservative and non-conservative forces represents one of the deepest insights in classical mechanics. Conservative forces—gravity, springs, electrostatic forces—possess a special mathematical property: the work they do is path-independent, depending only on endpoints. This path independence implies the existence of a potential energy function $U(\mathbf{r})$, a scalar field whose negative gradient gives the force: $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U$. The work-energy theorem then transforms into energy conservation: $K + U = $ constant.

From a mathematical perspective, conservative forces are precisely those with zero curl: $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$. This connects to fundamental theorems of vector calculus—a vector field is conservative (the gradient of a scalar) if and only if its curl vanishes. In simply connected domains, path-independent line integrals and zero curl are equivalent conditions. These mathematical structures reflect deep physical principles: conservative forces arise from interactions that can be assigned a state function (potential energy) depending only on configuration.

This article rigorously develops conservative forces and potential energy. We prove the equivalence between path independence and zero curl, derive potential energy functions from force laws using line integrals, and explore the gradient relationship. The techniques here—testing for conservativeness, computing curl in various coordinates, constructing potentials—are essential for energy methods and prepare for Lagrangian mechanics, where the entire formalism is built on kinetic and potential energy functions.

## Path Independence and Conservative Forces

A force $\mathbf{F}$ is **conservative** if the work done moving from A to B is independent of the path:
$$W_{AB} = \int_{C_1} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_{C_2} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$$

for any two paths $C_1$ and $C_2$ connecting A and B.

**Equivalent condition:** For any closed path $C$:
$$\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0$$

**Proof of equivalence:** For closed path formed by going from A to B along $C_1$ and back along $C_2^{-1}$:
$$\oint = \int_{C_1} + \int_{C_2^{-1}} = \int_{C_1} - \int_{C_2} = 0$$

if and only if $\int_{C_1} = \int_{C_2}$.

### Example 1: Testing Path Independence

Test whether $\mathbf{F} = y\hat{\mathbf{x}} + x\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is conservative by computing work along two paths from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$.

**Solution:** 

**Path 1:** Straight line $y = x$, so $dy = dx$.
$$W_1 = \int_0^1 (F_x dx + F_y dy) = \int_0^1 (y dx + x dy) = \int_0^1 (x dx + x dx) = \int_0^1 2x dx = x^2\Big|_0^1 = 1$$

**Path 2:** First along $x$-axis to $(1,0)$, then vertically to $(1,1)$.

Segment 1: $y = 0$, $dy = 0$, $x: 0 \to 1$
$$W_{2a} = \int_0^1 y dx = 0$$

Segment 2: $x = 1$, $dx = 0$, $y: 0 \to 1$
$$W_{2b} = \int_0^1 x dy = \int_0^1 1 dy = 1$$

Total: $W_2 = 0 + 1 = 1 = W_1$ ✓

The work is path-independent, so the force is conservative.

**Answer:** Conservative (both paths give $W = 1$ J)

## The Curl Test for Conservative Forces

**Theorem:** In a simply connected region, $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative if and only if $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$.

**Curl in Cartesian coordinates:**
$$\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{x}} & \hat{\mathbf{y}} & \hat{\mathbf{z}} \\ \partial/\partial x & \partial/\partial y & \partial/\partial z \\ F_x & F_y & F_z \end{vmatrix}$$

$$= \left(\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial z}\right)\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \left(\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial F_z}{\partial x}\right)\hat{\mathbf{y}} + \left(\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}\right)\hat{\mathbf{z}}$$

For planar forces ($F_z = 0$, no $z$-dependence):
$$\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \left(\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}\right)\hat{\mathbf{z}}$$

### Example 2: Curl Test for Conservative Force

Test whether $\mathbf{F} = (3x^2 y + 2z)\hat{\mathbf{x}} + (x^3 - z^2)\hat{\mathbf{y}} + (2x - 2yz)\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ is conservative.

**Solution:** Compute curl components:

$$\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial(2x - 2yz)}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial(x^3 - z^2)}{\partial z} = -2z - (-2z) = 0$$

$$\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial F_z}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial(3x^2y + 2z)}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial(2x - 2yz)}{\partial x} = 2 - 2 = 0$$

$$\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial(x^3 - z^2)}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial(3x^2y + 2z)}{\partial y} = 3x^2 - 3x^2 = 0$$

All components zero: $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$ ✓

**Answer:** Conservative (curl vanishes)

## Potential Energy and the Gradient

If $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative, there exists a scalar function $U(\mathbf{r})$ called **potential energy** such that:
$$\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U = -\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \frac{\partial U}{\partial y}\hat{\mathbf{y}} + \frac{\partial U}{\partial z}\hat{\mathbf{z}}\right)$$

The negative sign is convention: force points toward decreasing potential.

**Finding $U$ from $\mathbf{F}$:**

Method 1 (Integration): From $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U$:
$$F_x = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}, \quad F_y = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial y}, \quad F_z = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial z}$$

Integrate each equation and match integration constants.

Method 2 (Line integral): 
$$U(\mathbf{r}) - U(\mathbf{r}_0) = -\int_{\mathbf{r}_0}^{\mathbf{r}} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$$

Since $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative, any path works.

### Example 3: Finding Potential Energy Function

For $\mathbf{F} = (2xy + z^2)\hat{\mathbf{x}} + x^2\hat{\mathbf{y}} + 2xz\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ (shown conservative in Complex Analysis Unit 1), find $U(\mathbf{r})$.

**Solution:** From $F_x = -\partial U/\partial x = 2xy + z^2$:
$$U = -\int (2xy + z^2) dx = -x^2y - xz^2 + f(y,z)$$

From $F_y = -\partial U/\partial y = x^2$:
$$\frac{\partial U}{\partial y} = -x^2 \implies \frac{\partial}{\partial y}[-x^2y - xz^2 + f(y,z)] = -x^2 + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = -x^2$$

So $\partial f/\partial y = 0$, meaning $f(y,z) = g(z)$.

From $F_z = -\partial U/\partial z = 2xz$:
$$\frac{\partial U}{\partial z} = -2xz \implies \frac{\partial}{\partial z}[-x^2y - xz^2 + g(z)] = -2xz + g'(z) = -2xz$$

So $g'(z) = 0$, meaning $g(z) = C$ (constant).

**Potential energy:**
$$U(x,y,z) = -x^2y - xz^2 + C$$

Taking $C = 0$ (arbitrary reference):
$$U = -x^2y - xz^2$$

Verify: 

Given $\mathbf{F} = (2xy + z^2)\hat{\mathbf{x}} + x^2\hat{\mathbf{y}} + 2xz\hat{\mathbf{z}}$

If $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U$, then:
- $F_x = 2xy + z^2 = -\partial U/\partial x$
- $F_y = x^2 = -\partial U/\partial y$  
- $F_z = 2xz = -\partial U/\partial z$

Integrate first: $-\partial U/\partial x = 2xy + z^2$
$$U = -(x^2y + xz^2) + g(y,z) = -x^2y - xz^2 + g(y,z)$$

Check second: $-\partial U/\partial y = x^2$
$$-\frac{\partial}{\partial y}[-x^2y - xz^2 + g(y,z)] = x^2 - \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = x^2$$

So $\partial g/\partial y = 0$, thus $g(y,z) = h(z)$.

Check third: $-\partial U/\partial z = 2xz$
$$-\frac{\partial}{\partial z}[-x^2y - xz^2 + h(z)] = 2xz - h'(z) = 2xz$$

So $h'(z) = 0$, thus $h = C$.

**Answer:** $U(x,y,z) = -x^2y - xz^2 + C$

## Standard Potential Energy Functions

### Gravitational Potential Energy (near Earth)

For constant gravitational field $\mathbf{F}_g = -mg\hat{\mathbf{z}}$:
$$U_g = mgz$$

(taking $U = 0$ at $z = 0$)

### Universal Gravitation

For inverse-square law $\mathbf{F} = -\frac{GMm}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}$:
$$U_g = -\frac{GMm}{r}$$

(taking $U \to 0$ as $r \to \infty$)

### Elastic Potential Energy

For spring force $\mathbf{F} = -kx\hat{\mathbf{x}}$:
$$U_s = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$$

(taking $U = 0$ at equilibrium $x = 0$)

### Example 4: Gravitational Potential Energy Derivation

Derive $U = -GMm/r$ from $\mathbf{F} = -GMm/r^2 \hat{\mathbf{r}}$.

**Solution:** Use line integral along radial path:
$$U(r) - U(\infty) = -\int_\infty^r F_r dr = -\int_\infty^r \left(-\frac{GMm}{r'^2}\right) dr'$$

$$= GMm \int_\infty^r \frac{dr'}{r'^2} = GMm\left[-\frac{1}{r'}\right]_\infty^r = GMm\left(-\frac{1}{r} - 0\right) = -\frac{GMm}{r}$$

Taking $U(\infty) = 0$:
$$U(r) = -\frac{GMm}{r}$$

**Answer:** $U(r) = -GMm/r$ (negative, reflecting attractive force)

### Example 5: Multi-Particle Potential Energy

Three particles of equal mass $m$ are at vertices of an equilateral triangle with side length $a$. Find the total gravitational potential energy.

**Solution:** Potential energy for each pair:
$$U_{ij} = -\frac{Gm \cdot m}{r_{ij}} = -\frac{Gm^2}{a}$$

Three pairs: (1,2), (1,3), (2,3), each separated by $a$.

Total:
$$U_{\text{total}} = U_{12} + U_{13} + U_{23} = 3\left(-\frac{Gm^2}{a}\right) = -\frac{3Gm^2}{a}$$

**Answer:** $U = -3Gm^2/a$

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**

A force is conservative if and only if its curl vanishes: $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$.

---

**Problem 2: B**

$\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U = -\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(-\frac{Gm_1m_2}{r}\right)\hat{\mathbf{r}} = -Gm_1m_2 \cdot \frac{1}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}$

This is attractive (negative radial direction).

---

**Problem 3: A**

$$\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \left(\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}\right)\hat{\mathbf{z}} = \left(\frac{\partial(-kx)}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial(-ky)}{\partial y}\right)\hat{\mathbf{z}} = (-k - (-k))\hat{\mathbf{z}} = 0$$

---

**Problem 4: A**

$U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{1}{2}(500)(0.1)^2 = \frac{1}{2}(500)(0.01) = 2.5$ J

---

**Problem 5: B**

$F_x = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial x} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2y + y^3) = -2xy$
