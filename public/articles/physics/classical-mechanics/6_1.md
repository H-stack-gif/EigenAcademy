# Article 6.1: Central Force Motion

Central forces—those directed toward or away from a fixed point and depending only on distance—dominate gravitational and electrostatic interactions. The mathematical structure of central force problems exhibits beautiful symmetries: angular momentum conservation guarantees planar motion, while energy conservation reduces three-dimensional dynamics to effective one-dimensional radial motion. The interplay between attractive central force and angular momentum creates the centrifugal barrier, leading to bound orbits, stable circular motion, and scattering phenomena.

The effective potential $U_{eff}(r) = U(r) + L^2/(2mr^2)$ combines the actual central potential with a repulsive centrifugal term. This single function determines orbital characteristics: its minimum locates circular orbits, while energy relative to the effective potential classifies orbits as bound ellipses, parabolic escape trajectories, or hyperbolic scattering. Energy diagrams in the effective potential provide immediate qualitative understanding without solving differential equations.

This article develops central force motion systematically: establishing conservation laws, deriving the effective potential and radial equation, analyzing circular orbits and stability, and classifying orbital types by energy. The mathematics employs vector calculus, separation of variables, and graphical analysis of effective potentials. Applications focus on gravitational systems—planets, satellites, binary stars—where inverse-square forces govern motion.

## Properties of Central Forces

A force is **central** if it has the form:
$$\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}) = f(r)\hat{\mathbf{r}}$$

where $f(r)$ depends only on $r = |\mathbf{r}|$ and $\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{r}/r$ is the radial unit vector.

**Key properties:**

1. **Conservative:** Central forces are gradients of radial potentials:
   $$\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U(r) = -\frac{dU}{dr}\hat{\mathbf{r}}$$

2. **Zero torque:** About the force center:
   $$\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{r} \times (f(r)\hat{\mathbf{r}}) = f(r)(\mathbf{r} \times \hat{\mathbf{r}}) = \mathbf{0}$$
   
   since $\mathbf{r} \parallel \hat{\mathbf{r}}$.

3. **Angular momentum conserved:** From $d\mathbf{L}/dt = \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{0}$:
   $$\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times m\mathbf{v} = \text{constant}$$

4. **Planar motion:** Since $\mathbf{L} \perp \mathbf{r}$ always and $\mathbf{L}$ is constant, motion confined to plane $\perp \mathbf{L}$.

### Example 1: Verifying Central Force Properties

For $\mathbf{F} = -\frac{k}{r^3}\mathbf{r}$ (inverse-square written in vector form), show: (a) force is central, (b) find potential, (c) verify torque is zero.

**Solution:**

**(a)** Write $\mathbf{F} = -\frac{k}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ where $\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{r}/r$.

This has form $f(r)\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ with $f(r) = -k/r^2$, so it's central.

**(b)** For radial force:
$$U(r) = -\int f(r) dr = -\int \left(-\frac{k}{r^2}\right) dr = k\int \frac{dr}{r^2} = -\frac{k}{r} + C$$

Taking $U(\infty) = 0$: $U(r) = -k/r$

**(c)** Torque:
$$\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{r} \times \left(-\frac{k}{r^3}\mathbf{r}\right) = -\frac{k}{r^3}(\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{r}) = \mathbf{0}$$ ✓

**Answer:** Central force with $U(r) = -k/r$, zero torque

## Angular Momentum in Polar Coordinates

For planar motion, use polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ in the orbital plane.

**Position:** $\mathbf{r} = r\hat{\mathbf{r}}$

**Velocity:** $\mathbf{v} = \dot{r}\hat{\mathbf{r}} + r\dot{\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$

**Angular momentum magnitude:**
$$L = |\mathbf{r} \times m\mathbf{v}| = m|r\hat{\mathbf{r}} \times (\dot{r}\hat{\mathbf{r}} + r\dot{\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})|$$

$$= m|r^2\dot{\theta}(\hat{\mathbf{r}} \times \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})| = mr^2\dot{\theta}$$

(using $\hat{\mathbf{r}} \times \hat{\mathbf{r}} = 0$ and $|\hat{\mathbf{r}} \times \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}| = 1$)

**Conservation:** Since $L = mr^2\dot{\theta} = $ constant, we can write:
$$\dot{\theta} = \frac{L}{mr^2}$$

This relates angular velocity to radius.

### Example 2: Angular Momentum from Orbital Data

A satellite in elliptical orbit has $r_1 = 7000$ km with speed $v_1 = 8$ km/s perpendicular to radius. At another point, $r_2 = 10000$ km. Find $v_2$.

**Solution:**

At perihelion/aphelion (or any point where $\mathbf{v} \perp \mathbf{r}$), velocity is purely tangential:
$$L = mr_1v_1 = mr_2v_2$$

$$v_2 = \frac{r_1v_1}{r_2} = \frac{7000 \times 8}{10000} = 5.6 \text{ km/s}$$

**Answer:** $v_2 = 5.6$ km/s

## The Effective Potential

**Energy in polar coordinates:**
$$E = \frac{1}{2}m(\dot{r}^2 + r^2\dot{\theta}^2) + U(r)$$

Substitute $\dot{\theta} = L/(mr^2)$:
$$E = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{r}^2 + \frac{L^2}{2mr^2} + U(r)$$

Define **effective potential:**
$$U_{eff}(r) = U(r) + \frac{L^2}{2mr^2}$$

Then:
$$E = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{r}^2 + U_{eff}(r)$$

This is the energy equation for **one-dimensional radial motion** in effective potential $U_{eff}$.

**Centrifugal barrier:** The term $L^2/(2mr^2)$ acts as repulsive potential preventing $r \to 0$ (unless $L = 0$).

**Radial equation of motion:**
$$m\ddot{r} = -\frac{dU_{eff}}{dr} = -\frac{dU}{dr} + \frac{L^2}{mr^3}$$

### Example 3: Effective Potential for Inverse-Square Force

For gravitational force with $U(r) = -k/r$ (where $k = GMm$), derive and sketch $U_{eff}(r)$.

**Solution:**

$$U_{eff}(r) = -\frac{k}{r} + \frac{L^2}{2mr^2}$$

**Limits:**
- As $r \to 0$: $U_{eff} \to +\infty$ (centrifugal barrier dominates)
- As $r \to \infty$: $U_{eff} \to 0^-$ (from below)

**Minimum:** Find $dU_{eff}/dr = 0$:
$$\frac{dU_{eff}}{dr} = \frac{k}{r^2} - \frac{L^2}{mr^3} = 0$$

$$\frac{k}{r^2} = \frac{L^2}{mr^3}$$

$$r_0 = \frac{L^2}{mk}$$

**Value at minimum:**
$$U_{eff}(r_0) = -\frac{k}{L^2/(mk)} + \frac{L^2}{2m(L^2/(mk))^2} = -\frac{mk^2}{L^2} + \frac{mk^2}{2L^2} = -\frac{mk^2}{2L^2}$$

**Second derivative (stability):**
$$\frac{d^2U_{eff}}{dr^2} = -\frac{2k}{r^3} + \frac{3L^2}{mr^4}$$

At $r_0$: positive, so stable minimum.

**Answer:** $U_{eff} = -k/r + L^2/(2mr^2)$ with minimum at $r_0 = L^2/(mk)$

## Circular Orbits

**Condition for circular orbit:** $r = r_0$ constant requires $\dot{r} = 0$ and $\ddot{r} = 0$.

From effective potential: circular orbit at minimum of $U_{eff}$.

**Energy of circular orbit:**
$$E_{circ} = U_{eff}(r_0) = U(r_0) + \frac{L^2}{2mr_0^2}$$

For inverse-square force ($U = -k/r$):
$$E_{circ} = -\frac{k}{r_0} + \frac{L^2}{2mr_0^2}$$

But from $r_0 = L^2/(mk)$: $L^2 = mkr_0$

$$E_{circ} = -\frac{k}{r_0} + \frac{mkr_0}{2mr_0^2} = -\frac{k}{r_0} + \frac{k}{2r_0} = -\frac{k}{2r_0}$$

**Virial theorem:** For inverse-square force:
$$E = \frac{U}{2} = -\frac{K}{1}$$

where $K$ is kinetic energy.

### Example 4: Circular Orbit Speed

For gravitational force, find orbital speed for circular orbit at radius $r_0$.

**Solution:**

Centripetal force equals gravitational force:
$$\frac{mv^2}{r_0} = \frac{GMm}{r_0^2}$$

$$v^2 = \frac{GM}{r_0}$$

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_0}}$$

**Period:**
$$T = \frac{2\pi r_0}{v} = 2\pi r_0\sqrt{\frac{r_0}{GM}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r_0^3}{GM}}$$

This is Kepler's third law for circular orbits!

**For Earth orbit:** $GM_{\oplus} = gR^2 = 9.8(6.37 \times 10^6)^2 = 3.98 \times 10^{14}$ m³/s²

At $r_0 = R_{\oplus} + 400$ km $= 6.77 \times 10^6$ m:
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{3.98 \times 10^{14}}{6.77 \times 10^6}} = 7.67 \times 10^3 \text{ m/s} = 7.67 \text{ km/s}$$

**Answer:** $v = \sqrt{GM/r_0}$ (for Earth LEO: $\approx 7.7$ km/s)

## Orbit Classification by Energy

For effective potential with minimum $U_{eff,min}$:

**1. $E < U_{eff,min}$:** No motion possible (classically forbidden)

**2. $E = U_{eff,min}$:** Circular orbit at $r = r_0$

**3. $U_{eff,min} < E < 0$:** Bound orbit (ellipse)
   - Radial motion between $r_{min}$ and $r_{max}$ (turning points)
   - Perihelion: $r_{min}$ (closest approach)
   - Aphelion: $r_{max}$ (farthest point)

**4. $E = 0$:** Parabolic trajectory (marginally bound)
   - Escape velocity: just enough energy to reach $r = \infty$

**5. $E > 0$:** Unbound orbit (hyperbola)
   - Particle comes from infinity, scatters, returns to infinity

### Example 5: Orbit Type from Energy

A comet approaches Sun with speed $v_{\infty} = 10$ km/s at large distance. Mercury orbits at $r_M = 5.79 \times 10^{10}$ m with $v_M = 47.9$ km/s. Classify both orbits.

**Solution:**

Sun's gravitational parameter: $GM_{\odot}$ can be found from Mercury:
$$GM_{\odot} = v_M^2 r_M = (4.79 \times 10^4)^2(5.79 \times 10^{10}) = 1.33 \times 10^{20} \text{ m}^3\text{/s}^2$$

**Mercury (circular orbit):**
$$E_M = -\frac{GM_{\odot}m}{2r_M} = -\frac{1.33 \times 10^{20}m}{2(5.79 \times 10^{10})} < 0$$

Bound orbit (circular, $E = U_{eff,min}$)

**Comet:**
$$E_{comet} = \frac{1}{2}mv_{\infty}^2 = \frac{1}{2}m(10^4)^2 = 5 \times 10^7 m \text{ J/kg} > 0$$

Positive energy → unbound (hyperbolic) orbit

**Answer:** Mercury: circular (bound), Comet: hyperbolic (unbound)
