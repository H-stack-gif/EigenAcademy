# Article 4.5: Conservation of Angular Momentum

The conservation of angular momentum stands alongside energy and momentum conservation as one of the fundamental principles of physics. When the net external torque on a system vanishes, angular momentum remains constant—a principle with profound consequences. Ice skaters spin faster as they pull in their arms, planets sweep out equal areas in equal times, and gyroscopes maintain their orientation. These diverse phenomena all stem from the single mathematical statement: $\mathbf{L} = \text{constant}$ when $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{ext}} = \mathbf{0}$.

For central forces, where force points toward a fixed center, angular momentum conservation follows immediately: the torque vanishes because $\mathbf{r} \parallel \mathbf{F}$. This explains planetary motion—Kepler's second law is simply angular momentum conservation translated into geometry. In collisions and explosions, angular momentum about any fixed point is conserved if external torques are negligible. These conservation principles often provide the simplest route to solving rotational dynamics problems.

This article explores conservation of angular momentum across diverse applications: orbital mechanics, spinning systems, collisions involving rotation, gyroscopic effects, and precession. We emphasize both the vector nature of angular momentum—requiring component-wise conservation—and the connection to symmetries through Noether's theorem. The mathematics involves vector conservation equations, rotational kinematics, and geometric analysis of orbital motion.

## Conservation Law for Angular Momentum

**Theorem:** If total external torque is zero, angular momentum is conserved:
$$\text{If } \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{ext}} = \mathbf{0}, \text{ then } \frac{d\mathbf{L}}{dt} = \mathbf{0}, \text{ so } \mathbf{L} = \text{constant}$$

**Component-wise conservation:** Even if total torque is nonzero, angular momentum components may be conserved in directions where torque vanishes:
$$\text{If } \tau_z = 0, \text{ then } L_z = \text{constant}$$

**Examples of conserved angular momentum:**
- Central forces (gravity, electrostatics)
- Isolated systems (no external torques)
- Frictionless pivots or bearings
- Systems with axial symmetry

### Example 1: Ice Skater Spinning

An ice skater with moment of inertia $I_i = 3$ kg} \cdot \text {m² spins at $\omega_i = 2$ rad/s with arms extended. She pulls her arms in, reducing $I$ to $I_f = 1$ kg} \cdot \text {m². Find final angular velocity.

**Solution:**

Angular momentum: $L = I\omega$ (for rotation about fixed axis)

Conservation: $L_i = L_f$
$$I_i\omega_i = I_f\omega_f$$
$$3(2) = 1 \cdot \omega_f$$
$$\omega_f = 6 \text{ rad/s}$$

**Energy change:**
$$K_i = \frac{1}{2}I_i\omega_i^2 = \frac{1}{2}(3)(4) = 6 \text{ J}$$
$$K_f = \frac{1}{2}I_f\omega_f^2 = \frac{1}{2}(1)(36) = 18 \text{ J}$$

Energy increases by $12$ J—the skater does work pulling arms in against centrifugal effect.

**Answer:** $\omega_f = 6$ rad/s (triples), kinetic energy increases

## Central Forces and Kepler's Second Law

For central force $\mathbf{F} = f(r)\hat{\mathbf{r}}$:
$$\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{r} \times (f(r)\hat{\mathbf{r}}) = f(r)(\mathbf{r} \times \hat{\mathbf{r}}) = \mathbf{0}$$

Therefore $\mathbf{L} = $ constant.

**Consequences:**
1. **Planar motion:** Since $\mathbf{L} \perp \mathbf{r}$ always, and $\mathbf{L} =$ constant, motion is confined to plane perpendicular to $\mathbf{L}$.

2. **Areal velocity constant (Kepler's 2nd law):**

In polar coordinates: $\mathbf{L} = mr^2\dot{\theta}\hat{\mathbf{z}}$

$$L = mr^2\dot{\theta} = \text{constant}$$

**Area swept out:** In time $dt$, radius vector sweeps area:
$$dA = \frac{1}{2}r^2 d\theta$$

Areal velocity:
$$\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{1}{2}r^2\frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{1}{2}r^2\dot{\theta} = \frac{L}{2m} = \text{constant}$$

This is **Kepler's second law**: A planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times.

### Example 2: Elliptical Orbit Speeds

A planet orbits the Sun in an ellipse. At perihelion (closest approach) $r_p = 1.0$ AU with speed $v_p = 35$ km/s. At aphelion (farthest) $r_a = 1.5$ AU. Find speed at aphelion.

**Solution:**

At perihelion and aphelion, velocity is perpendicular to radius, so:
$$L_p = mr_pv_p, \quad L_a = mr_av_a$$

Conservation: $L_p = L_a$
$$mr_pv_p = mr_av_a$$
$$v_a = \frac{r_p}{r_a}v_p = \frac{1.0}{1.5}(35) = 23.3 \text{ km/s}$$

**Answer:** $v_a = 23.3$ km/s (slower at greater distance)

## Angular Momentum in Collisions

For collisions where external torques are negligible (or collision time is short), angular momentum about any point is conserved:
$$\mathbf{L}_{\text{before}} = \mathbf{L}_{\text{after}}$$

This adds constraints beyond linear momentum conservation.

### Example 3: Collision with Angular Momentum

A particle of mass $m = 2$ kg moving at $\mathbf{v} = 10\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ m/s strikes and sticks to a rod of length $L = 4$ m and mass $M = 6$ kg at its end. The rod is initially at rest and free to rotate about its center. Find the angular velocity after collision.

**Solution:**

**Angular momentum before collision** (about rod's center):

Particle is at distance $L/2 = 2$ m from pivot, moving perpendicular:
$$L_i = mvr = 2(10)(2) = 40 \text{ kg} \cdot \text {m}^2\text{/s}$$

Direction: $\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ (into page for given velocities)

**After collision:**

Combined system rotates with angular velocity $\omega$. Moment of inertia:
- Rod about center: $I_{\text{rod}} = \frac{1}{12}ML^2 = \frac{1}{12}(6)(16) = 8$ kg} \cdot \text {m²
- Particle at end: $I_{\text{particle}} = m(L/2)^2 = 2(4) = 8$ kg} \cdot \text {m²
- Total: $I = 16$ kg} \cdot \text {m²

Angular momentum after:
$$L_f = I\omega = 16\omega$$

Conservation:
$$40 = 16\omega$$
$$\omega = 2.5 \text{ rad/s}$$

**Answer:** $\omega = 2.5$ rad/s

### Example 4: Off-Center Collision

Two disks on a frictionless table: Disk 1 ($m_1 = 2$ kg, radius $r_1 = 0.2$ m) slides at $v_1 = 5$ m/s and strikes stationary Disk 2 ($m_2 = 3$ kg, radius $r_2 = 0.3$ m) at distance $d = 0.1$ m from its center. If they stick together, find their final velocities.

**Solution:**

**Linear momentum conservation:**
$$m_1 v_1 = (m_1 + m_2)v_{\text{CM}}$$
$$2(5) = 5v_{\text{CM}}$$
$$v_{\text{CM}} = 2 \text{ m/s}$$

**Angular momentum about CM of Disk 2:**

Initial: $L_i = m_1 v_1 d = 2(5)(0.1) = 1$ kg} \cdot \text {m²/s

Final: System rotates about combined CM. This requires knowing new CM position and combined moment of inertia—calculation becomes complex for off-center collision.

For simplicity, if assuming Disk 2's center as pivot:
$$L_f = I_2\omega$$

where $I_2 = \frac{1}{2}m_2 r_2^2 = \frac{1}{2}(3)(0.09) = 0.135$ kg} \cdot \text {m²

$$\omega = \frac{1}{0.135} = 7.4 \text{ rad/s}$$

**Answer:** $v_{\text{CM}} = 2$ m/s, $\omega \approx 7.4$ rad/s (simplified analysis)

## Gyroscopic Motion and Precession

A spinning gyroscope with angular momentum $\mathbf{L}$ experiences torque $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}_g$ (where $\mathbf{F}_g$ is gravity acting at CM).

Since $\boldsymbol{\tau} = d\mathbf{L}/dt$, the angular momentum vector changes direction, causing **precession**:
$$\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\text{prec}} = \frac{\boldsymbol{\tau}}{L}$$

The gyroscope axis traces a cone about the vertical.

**Precession angular velocity:**
$$\Omega = \frac{\tau}{L} = \frac{mgr}{I\omega}$$

where $r$ is distance from pivot to CM, $\omega$ is spin angular velocity.

### Example 5: Gyroscope Precession Rate

A gyroscope with moment of inertia $I = 0.01$ kg} \cdot \text {m², spinning at $\omega = 100$ rad/s, has mass $m = 0.5$ kg with CM at distance $r = 0.1$ m from pivot. Find precession rate.

**Solution:**

Spin angular momentum: $L = I\omega = 0.01(100) = 1$ kg} \cdot \text {m²/s

Gravitational torque: $\tau = mgr = 0.5(9.8)(0.1) = 0.49$ N} \cdot \text {m

Precession rate:
$$\Omega = \frac{\tau}{L} = \frac{0.49}{1} = 0.49 \text{ rad/s}$$

Period: $T = 2\pi/\Omega = 2\pi/0.49 = 12.8$ s

**Answer:** $\Omega = 0.49$ rad/s, period $12.8$ s

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**

$L = I\omega$ conserved. If $I_f = I_i/3$, then $\omega_f = 3\omega_i$ (increases by 3).

---

**Problem 2: C**

Equal areas in equal times ⟺ constant areal velocity ⟺ constant angular momentum.

---

**Problem 3: C**

Central forces produce zero torque, so angular momentum is conserved.

---

**Problem 4: C**

Both linear and angular momentum are conserved when respective external influences (force and torque) are negligible.

---

**Problem 5: B**

Precession occurs because gravitational torque $\boldsymbol{\tau} = d\mathbf{L}/dt$ changes direction of $\mathbf{L}$, not its magnitude.
