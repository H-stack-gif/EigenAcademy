# Article 2.4: Non-inertial Reference Frames

Most introductory physics assumes inertial reference frames—frames where Newton's laws hold in their simple form. But many practical situations involve non-inertial frames: rotating platforms, accelerating vehicles, and most importantly, the rotating Earth. In such frames, objects appear to experience forces that have no identifiable source in interactions with other matter. These **fictitious forces** or **pseudo-forces** arise purely from the acceleration of the reference frame itself.

Understanding non-inertial frames is essential for analyzing motion on Earth, where Coriolis effects deflect projectiles and air masses, creating weather patterns and influencing ballistics. Engineers designing centrifuges, rotating machinery, and amusement park rides must account for centrifugal forces. Physicists studying tidal forces recognize them as manifestations of non-uniform gravitational fields—equivalently, as fictitious forces in the non-inertial frame of an orbiting body.

This article systematically develops the transformation between inertial and non-inertial frames, derives the fictitious forces that appear in accelerating and rotating frames, and applies these concepts to physical systems. We emphasize that fictitious forces are real to observers in non-inertial frames—they produce measurable effects and must be included in force balances. The mathematical framework developed here prepares for advanced topics including rigid body dynamics, gyroscopes, and the equivalence principle in general relativity.

## Linearly Accelerating Reference Frames

Consider frame $S'$ accelerating with constant acceleration $\mathbf{A}$ relative to inertial frame $S$.

In frame $S$: $\sum \mathbf{F}_{\text{real}} = m\mathbf{a}$

In frame $S'$, the acceleration is: $\mathbf{a}' = \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{A}$

Rearranging:
$$m\mathbf{a}' = m\mathbf{a} - m\mathbf{A} = \sum \mathbf{F}_{\text{real}} - m\mathbf{A}$$

Define **fictitious force**: $\mathbf{F}_{\text{fict}} = -m\mathbf{A}$

Then in frame $S'$:
$$m\mathbf{a}' = \sum \mathbf{F}_{\text{real}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{fict}}$$

The fictitious force points opposite to the frame's acceleration and is proportional to mass (like gravity).

### Example 1: Pendulum in Accelerating Train

A pendulum of length $L = 1$ m hangs in a train accelerating at $a = 2$ m/s². Find the equilibrium angle.

**Solution:**

In the train's frame (non-inertial), there's a fictitious force $F_{\text{fict}} = ma$ pointing backward (opposite to acceleration).

At equilibrium, the bob is stationary in the train frame. Forces:
- Tension $T$ (along string at angle $\theta$ from vertical)
- Weight $mg$ (downward)
- Fictitious force $ma$ (horizontal, backward)

**Horizontal balance:**
$$T\sin\theta = ma$$

**Vertical balance:**
$$T\cos\theta = mg$$

Divide:
$$\tan\theta = \frac{a}{g} = \frac{2}{9.8} = 0.204$$
$$\theta = \arctan(0.204) = 11.5°$$

**Answer:** $\theta = 11.5°$ (tilted backward)

## Rotating Reference Frames

For a frame rotating with constant angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ relative to an inertial frame, the relationship between time derivatives is:
$$\left(\frac{d\mathbf{A}}{dt}\right)_{\text{inertial}} = \left(\frac{d\mathbf{A}}{dt}\right)_{\text{rotating}} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{A}$$

Applying this twice to position $\mathbf{r}$ gives the acceleration transformation:
$$\mathbf{a}_{\text{inertial}} = \mathbf{a}_{\text{rotating}} + 2\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}_{\text{rotating}} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r}) + \dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \times \mathbf{r}$$

For constant $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ (steady rotation):
$$\mathbf{a}_{\text{inertial}} = \mathbf{a}_{\text{rotating}} + 2\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}_{\text{rotating}} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r})$$

In the rotating frame, Newton's second law becomes:
$$m\mathbf{a}_{\text{rotating}} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{real}} - 2m\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}_{\text{rotating}} - m\boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r})$$

Define fictitious forces:
- **Coriolis force:** $\mathbf{F}_{\text{Cor}} = -2m\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}_{\text{rotating}}$
- **Centrifugal force:** $\mathbf{F}_{\text{cf}} = -m\boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r})$

The centrifugal force can be rewritten: For rotation about the $z$-axis, $\mathbf{F}_{\text{cf}} = m\omega^2\rho\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}$ where $\rho$ is perpendicular distance from axis.

## Centrifugal Force

The centrifugal force points radially outward from the rotation axis with magnitude:
$$F_{\text{cf}} = m\omega^2\rho$$

where $\rho$ is the perpendicular distance to the axis.

**Physical interpretation:** In the rotating frame, objects "feel" an outward force. This isn't a real interaction but rather the manifestation of inertia—the tendency to move in straight lines—viewed from a rotating perspective.

### Example 2: Water Surface in Rotating Bucket

A cylindrical bucket of radius $R = 0.2$ m rotates about its vertical axis at $\omega = 10$ rad/s. Describe the water surface shape.

**Solution:**

In the rotating frame, water surface is perpendicular to the effective gravity $\mathbf{g}_{\text{eff}} = \mathbf{g} + \mathbf{a}_{\text{fictitious}}$.

At radius $\rho$ from axis:
- Gravitational acceleration: $-g\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ (downward)
- Centrifugal acceleration: $\omega^2\rho\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}$ (outward)

The surface tilts at angle $\theta$ where:
$$\tan\theta = \frac{\omega^2\rho}{g}$$

This describes a parabola: $z(\rho) = \frac{\omega^2\rho^2}{2g} + C$

At $\rho = R = 0.2$ m:
$$z(R) - z(0) = \frac{\omega^2 R^2}{2g} = \frac{(10)^2(0.2)^2}{2(9.8)} = \frac{4}{19.6} = 0.204 \text{ m} = 20.4 \text{ cm}$$

The edge is 20.4 cm higher than the center.

**Answer:** Parabolic surface, edge 20.4 cm higher than center

## Coriolis Force

The Coriolis force is:
$$\mathbf{F}_{\text{Cor}} = -2m\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}$$

**Properties:**
- Perpendicular to both $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ and $\mathbf{v}$
- Depends on velocity (vanishes for stationary objects)
- Deflects moving objects sideways (not in direction of motion)
- In Northern Hemisphere: deflects to the right
- In Southern Hemisphere: deflects to the left

**Magnitude:** $F_{\text{Cor}} = 2m\omega v\sin\alpha$ where $\alpha$ is angle between $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ and $\mathbf{v}$.

### Example 3: Coriolis Deflection of Projectile

A projectile is fired northward at latitude $\lambda = 45°$ N with speed $v = 500$ m/s. Estimate the eastward deflection after traveling $d = 10$ km.

**Solution:**

Earth's angular velocity: $\omega = 7.3 \times 10^{-5}$ rad/s.

For northward motion at latitude $\lambda$, the Coriolis acceleration is approximately:
$$a_{\text{Cor}} = 2\omega v\sin\lambda = 2(7.3 \times 10^{-5})(500)\sin(45°) = 0.0516 \text{ m/s}^2$$

(eastward deflection)

Time to travel $d = 10$ km: $t = d/v = 10000/500 = 20$ s.

Eastward deflection:
$$x = \frac{1}{2}a_{\text{Cor}}t^2 = \frac{1}{2}(0.0516)(20)^2 = \frac{1}{2}(0.0516)(400) = 10.3 \text{ m}$$

**Answer:** Eastward deflection $\approx 10$ m

### Example 4: Cyclones and Weather Patterns

Explain why low-pressure systems rotate counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere.

**Solution:**

Air flows from high to low pressure. In the Northern Hemisphere:
- Air flowing toward a low-pressure center experiences Coriolis deflection to the right
- Air approaching from the north is deflected eastward (to the right)
- Air approaching from the south is deflected westward (to the right)
- Air approaching from the east is deflected southward (to the right)
- Air approaching from the west is deflected northward (to the right)

This systematic rightward deflection creates counterclockwise circulation around the low.

By Newton's third law analogue, high-pressure systems (air flowing outward) rotate clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere.

In the Southern Hemisphere, Coriolis deflection is to the left, reversing the rotation directions.

**Answer:** Coriolis deflection to right (N. Hemisphere) creates counterclockwise low-pressure circulation

### Example 5: Foucault Pendulum

A Foucault pendulum at latitude $\lambda$ has its plane of oscillation rotate. Find the rotation period.

**Solution:**

The Coriolis force on the pendulum bob causes the oscillation plane to precess.

The effective vertical component of Earth's rotation is:
$$\omega_{\text{eff}} = \omega\sin\lambda$$

The precession angular velocity equals this component:
$$\omega_{\text{precess}} = \omega\sin\lambda$$

Period:
$$T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_{\text{precess}}} = \frac{2\pi}{\omega\sin\lambda} = \frac{24 \text{ hr}}{\sin\lambda}$$

At North Pole ($\lambda = 90°$): $T = 24$ hr

At $\lambda = 30°$: $T = 24/0.5 = 48$ hr

At equator ($\lambda = 0°$): $T = \infty$ (no precession)

**Answer:** $T = 24 \text{ hr}/\sin\lambda$

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**

Apparent weight: $N = m(g + a) = 60(9.8 + 3) = 60(12.8) = 768$ N

---

**Problem 2: B**

Centrifugal acceleration: $a_{\text{cf}} = \omega^2r = (2)^2(1) = 4$ m/s²

---

**Problem 3: B**

Coriolis force depends on velocity: $\mathbf{F}_{\text{Cor}} = -2m\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}$. If $\mathbf{v} = 0$, then $\mathbf{F}_{\text{Cor}} = 0$.

---

**Problem 4: B**

At equator, Earth's rotation vector $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ is horizontal. For horizontal motion (velocity perpendicular to $\boldsymbol{\omega}$), the Coriolis force is vertical, not deflecting horizontal motion. The horizontal deflection is proportional to $\sin\lambda$, which is zero at equator.

---

**Problem 5: C**

$T = 24 \text{ hr}/\sin(60°) = 24/0.866 = 27.7 \approx 28$ hr
