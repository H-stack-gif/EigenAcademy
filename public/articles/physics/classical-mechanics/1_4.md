# Article 1.4: Relative Motion and Moving Reference Frames

Einstein taught us that the laws of physics should be the same in all inertial reference frames—frames moving at constant velocity relative to each other. But the Earth rotates, and so do merry-go-rounds, centrifuges, and weather systems. When we analyze motion from a rotating reference frame, Newton's second law $\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$ seems to fail: objects curve when no apparent force acts on them, and forces appear that have no source in interactions with other bodies. These are the fictitious forces—centrifugal and Coriolis—that arise purely from the non-inertial nature of our reference frame.

Yet these forces are not mere mathematical artifacts. To an observer rotating with a merry-go-round, the centrifugal force throwing them outward is viscerally real. To a meteorologist analyzing air currents, the Coriolis force deflecting winds and creating cyclonic rotation is an indispensable tool. The key is understanding that fictitious forces are coordinate effects: they represent the response of Newton's laws to acceleration of the reference frame itself. In the rotating frame, we can still use $\mathbf{F}_{\text{total}} = m\mathbf{a}$, but $\mathbf{F}_{\text{total}}$ now includes both real forces and fictitious contributions.

This article derives the relationship between time derivatives in inertial and rotating frames, develops the centrifugal and Coriolis forces, and applies them to motion on Earth. We see how the Coriolis force explains trade winds, why hurricanes rotate, and why free-fall trajectories are deflected. Understanding rotating frames is essential preparation for rigid body dynamics, gyroscopes, and precession—topics where rotation dominates the physics.

## Inertial vs. Non-Inertial Reference Frames

An **inertial reference frame** is one where Newton's first law holds: a free particle (no forces) moves with constant velocity. In such a frame, $\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$ with no extra terms.

A **non-inertial reference frame** is accelerating (linearly or rotationally) relative to an inertial frame. In such a frame, free particles appear to accelerate even when no forces act, requiring fictitious forces to preserve the form of Newton's laws.

Examples:
- Inertial: Frame attached to distant stars (approximately)
- Inertial: Frame moving at constant velocity relative to stars
- Non-inertial: Frame rotating with Earth
- Non-inertial: Accelerating car, elevator, or rocket

## Time Derivatives in Rotating Frames

Consider an inertial frame $S$ and a rotating frame $S'$ with angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\omega}$. For any vector $\mathbf{A}$, the time derivatives in the two frames are related by:
$$\left(\frac{d\mathbf{A}}{dt}\right)_S = \left(\frac{d\mathbf{A}}{dt}\right)_{S'} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{A}$$

This says: the rate of change seen in the inertial frame equals the rate seen in the rotating frame plus a correction $\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{A}$ due to rotation of the basis vectors.

Applying this to position $\mathbf{r}$:
$$\mathbf{v}_S = \mathbf{v}_{S'} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r}$$

where $\mathbf{v}_S$ is velocity in inertial frame and $\mathbf{v}_{S'}$ is velocity in rotating frame.

Applying again to velocity:
$$\mathbf{a}_S = \mathbf{a}_{S'} + 2\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}_{S'} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r}) + \dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \times \mathbf{r}$$

For constant $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ (steady rotation):
$$\mathbf{a}_S = \mathbf{a}_{S'} + 2\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}_{S'} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r})$$

### Example 1: Rotating Unit Vector

Consider unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ in polar coordinates rotating with angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\omega} = \dot{\theta}\hat{\mathbf{z}}$. Verify that $d\hat{\mathbf{r}}/dt = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \hat{\mathbf{r}}$.

**Solution:** We have $\boldsymbol{\omega} = \dot{\theta}\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \cos\theta\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \sin\theta\hat{\mathbf{y}}$.

$$\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \hat{\mathbf{r}} = \dot{\theta}\hat{\mathbf{z}} \times (\cos\theta\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \sin\theta\hat{\mathbf{y}})$$
$$= \dot{\theta}\cos\theta(\hat{\mathbf{z}} \times \hat{\mathbf{x}}) + \dot{\theta}\sin\theta(\hat{\mathbf{z}} \times \hat{\mathbf{y}})$$
$$= \dot{\theta}\cos\theta\hat{\mathbf{y}} + \dot{\theta}\sin\theta(-\hat{\mathbf{x}})$$
$$= -\dot{\theta}\sin\theta\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \dot{\theta}\cos\theta\hat{\mathbf{y}}$$

From calculus:
$$\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}(\cos\theta\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \sin\theta\hat{\mathbf{y}}) = -\dot{\theta}\sin\theta\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \dot{\theta}\cos\theta\hat{\mathbf{y}}$$

These match, confirming $d\hat{\mathbf{r}}/dt = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \hat{\mathbf{r}}$.

**Answer:** Verified: $d\hat{\mathbf{r}}/dt = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \hat{\mathbf{r}} = \dot{\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$

## Centrifugal Force

In the rotating frame, Newton's second law becomes:
$$\mathbf{F}_{\text{real}} = m\mathbf{a}_{S'}  + 2m\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}_{S'} + m\boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r})$$

Rearranging:
$$m\mathbf{a}_{S'} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{real}} - 2m\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}_{S'} - m\boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r})$$

The last two terms are fictitious forces:

**Centrifugal force:** $\mathbf{F}_{\text{centrifugal}} = -m\boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r})$

This can be rewritten. For rotation about $z$-axis with $\boldsymbol{\omega} = \omega\hat{\mathbf{z}}$:
$$\boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r}) = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\omega\hat{\mathbf{z}} \times (x\hat{\mathbf{x}} + y\hat{\mathbf{y}} + z\hat{\mathbf{z}}))$$
$$= \omega\boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\omega y\hat{\mathbf{x}} - \omega x\hat{\mathbf{y}})$$
$$= -\omega^2 x\hat{\mathbf{x}} - \omega^2 y\hat{\mathbf{y}}$$

So: $\mathbf{F}_{\text{centrifugal}} = m\omega^2 x\hat{\mathbf{x}} + m\omega^2 y\hat{\mathbf{y}} = m\omega^2\mathbf{r}_\perp$

where $\mathbf{r}_\perp$ is the perpendicular distance from the rotation axis. The centrifugal force points **outward**, perpendicular to the axis.

### Example 2: Mass on Rotating Table

A mass $m = 2$ kg sits at distance $r = 0.5$ m from the center of a rotating table with $\omega = 4$ rad/s. What centrifugal force does it experience in the rotating frame?

**Solution:** The centrifugal force magnitude is:
$$F_{\text{centrifugal}} = m\omega^2 r = (2)(16)(0.5) = 16 \text{ N}$$

Direction: radially outward from the rotation axis.

In the rotating frame, this force is needed to keep the mass stationary. In the inertial frame, there's no such force—instead, a real inward force (friction or tension) provides the centripetal acceleration $a_c = \omega^2 r$ to keep the mass in circular motion.

**Answer:** $F_{\text{centrifugal}} = 16$ N, radially outward

## Coriolis Force

**Coriolis force:** $\mathbf{F}_{\text{Coriolis}} = -2m\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}_{S'}$

This force acts on particles moving in the rotating frame, perpendicular to both the angular velocity and the velocity. For $\boldsymbol{\omega} = \omega\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ and velocity $\mathbf{v} = v_x\hat{\mathbf{x}} + v_y\hat{\mathbf{y}}$:
$$\mathbf{F}_{\text{Coriolis}} = -2m\omega\hat{\mathbf{z}} \times (v_x\hat{\mathbf{x}} + v_y\hat{\mathbf{y}}) = -2m\omega(v_x\hat{\mathbf{y}} - v_y\hat{\mathbf{x}})$$
$$= 2m\omega v_y\hat{\mathbf{x}} - 2m\omega v_x\hat{\mathbf{y}}$$

In the Northern Hemisphere (where $\omega > 0$ looking down), the Coriolis force deflects moving objects to the right of their direction of motion. In the Southern Hemisphere, it deflects to the left.

### Example 3: Coriolis Deflection on Earth

A projectile is fired northward at latitude $\lambda = 45°$ N with speed $v = 100$ m/s. Estimate the Coriolis deflection after $t = 10$ s.

**Solution:** Earth's angular velocity: $\omega = 2\pi/(24 \times 3600) \approx 7.3 \times 10^{-5}$ rad/s.

The component of $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ perpendicular to the surface at latitude $\lambda$ is $\omega\sin\lambda$.

For northward velocity, the Coriolis acceleration is eastward with magnitude:
$$a_{\text{Cor}} = 2\omega v\sin\lambda = 2(7.3 \times 10^{-5})(100)\sin(45°) = 2(7.3 \times 10^{-5})(100)(0.707)$$
$$\approx 0.0103 \text{ m/s}^2$$

After $t = 10$ s:
$$\Delta x = \frac{1}{2}a_{\text{Cor}}t^2 = \frac{1}{2}(0.0103)(100) \approx 0.52 \text{ m}$$

The projectile is deflected about half a meter to the east.

**Answer:** Eastward deflection $\approx 0.5$ m

### Example 4: Foucault Pendulum

A Foucault pendulum at the North Pole ($\lambda = 90°$) has its plane of oscillation rotate. How long does it take for one complete rotation in the rotating (Earth) frame?

**Solution:** At the North Pole, $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ points vertically upward. The Coriolis force on the pendulum bob causes the plane of oscillation to precess.

The angular velocity of precession equals the vertical component of Earth's rotation:
$$\omega_{\text{precess}} = \omega\sin\lambda = \omega\sin(90°) = \omega$$

Period: $T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = 24$ hours.

At other latitudes: $T = \frac{24 \text{ hr}}{\sin\lambda}$.

At $\lambda = 45°$: $T = 24/\sin(45°) = 24/0.707 \approx 34$ hours.

**Answer:** At North Pole: $T = 24$ hours

### Example 5: Trade Winds and Cyclones

Explain qualitatively how the Coriolis force affects large-scale air circulation and creates rotating cyclones.

**Solution:** Air flows from high pressure to low pressure regions. In the Northern Hemisphere, the Coriolis force deflects this motion to the right:
- Air flowing toward equator (southward): deflected westward → easterly trade winds
- Air flowing toward poles (northward): deflected eastward → westerly winds at mid-latitudes

For a low-pressure center (cyclone):
- Air flows inward toward the low
- Coriolis force deflects it to the right (Northern Hemisphere)
- This creates counterclockwise rotation around the low

For a high-pressure center (anticyclone):
- Air flows outward from the high
- Deflection creates clockwise rotation

In the Southern Hemisphere, deflection is to the left, reversing the rotation directions.

This explains why hurricanes rotate counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere.

**Answer:** Coriolis force deflects air currents, creating counterclockwise cyclones (Northern Hemisphere) and clockwise anticyclones, explaining trade winds and hurricane rotation.

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**

Centrifugal acceleration: $a_{\text{centrifugal}} = \omega^2 r = (0.5)^2(3) = 0.25(3) = 0.75$ m/s².

---

**Problem 2: B**

Coriolis acceleration magnitude: $a_{\text{Cor}} = 2\omega v = 2(0.5)(1) = 1.0$ m/s².

Direction: tangential (perpendicular to both $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ and $\mathbf{v}$).

---

**Problem 3: B**

In the Northern Hemisphere, the Coriolis force deflects objects to the right of their direction of motion.

---

**Problem 4: A**

At rest in rotating frame: $\mathbf{v}_{S'} = \mathbf{0}$, so Coriolis force $\mathbf{F}_{\text{Cor}} = -2m\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}_{S'} = \mathbf{0}$.

But centrifugal force $\mathbf{F}_{\text{centrifugal}} = -m\boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r}) \neq \mathbf{0}$ (unless at axis).

---

**Problem 5: D**

At latitude $\lambda$, the Foucault pendulum period is:
$$T = \frac{24 \text{ hr}}{\sin\lambda} = \frac{24}{\sin(30°)} = \frac{24}{0.5} = 48 \text{ hours}$$
