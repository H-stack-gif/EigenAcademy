# Article 4.1: Linear Momentum

Linear momentum represents one of the most fundamental quantities in physics—more fundamental, in some sense, than force itself. While forces describe interactions, momentum describes the state of motion. Newton's second law, originally formulated as $\mathbf{F} = d\mathbf{p}/dt$ rather than $\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$, reveals momentum as the primary quantity whose rate of change defines force. When no external forces act, momentum is conserved—a principle that holds not just in classical mechanics but in all of physics, from quantum mechanics to relativistic field theory.

The conservation of momentum follows from Newton's third law and the principle of translational invariance (by Noether's theorem). For isolated systems, the total momentum remains constant regardless of internal interactions. This makes momentum conservation a powerful tool: we can predict final states without solving the detailed dynamics. Collisions, explosions, and rocket propulsion all yield to momentum analysis even when forces are complex or unknown. The impulse-momentum theorem connects momentum change to the time integral of force, providing yet another route to solving dynamics problems.

This article develops linear momentum systematically from single particles to systems, derives the impulse-momentum theorem, establishes conservation laws, and explores applications including variable mass systems. We emphasize both the vector nature of momentum—requiring component-wise analysis—and the scalar quantity impulse. The mathematical framework employs vector calculus, integration, and systems of differential equations, while physical applications range from collisions to rocket propulsion.

## Definition and Properties

For a particle of mass $m$ with velocity $\mathbf{v}$, **linear momentum** is:
$$\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v}$$

**Units:** kg} \cdot \text {m/s (no special name)

**Newton's Second Law (original form):**
$$\mathbf{F} = \frac{d\mathbf{p}}{dt}$$

For constant mass:
$$\mathbf{F} = \frac{d(m\mathbf{v})}{dt} = m\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = m\mathbf{a}$$

For variable mass:
$$\mathbf{F} = \frac{dm}{dt}\mathbf{v} + m\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}$$

**System of particles:** Total momentum is the vector sum:
$$\mathbf{P} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{p}_i = \sum_{i=1}^{N} m_i\mathbf{v}_i$$

### Example 1: Momentum Calculation in 2D

Two particles move in a plane: $m_1 = 2$ kg at $\mathbf{v}_1 = 3\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 4\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ m/s, and $m_2 = 3$ kg at $\mathbf{v}_2 = -2\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \hat{\mathbf{y}}$ m/s. Find the total momentum magnitude and direction.

**Solution:**

Individual momenta:
$$\mathbf{p}_1 = m_1\mathbf{v}_1 = 2(3\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 4\hat{\mathbf{y}}) = 6\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 8\hat{\mathbf{y}} \text{ kg} \cdot \text {m/s}$$

$$\mathbf{p}_2 = m_2\mathbf{v}_2 = 3(-2\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \hat{\mathbf{y}}) = -6\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 3\hat{\mathbf{y}} \text{ kg } \cdot \text { m/s}$$

Total momentum:
$$\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{p}_1 + \mathbf{p}_2 = (6-6)\hat{\mathbf{x}} + (8+3)\hat{\mathbf{y}} = 11\hat{\mathbf{y}} \text{ kg } \cdot \text { m/s}$$

Magnitude: $|\mathbf{P}| = 11$ kg} \cdot \text {m/s

Direction: Pure $+y$ direction ($\theta = 90°$)

**Answer:** $\mathbf{P} = 11\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ kg} \cdot \text {m/s, magnitude 11 kg} \cdot \text {m/s

## Impulse-Momentum Theorem

**Impulse** is the time integral of force:
$$\mathbf{J} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \mathbf{F}(t) \, dt$$

**Impulse-Momentum Theorem:** Impulse equals change in momentum:
$$\mathbf{J} = \Delta\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{p}_f - \mathbf{p}_i$$

**Derivation:** From $\mathbf{F} = d\mathbf{p}/dt$:
$$\int_{t_1}^{t_2} \mathbf{F} \, dt = \int_{\mathbf{p}_i}^{\mathbf{p}_f} d\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{p}_f - \mathbf{p}_i$$

**Average force:** If impulse $\mathbf{J}$ is known:
$$\mathbf{F}_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\mathbf{J}}{\Delta t} = \frac{\Delta\mathbf{p}}{\Delta t}$$

### Example 2: Impulse from Variable Force

A force $\mathbf{F}(t) = (6t)\hat{\mathbf{x}} + (3t^2)\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ N acts on a 2 kg mass from $t = 0$ to $t = 2$ s. Find the impulse and final velocity if initially at rest.

**Solution:**

Impulse components:
$$J_x = \int_0^2 6t \, dt = 3t^2\Big|_0^2 = 12 \text{ N} \cdot \text {s}$$

$$J_y = \int_0^2 3t^2 \, dt = t^3\Big|_0^2 = 8 \text{ N} \cdot \text {s}$$

$$\mathbf{J} = 12\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 8\hat{\mathbf{y}} \text{ N} \cdot \text {s}$$

Initial momentum: $\mathbf{p}_i = \mathbf{0}$

Final momentum: $\mathbf{p}_f = \mathbf{p}_i + \mathbf{J} = 12\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 8\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ kg} \cdot \text {m/s

Final velocity:
$$\mathbf{v}_f = \frac{\mathbf{p}_f}{m} = \frac{12\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 8\hat{\mathbf{y}}}{2} = 6\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 4\hat{\mathbf{y}} \text{ m/s}$$

Magnitude: $|\mathbf{v}_f| = \sqrt{36 + 16} = \sqrt{52} = 2\sqrt{13}$ m/s $\approx 7.21$ m/s

**Answer:** $\mathbf{J} = 12\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 8\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ N} \cdot \text {s, $\mathbf{v}_f = 6\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 4\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ m/s

## Conservation of Linear Momentum

For a system of particles, the equation of motion for total momentum is:
$$\frac{d\mathbf{P}}{dt} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}$$

where $\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}$ is the total external force. Internal forces cancel by Newton's third law.

**Derivation:** For particle $i$:
$$\frac{d\mathbf{p}_i}{dt} = \mathbf{F}_i^{\text{ext}} + \sum_{j \neq i} \mathbf{F}_{ji}$$

Sum over all particles:
$$\frac{d\mathbf{P}}{dt} = \sum_i \frac{d\mathbf{p}_i}{dt} = \sum_i \mathbf{F}_i^{\text{ext}} + \sum_i \sum_{j \neq i} \mathbf{F}_{ji}$$

The double sum includes each pair twice: $\mathbf{F}_{ji} + \mathbf{F}_{ij} = \mathbf{0}$ (Newton's third law).

Therefore:
$$\frac{d\mathbf{P}}{dt} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}$$

**Conservation:** If $\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} = \mathbf{0}$, then $\mathbf{P} = \text{constant}$.

**Component-wise conservation:** Even if total external force is nonzero, momentum may be conserved in directions where external force vanishes.

### Example 3: Explosion in Two Dimensions

A stationary object of mass $M = 6$ kg explodes into three fragments: $m_1 = 2$ kg moves at $\mathbf{v}_1 = 10\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ m/s, $m_2 = 1$ kg moves at $\mathbf{v}_2 = 20\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ m/s. Find the velocity of the third fragment ($m_3 = 3$ kg).

**Solution:**

Initial momentum: $\mathbf{P}_i = \mathbf{0}$ (at rest)

Final momentum: $\mathbf{P}_f = m_1\mathbf{v}_1 + m_2\mathbf{v}_2 + m_3\mathbf{v}_3$

Conservation: $\mathbf{P}_f = \mathbf{P}_i = \mathbf{0}$

$$2(10\hat{\mathbf{x}}) + 1(20\hat{\mathbf{y}}) + 3\mathbf{v}_3 = \mathbf{0}$$

$$20\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 20\hat{\mathbf{y}} + 3\mathbf{v}_3 = \mathbf{0}$$

$$\mathbf{v}_3 = -\frac{20\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 20\hat{\mathbf{y}}}{3} = -\frac{20}{3}\hat{\mathbf{x}} - \frac{20}{3}\hat{\mathbf{y}} \text{ m/s}$$

$$\mathbf{v}_3 = -6.67\hat{\mathbf{x}} - 6.67\hat{\mathbf{y}} \text{ m/s}$$

Magnitude: $|\mathbf{v}_3| = \frac{20}{3}\sqrt{2} = \frac{20\sqrt{2}}{3} \approx 9.43$ m/s

Direction: $225°$ from $+x$ axis (third quadrant)

**Answer:** $\mathbf{v}_3 = -6.67\hat{\mathbf{x}} - 6.67\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ m/s

## Variable Mass Systems

For systems where mass changes (rockets, conveyor belts), Newton's second law becomes:
$$\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} = \frac{d\mathbf{p}}{dt} = \frac{d(m\mathbf{v})}{dt} = m\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} + \mathbf{v}\frac{dm}{dt}$$

Rearranging:
$$m\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} - \mathbf{v}\frac{dm}{dt}$$

For a rocket ejecting mass at velocity $\mathbf{u}_{\text{rel}}$ relative to the rocket:
$$\mathbf{v}_{\text{exhaust}} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}_{\text{rel}}$$

The momentum of ejected mass:
$$d\mathbf{p}_{\text{exhaust}} = (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}_{\text{rel}})(-dm)$$

(Negative because rocket loses mass)

Rocket equation:
$$m\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} - \mathbf{u}_{\text{rel}}\frac{dm}{dt}$$

Define **thrust**: $\mathbf{F}_{\text{thrust}} = -\mathbf{u}_{\text{rel}}\frac{dm}{dt} = v_{\text{ex}}\frac{dm}{dt}$

(where $v_{\text{ex}} = |\mathbf{u}_{\text{rel}}|$ is exhaust speed)

### Example 4: Tsiolkovsky Rocket Equation

A rocket in deep space (no external forces) has initial mass $m_0$ and ejects exhaust at constant speed $v_{\text{ex}}$ relative to itself. Derive the final velocity after burning fuel mass $\Delta m$.

**Solution:**

No external forces: $\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} = \mathbf{0}$

Rocket equation in one dimension:
$$m\frac{dv}{dt} = -v_{\text{ex}}\frac{dm}{dt}$$

Separate variables:
$$m \, dv = -v_{\text{ex}} \, dm$$

$$dv = -v_{\text{ex}}\frac{dm}{m}$$

Integrate from initial state $(m_0, v_0)$ to final state $(m_f, v_f)$:
$$\int_{v_0}^{v_f} dv = -v_{\text{ex}}\int_{m_0}^{m_f} \frac{dm}{m}$$

$$v_f - v_0 = -v_{\text{ex}}[\ln m]_{m_0}^{m_f} = -v_{\text{ex}}(\ln m_f - \ln m_0)$$

$$\Delta v = v_f - v_0 = v_{\text{ex}}\ln\left(\frac{m_0}{m_f}\right)$$

This is the **Tsiolkovsky rocket equation**.

**Numerical example:** $m_0 = 1000$ kg, $m_f = 400$ kg (burned 600 kg fuel), $v_{\text{ex}} = 2000$ m/s, $v_0 = 0$:

$$\Delta v = 2000\ln(1000/400) = 2000\ln(2.5) = 2000(0.916) = 1832 \text{ m/s}$$

**Answer:** $\Delta v = v_{\text{ex}}\ln(m_0/m_f)$ (Tsiolkovsky equation)

### Example 5: Multi-Stage Rocket

A two-stage rocket has: Stage 1 with $m_{\text{fuel,1}} = 800$ kg, $m_{\text{struct,1}} = 200$ kg; Stage 2 with $m_{\text{fuel,2}} = 150$ kg, $m_{\text{struct,2}} = 50$ kg; payload $m_p = 100$ kg. Exhaust speed $v_{\text{ex}} = 2500$ m/s for both stages. Find total $\Delta v$.

**Solution:**

**Stage 1:**
- Initial mass: $m_{0,1} = 800 + 200 + 150 + 50 + 100 = 1300$ kg
- Final mass (after fuel burned): $m_{f,1} = 200 + 150 + 50 + 100 = 500$ kg
- Then jettison stage 1 structure: $m_{0,2} = 150 + 50 + 100 = 300$ kg

$$\Delta v_1 = v_{\text{ex}}\ln(1300/500) = 2500\ln(2.6) = 2500(0.956) = 2390 \text{ m/s}$$

**Stage 2:**
- Initial mass: $m_{0,2} = 300$ kg
- Final mass: $m_{f,2} = 50 + 100 = 150$ kg

$$\Delta v_2 = v_{\text{ex}}\ln(300/150) = 2500\ln(2) = 2500(0.693) = 1733 \text{ m/s}$$

**Total:**
$$\Delta v_{\text{total}} = \Delta v_1 + \Delta v_2 = 2390 + 1733 = 4123 \text{ m/s}$$

**Answer:** Total $\Delta v = 4123$ m/s (achievable with staging)
