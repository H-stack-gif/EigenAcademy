# Article 4.2: Center of Mass

The center of mass represents the average position of a system's mass, weighted by each particle's mass. For extended objects or multi-particle systems, the center of mass moves as if all mass were concentrated there and all external forces acted there. This remarkable simplification allows us to separate complicated internal motions from overall translational motion. A spinning, tumbling object follows a parabolic trajectory—not each individual piece, but its center of mass.

Mathematically, the center of mass is defined through a vector sum (discrete masses) or vector integral (continuous distributions). The power of this concept lies in the theorem that the center of mass obeys $M\mathbf{a}_{\text{CM}} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}$, where $M$ is total mass. Internal forces—no matter how complex—don't affect center of mass motion. This separates the problem: solve for center of mass motion using external forces, then analyze motion relative to the center of mass separately.

This article develops the center of mass concept rigorously, derives its equation of motion, introduces the center of mass reference frame, and applies these ideas to multi-particle systems and continuous distributions. The mathematics involves vector operations, multiple integrals for continuous systems, and coordinate transformations. Applications range from finding balance points to analyzing complex collisions in the CM frame.

## Definition of Center of Mass

For discrete particles, the **center of mass position** is:
$$\mathbf{r}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N} m_i\mathbf{r}_i$$

where $M = \sum m_i$ is total mass.

In component form:
$$x_{\text{CM}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum m_i x_i, \quad y_{\text{CM}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum m_i y_i, \quad z_{\text{CM}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum m_i z_i$$

For continuous distributions:
$$\mathbf{r}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{1}{M}\int \mathbf{r} \, dm$$

where $dm = \rho dV$ (volume), $dm = \sigma dA$ (surface), or $dm = \lambda ds$ (line).

### Example 1: Center of Mass of Three Particles

Find the center of mass of three particles: $m_1 = 2$ kg at $(1, 0, 0)$ m, $m_2 = 3$ kg at $(0, 2, 0)$ m, $m_3 = 5$ kg at $(0, 0, 1)$ m.

**Solution:**

Total mass: $M = 2 + 3 + 5 = 10$ kg

$$x_{\text{CM}} = \frac{2(1) + 3(0) + 5(0)}{10} = \frac{2}{10} = 0.2 \text{ m}$$

$$y_{\text{CM}} = \frac{2(0) + 3(2) + 5(0)}{10} = \frac{6}{10} = 0.6 \text{ m}$$

$$z_{\text{CM}} = \frac{2(0) + 3(0) + 5(1)}{10} = \frac{5}{10} = 0.5 \text{ m}$$

$$\mathbf{r}_{\text{CM}} = (0.2, 0.6, 0.5) \text{ m}$$

**Answer:** $\mathbf{r}_{\text{CM}} = 0.2\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 0.6\hat{\mathbf{y}} + 0.5\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ m

## Motion of the Center of Mass

**Center of mass velocity:**
$$\mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{d\mathbf{r}_{\text{CM}}}{dt} = \frac{1}{M}\sum m_i\mathbf{v}_i = \frac{\mathbf{P}}{M}$$

**Center of mass acceleration:**
$$\mathbf{a}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{d\mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}}}{dt} = \frac{1}{M}\frac{d\mathbf{P}}{dt}$$

**Theorem (CM equation of motion):**
$$M\mathbf{a}_{\text{CM}} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}$$

**Proof:** From $d\mathbf{P}/dt = \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}$ and $\mathbf{P} = M\mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}}$:
$$M\frac{d\mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}}}{dt} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}$$

This is Newton's second law for the center of mass, treating it as a point particle of mass $M$.

### Example 2: CM Motion in Explosion

A 10 kg object at rest explodes into two fragments: 6 kg moves at $5\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ m/s. Describe the CM motion.

**Solution:**

Before explosion: $\mathbf{v}_{\text{CM},i} = \mathbf{0}$ (system at rest)

No external forces (explosion is internal): $\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} = \mathbf{0}$

Therefore: $\mathbf{a}_{\text{CM}} = \mathbf{0}$, so $\mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}} = \text{constant} = \mathbf{0}$

The center of mass remains stationary despite the explosion!

**Verify:** Fragment 2 has mass $m_2 = 4$ kg. By momentum conservation:
$$6(5\hat{\mathbf{x}}) + 4\mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{0}$$
$$\mathbf{v}_2 = -7.5\hat{\mathbf{x}} \text{ m/s}$$

CM velocity:
$$\mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{6(5\hat{\mathbf{x}}) + 4(-7.5\hat{\mathbf{x}})}{10} = \frac{30 - 30}{10}\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{0}$$ ✓

**Answer:** CM remains stationary (no external forces)

## Center of Mass for Continuous Distributions

For continuous body with density $\rho(\mathbf{r})$:
$$\mathbf{r}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{1}{M}\int_V \mathbf{r} \rho(\mathbf{r}) \, dV$$

where $M = \int_V \rho \, dV$.

For symmetric objects with uniform density, CM is at geometric center.

### Example 3: CM of Triangular Plate

Find the center of mass of a uniform triangular plate with vertices at $(0,0)$, $(L,0)$, and $(0,L)$.

**Solution:**

For uniform density $\sigma$ (mass per area), use:
$$x_{\text{CM}} = \frac{\int x \, dA}{\int dA}, \quad y_{\text{CM}} = \frac{\int y \, dA}{\int dA}$$

**Area element:** For triangle, use vertical strips. At position $x$, the height is $h(x) = L - x$.

$$dA = h(x) \, dx = (L-x) \, dx$$

**Total area:**
$$A = \int_0^L (L-x) \, dx = \left[Lx - \frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^L = L^2 - \frac{L^2}{2} = \frac{L^2}{2}$$

**$x$-coordinate:**
$$\int x \, dA = \int_0^L x(L-x) \, dx = \int_0^L (Lx - x^2) \, dx = \left[\frac{Lx^2}{2} - \frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^L$$
$$= \frac{L^3}{2} - \frac{L^3}{3} = \frac{L^3}{6}$$

$$x_{\text{CM}} = \frac{L^3/6}{L^2/2} = \frac{L}{3}$$

By symmetry (or similar calculation): $y_{\text{CM}} = L/3$

**Answer:** $\mathbf{r}_{\text{CM}} = (L/3, L/3)$ (centroid at one-third from each leg)

### Example 4: CM of Hemisphere

Find the center of mass of a uniform solid hemisphere of radius $R$.

**Solution:**

Use spherical coordinates with flat face in $xy$-plane, curved surface extending in $+z$ direction.

By symmetry: $x_{\text{CM}} = y_{\text{CM}} = 0$

For $z_{\text{CM}}$:
$$z_{\text{CM}} = \frac{\int z \, dV}{\int dV}$$

**Volume element:** $dV = r^2\sin\theta \, dr \, d\theta \, d\phi$

**Limits:** $0 \leq r \leq R$, $0 \leq \theta \leq \pi/2$, $0 \leq \phi \leq 2\pi$

In spherical coordinates: $z = r\cos\theta$

**Numerator:**
$$\int z \, dV = \int_0^{2\pi} d\phi \int_0^{\pi/2} \int_0^R (r\cos\theta) r^2\sin\theta \, dr \, d\theta$$

$$= 2\pi \int_0^R r^3 dr \int_0^{\pi/2} \cos\theta\sin\theta \, d\theta$$

$$= 2\pi \left[\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^R \left[\frac{\sin^2\theta}{2}\right]_0^{\pi/2}$$

$$= 2\pi \cdot \frac{R^4}{4} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{\pi R^4}{4}$$

**Denominator (hemisphere volume):**
$$V = \frac{2}{3}\pi R^3$$

**Result:**
$$z_{\text{CM}} = \frac{\pi R^4/4}{2\pi R^3/3} = \frac{\pi R^4}{4} \cdot \frac{3}{2\pi R^3} = \frac{3R}{8}$$

**Answer:** $z_{\text{CM}} = 3R/8$ above flat face

## Center of Mass Reference Frame

The **center of mass frame** (CM frame) is a reference frame moving with velocity $\mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}}$.

In this frame, the total momentum is zero:
$$\mathbf{P}_{\text{CM}} = \sum m_i\mathbf{v}_i' = \mathbf{0}$$

where $\mathbf{v}_i' = \mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}}$ is velocity relative to CM frame.

**Proof:**
$$\mathbf{P}_{\text{CM}} = \sum m_i(\mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}}) = \sum m_i\mathbf{v}_i - M\mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}} = \mathbf{P} - M\mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}} = \mathbf{P} - \mathbf{P} = \mathbf{0}$$

**Kinetic energy decomposition:**
$$K_{\text{total}} = \frac{1}{2}M v_{\text{CM}}^2 + K_{\text{rel}}$$

where $K_{\text{rel}} = \frac{1}{2}\sum m_i v_i'^2$ is kinetic energy relative to CM.

### Example 5: Two-Particle System in CM Frame

Two particles: $m_1 = 3$ kg at $\mathbf{v}_1 = 10\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ m/s, $m_2 = 2$ kg at $\mathbf{v}_2 = -15\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ m/s. Find velocities in CM frame and the kinetic energy decomposition.

**Solution:**

CM velocity:
$$\mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{m_1\mathbf{v}_1 + m_2\mathbf{v}_2}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{3(10) + 2(-15)}{5}\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{30-30}{5}\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{0}$$

Velocities in CM frame:
$$\mathbf{v}_1' = \mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}} = 10\hat{\mathbf{x}} \text{ m/s}$$
$$\mathbf{v}_2' = \mathbf{v}_2 - \mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}} = -15\hat{\mathbf{x}} \text{ m/s}$$

(In this case, CM frame is same as lab frame since $\mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}} = \mathbf{0}$)

**Kinetic energies:**
$$K_{\text{CM}} = \frac{1}{2}M v_{\text{CM}}^2 = 0$$

$$K_{\text{rel}} = \frac{1}{2}(3)(10)^2 + \frac{1}{2}(2)(15)^2 = 150 + 225 = 375 \text{ J}$$

$$K_{\text{total}} = 0 + 375 = 375 \text{ J}$$

**Answer:** $\mathbf{v}_1' = 10\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ m/s, $\mathbf{v}_2' = -15\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ m/s, $K_{\text{total}} = 375$ J

**Problem 3:** If no external forces act, which statement is TRUE?

A) $\mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}}$ is constant

B) $\mathbf{r}_{\text{CM}}$ is constant

C) Internal forces change $\mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}}$

D) Explosions accelerate CM

E) CM velocity increases

---

**Problem 4:** In the CM frame, the total momentum is:

A) Maximum

B) Minimum

C) Zero

D) Equal to kinetic energy

E) Undefined

---

**Problem 5:** A uniform semicircular disk (radius $R$) has CM distance from center:

A) $R/2$

B) $2R/3$

C) $3R/8$

D) $4R/(3\pi)$

E) $R/\pi$

---

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**

For equal masses at symmetric positions, CM is at geometric center (centroid).

---

**Problem 2: C**

For uniform rod, CM is at geometric center: $L/2$ from either end.

---

**Problem 3: A**

$M\mathbf{a}_{\text{CM}} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}$. If $\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} = \mathbf{0}$, then $\mathbf{a}_{\text{CM}} = \mathbf{0}$, so $\mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}} = $ constant.

---

**Problem 4: C**

By definition, CM frame moves with $\mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}}$, so total momentum in this frame is zero.

---

**Problem 5: D**

For uniform semicircular disk: $y_{\text{CM}} = 4R/(3\pi)$ from center along symmetry axis.
