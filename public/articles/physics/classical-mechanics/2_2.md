# Article 2.2: Common Force Laws

Newton's second law provides the framework for dynamics, but to predict motion we need specific force laws. These laws describe how forces depend on properties like position, velocity, mass, charge, and material constants. Some forces—like gravity and springs—are conservative and derivable from potential energy. Others—like friction and drag—dissipate energy. Understanding these force laws allows us to model real physical systems, from planetary orbits to damped oscillators to terminal velocity of falling objects.

The force laws presented here represent empirical observations refined over centuries. The universal law of gravitation emerged from Kepler's astronomical data and Newton's theoretical insight. Hooke's law for springs arose from experimental observations of elastic materials. Friction laws remain phenomenological, lacking a complete microscopic theory but invaluable for engineering applications. These forces form the vocabulary of classical mechanics, appearing repeatedly across all applications.

This article systematically develops the most important force laws in classical mechanics. We derive their mathematical forms, explore their physical origins where possible, and emphasize the regimes where each law applies. Mastery of these forces—their vector nature, their dependencies, and their limitations—is essential for solving realistic problems and for appreciating when more sophisticated models (like field theories or quantum mechanics) become necessary.

## Gravitational Force

**Universal Law of Gravitation (Newton):** Two point masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ separated by distance $r$ experience attractive forces:
$$\mathbf{F}_{12} = -G\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{12}$$

where $G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}$ N·m²/kg² is the gravitational constant, and $\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{12}$ points from mass 1 to mass 2.

**Properties:**
- Always attractive (negative sign indicates attraction)
- Falls off as $1/r^2$ (inverse-square law)
- Conservative force: $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U$ with $U = -Gm_1m_2/r$
- Obeys superposition: total force is vector sum of individual forces

**Near Earth's surface:** For objects at height $h \ll R_E$ (Earth's radius):
$$F_g = \frac{GMm}{(R_E + h)^2} \approx \frac{GMm}{R_E^2} = mg$$

where $g = GM/R_E^2 = 9.8$ m/s² is the gravitational acceleration. The weight is $\mathbf{F}_g = -mg\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ (taking $z$ upward).

### Example 1: Gravitational Force Between Two Objects

Two spheres of masses $m_1 = 5$ kg and $m_2 = 10$ kg are separated by $r = 0.5$ m. Find the gravitational force between them.

**Solution:** Apply Newton's law of gravitation:
$$F = G\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} = (6.674 \times 10^{-11})\frac{(5)(10)}{(0.5)^2}$$
$$= (6.674 \times 10^{-11})\frac{50}{0.25} = (6.674 \times 10^{-11})(200) = 1.33 \times 10^{-8} \text{ N}$$

This is an extremely weak force! Gravity between everyday objects is negligible compared to electromagnetic forces.

**Answer:** $F = 1.33 \times 10^{-8}$ N (attractive)

## Spring Forces (Hooke's Law)

**Hooke's Law:** An ideal spring exerts a restoring force proportional to displacement from equilibrium:
$$\mathbf{F} = -k\mathbf{x}$$

where $k$ is the **spring constant** (units: N/m), and $\mathbf{x}$ is displacement from the natural length.

For a spring along the $x$-axis with natural length $L_0$:
$$F_x = -k(x - L_0)$$

**Properties:**
- Restoring force: always points toward equilibrium
- Conservative: $U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$ (taking $U = 0$ at equilibrium)
- Linear regime: Hooke's law applies for small displacements
- Real springs: deviate from Hooke's law for large stretches

**Energy storage:** Springs store elastic potential energy $U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$ when compressed or stretched.

### Example 2: Spring-Mass System

A mass $m = 0.5$ kg hangs from a spring with $k = 100$ N/m. Find the equilibrium stretch and the frequency of oscillation.

**Solution:** At equilibrium, spring force balances weight:
$$kx_{eq} = mg \implies x_{eq} = \frac{mg}{k} = \frac{(0.5)(9.8)}{100} = \frac{4.9}{100} = 0.049 \text{ m} = 4.9 \text{ cm}$$

For small oscillations about equilibrium, the equation of motion is:
$$m\ddot{x} = -kx$$

This gives simple harmonic motion with angular frequency:
$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{100}{0.5}} = \sqrt{200} = 14.1 \text{ rad/s}$$

Frequency: $f = \omega/(2\pi) = 14.1/(2\pi) = 2.25$ Hz.

**Answer:** $x_{eq} = 4.9$ cm, $f = 2.25$ Hz

## Friction Forces

**Static Friction:** Opposes the *tendency* to slide. Adjusts to prevent motion up to a maximum:
$$f_s \leq \mu_s N$$

where $\mu_s$ is the coefficient of static friction and $N$ is the normal force.

**Kinetic Friction:** Opposes sliding motion with (approximately) constant magnitude:
$$f_k = \mu_k N$$

where $\mu_k$ is the coefficient of kinetic friction. Typically $\mu_k < \mu_s$.

**Properties:**
- Direction: opposite to velocity (kinetic) or opposing tendency to move (static)
- Magnitude: proportional to normal force, independent of contact area
- Non-conservative: dissipates energy as heat
- Phenomenological: microscopic origins complex (adhesion, surface roughness)

**Important:** $f_s$ is not always $\mu_s N$; that's the *maximum*. The actual static friction adjusts to keep the object stationary, up to this limit.

### Example 3: Block on Incline with Friction

A block of mass $m = 5$ kg rests on an incline at $\theta = 30°$ with $\mu_s = 0.5$. Does the block slip?

**Solution:** 

Forces parallel to incline:
- Gravity component: $F_{\parallel} = mg\sin\theta = 5(9.8)\sin(30°) = 24.5$ N (down incline)
- Maximum static friction: $f_{s,\max} = \mu_s N = \mu_s mg\cos\theta = 0.5(5)(9.8)\cos(30°) = 21.2$ N (up incline)

Since $F_{\parallel} = 24.5$ N $> f_{s,\max} = 21.2$ N, the block **will slip**.

If it slips, kinetic friction acts: $f_k = \mu_k N$. Assuming $\mu_k = 0.4$:
$$f_k = 0.4(5)(9.8)\cos(30°) = 17.0 \text{ N}$$

Net force: $F_{\text{net}} = mg\sin\theta - f_k = 24.5 - 17.0 = 7.5$ N

Acceleration: $a = F_{\text{net}}/m = 7.5/5 = 1.5$ m/s² (down incline)

**Answer:** Block slips with $a = 1.5$ m/s²

## Tension and Normal Forces

**Tension ($T$):** Force transmitted through a rope, string, or cable. For massless, inextensible strings:
- Tension is the same throughout the string
- Tension adjusts to maintain constraint (e.g., constant length)
- Tension always pulls (never pushes)

**Normal Force ($N$):** Contact force perpendicular to a surface. 
- Adjusts to prevent interpenetration of objects
- Not always equal to weight! (e.g., on inclines, in elevators)
- Can be found using $\sum F_{\perp} = ma_{\perp}$

### Example 4: Atwood's Machine

Two masses $m_1 = 2$ kg and $m_2 = 3$ kg hang from a pulley via a massless string. Find the acceleration and tension.

**Solution:** 

Let $a$ be the magnitude of acceleration ($m_2$ accelerates down, $m_1$ up).

For $m_1$ (upward positive): $T - m_1g = m_1 a$

For $m_2$ (downward positive): $m_2g - T = m_2 a$

Add equations:
$$(m_2 - m_1)g = (m_1 + m_2)a$$
$$a = \frac{(m_2 - m_1)g}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{(3-2)(9.8)}{5} = \frac{9.8}{5} = 1.96 \text{ m/s}^2$$

From first equation:
$$T = m_1(g + a) = 2(9.8 + 1.96) = 2(11.76) = 23.5 \text{ N}$$

Check: $m_2g - T = 3(9.8) - 23.5 = 29.4 - 23.5 = 5.9 = 3(1.96)$ ✓

**Answer:** $a = 1.96$ m/s², $T = 23.5$ N

## Air Resistance and Drag Forces

**Linear drag (low velocity):** $\mathbf{F}_{\text{drag}} = -b\mathbf{v}$

where $b$ is the drag coefficient (units: kg/s).

**Quadratic drag (high velocity):** $\mathbf{F}_{\text{drag}} = -\frac{1}{2}\rho C_d A v^2\hat{\mathbf{v}}$

where:
- $\rho$ is fluid density
- $C_d$ is drag coefficient (dimensionless, depends on shape)
- $A$ is cross-sectional area
- $\hat{\mathbf{v}}$ is direction of velocity

**Terminal velocity:** When drag balances weight, acceleration becomes zero:

For linear drag: $v_{\text{term}} = mg/b$

For quadratic drag: $v_{\text{term}} = \sqrt{2mg/(\rho C_d A)}$

### Example 5: Terminal Velocity of a Falling Sphere

A sphere of mass $m = 0.1$ kg, radius $r = 0.05$ m falls through air ($\rho = 1.2$ kg/m³) with $C_d = 0.5$. Find terminal velocity.

**Solution:** At terminal velocity, drag balances weight:
$$\frac{1}{2}\rho C_d A v_{\text{term}}^2 = mg$$

Cross-sectional area: $A = \pi r^2 = \pi(0.05)^2 = 7.85 \times 10^{-3}$ m²

Solve for $v_{\text{term}}$:
$$v_{\text{term}} = \sqrt{\frac{2mg}{\rho C_d A}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(0.1)(9.8)}{(1.2)(0.5)(7.85 \times 10^{-3})}}$$
$$= \sqrt{\frac{1.96}{4.71 \times 10^{-3}}} = \sqrt{416} = 20.4 \text{ m/s}$$

**Answer:** $v_{\text{term}} = 20.4$ m/s $\approx 73$ km/h
