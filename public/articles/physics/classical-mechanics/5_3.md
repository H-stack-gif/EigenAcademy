# Article 5.3: Driven Oscillations and Resonance

When an external periodic force drives an oscillator, the system exhibits rich dynamical behavior. The steady-state response depends critically on the driving frequency: near the natural frequency $\omega_0$, the amplitude becomes very large—resonance. This phenomenon appears throughout physics: mechanical resonances can destroy bridges, electrical resonances tune radios, and quantum resonances enable spectroscopy. Understanding driven oscillators requires distinguishing transient behavior (initial response) from steady-state motion (long-time limit after transients decay).

The mathematics of driven oscillators introduces inhomogeneous differential equations whose solutions combine homogeneous (transient) and particular (steady-state) parts. The steady-state amplitude and phase depend on driving frequency through resonance curves. At resonance, the amplitude is limited only by damping—zero damping would yield infinite amplitude. The power absorbed from the driving force peaks sharply at resonance, with width determined by the quality factor $Q$.

This article develops driven harmonic oscillators systematically: deriving the differential equation, solving for transient and steady-state responses, analyzing amplitude and phase as functions of frequency, exploring resonance phenomena, and calculating power absorption. The mathematics employs complex exponentials to simplify calculations, parametric resonance curves, and energy transfer analysis. Applications span mechanical systems, electrical circuits, and forced vibrations.

## Equation of Motion

For a damped oscillator driven by external force $F(t) = F_0\cos(\omega t)$:
$$m\ddot{x} + b\dot{x} + kx = F_0\cos(\omega t)$$

Dividing by $m$:
$$\ddot{x} + 2\gamma\dot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = f_0\cos(\omega t)$$

where $f_0 = F_0/m$ is force per unit mass.

**General solution:** $x(t) = x_h(t) + x_p(t)$
- $x_h(t)$: homogeneous solution (transient, decays as $e^{-\gamma t}$)
- $x_p(t)$: particular solution (steady-state, oscillates at driving frequency $\omega$)

For long times ($t \gg 1/\gamma$), transient decays, leaving only steady-state.

## Steady-State Solution Using Complex Notation

Write driving force as: $F(t) = \text{Re}[F_0 e^{i\omega t}]$

Seek solution: $x(t) = \text{Re}[\tilde{x} e^{i\omega t}]$ where $\tilde{x}$ is complex amplitude.

Substitute into ODE:
$$(-\omega^2 + 2i\gamma\omega + \omega_0^2)\tilde{x} e^{i\omega t} = f_0 e^{i\omega t}$$

$$\tilde{x} = \frac{f_0}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2) + 2i\gamma\omega}$$

**Magnitude:**
$$|\tilde{x}| = A(\omega) = \frac{f_0}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + (2\gamma\omega)^2}}$$

**Phase:**
$$\tan\delta = \frac{2\gamma\omega}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2}$$

where $\delta$ is phase lag of response behind driving force.

**Steady-state solution:**
$$x_{ss}(t) = A(\omega)\cos(\omega t - \delta)$$

### Example 1: Steady-State Amplitude

For $m = 2$ kg, $k = 50$ N/m, $b = 4$ kg/s, driven by $F_0 = 10$ N at frequency $\omega = 5$ rad/s, find steady-state amplitude.

**Solution:**

Parameters:
$$\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{50/2} = 5 \text{ rad/s}$$
$$\gamma = b/(2m) = 4/4 = 1 \text{ s}^{-1}$$
$$f_0 = F_0/m = 10/2 = 5 \text{ m/s}^2$$

Amplitude:
$$A = \frac{f_0}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + (2\gamma\omega)^2}}$$

At $\omega = \omega_0 = 5$ rad/s (resonance):
$$A = \frac{5}{\sqrt{0 + (2 \cdot 1 \cdot 5)^2}} = \frac{5}{10} = 0.5 \text{ m}$$

**Answer:** $A = 0.5$ m at resonance

## Resonance: Amplitude vs. Frequency

**Resonance occurs** when amplitude is maximum. Find $dA/d\omega = 0$:

Maximum amplitude at:
$$\omega_{\text{res}} = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2\gamma^2}$$

For weak damping ($\gamma \ll \omega_0$): $\omega_{\text{res}} \approx \omega_0$

**Maximum amplitude:**
$$A_{\max} = \frac{f_0}{2\gamma\omega_0} = \frac{f_0}{\omega_0} \cdot \frac{Q}{1}$$

where $Q = \omega_0/(2\gamma)$.

**Resonance behavior:**
- High $Q$ → sharp resonance peak, large amplitude
- Low $Q$ → broad resonance, smaller amplitude
- At resonance, response lags driving force by $\delta = 90°$

### Example 2: Resonance Peak

For oscillator with $\omega_0 = 10$ rad/s, $Q = 20$, and $f_0 = 5$ m/s², find: (a) resonance frequency, (b) maximum amplitude, (c) amplitude at $\omega = \omega_0 \pm \gamma$.

**Solution:**

From $Q = 20$: $\gamma = \omega_0/(2Q) = 10/40 = 0.25$ s⁻¹

**(a) Resonance frequency:**
$$\omega_{\text{res}} = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2\gamma^2} = \sqrt{100 - 2(0.0625)} = \sqrt{99.875} \approx 9.99 \text{ rad/s}$$

For high $Q$, essentially $\omega_{\text{res}} \approx \omega_0$.

**(b) Maximum amplitude:**
$$A_{\max} = \frac{f_0}{2\gamma\omega_0} = \frac{5}{2(0.25)(10)} = \frac{5}{5} = 1 \text{ m}$$

**(c) At $\omega = \omega_0 \pm \gamma = 10 \pm 0.25$:**

At $\omega = 10.25$ rad/s:
$$A = \frac{5}{\sqrt{(100-105.06)^2 + (2 \cdot 0.25 \cdot 10.25)^2}} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{25.6 + 26.3}} = \frac{5}{7.2} = 0.69 \text{ m}$$

This is approximately $A_{\max}/\sqrt{2} = 0.707$ m (half-power point).

**Answer:** (a) $\omega_{\text{res}} \approx 10$ rad/s, (b) $A_{\max} = 1$ m, (c) $A \approx 0.7$ m

## Phase Response

Phase lag $\delta$ varies with frequency:
$$\tan\delta = \frac{2\gamma\omega}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2}$$

**Behavior:**
- $\omega \ll \omega_0$: $\delta \approx 0$ (in phase with driving force)
- $\omega = \omega_0$: $\delta = 90°$ (quarter cycle lag)
- $\omega \gg \omega_0$: $\delta \approx 180°$ (out of phase)

**Physical interpretation:**
- Below resonance: oscillator follows driving force nearly in phase
- At resonance: maximum energy transfer, $90°$ lag
- Above resonance: oscillator opposes driving force

### Example 3: Phase at Different Frequencies

For $\omega_0 = 8$ rad/s and $\gamma = 1$ s⁻¹, find phase lag at: (a) $\omega = 4$ rad/s, (b) $\omega = 8$ rad/s, (c) $\omega = 12$ rad/s.

**Solution:**

**(a) $\omega = 4$ rad/s:**
$$\tan\delta = \frac{2(1)(4)}{64-16} = \frac{8}{48} = 0.167$$
$$\delta = \arctan(0.167) = 9.5°$$

**(b) $\omega = 8$ rad/s (resonance):**
$$\tan\delta = \frac{2(1)(8)}{64-64} = \frac{16}{0} = \infty$$
$$\delta = 90°$$

**(c) $\omega = 12$ rad/s:**
$$\tan\delta = \frac{2(1)(12)}{64-144} = \frac{24}{-80} = -0.3$$

Since denominator is negative and numerator positive, $\delta > 90°$:
$$\delta = 180° - \arctan(0.3) = 180° - 16.7° = 163.3°$$

**Answer:** (a) $9.5°$, (b) $90°$, (c) $163°$

## Power Absorption and Resonance Width

**Instantaneous power** delivered by driving force:
$$P(t) = F(t) \cdot v(t) = F_0\cos(\omega t) \cdot \dot{x}(t)$$

**Time-averaged power:**
$$\langle P \rangle = \frac{1}{2}F_0\omega A\sin\delta = \frac{f_0^2 m\gamma\omega^2}{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2 + (2\gamma\omega)^2}$$

**Maximum power at resonance** ($\omega = \omega_0$):
$$\langle P \rangle_{\max} = \frac{f_0^2 m}{4\gamma}$$

**Half-power points:** Where $\langle P \rangle = \langle P \rangle_{\max}/2$

For high $Q$, these occur at:
$$\omega_{\pm} = \omega_0 \pm \gamma$$

**Resonance width (FWHM):**
$$\Delta\omega = \omega_+ - \omega_- = 2\gamma = \frac{\omega_0}{Q}$$

### Example 4: Power and Bandwidth

For oscillator with $\omega_0 = 100$ rad/s and $Q = 50$, find the resonance width in rad/s and in Hz.

**Solution:**

Damping coefficient:
$$\gamma = \frac{\omega_0}{2Q} = \frac{100}{100} = 1 \text{ s}^{-1}$$

Resonance width:
$$\Delta\omega = 2\gamma = 2 \text{ rad/s}$$

In Hz:
$$\Delta f = \frac{\Delta\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{2}{2\pi} = \frac{1}{\pi} \approx 0.318 \text{ Hz}$$

**Relation to Q:**
$$\frac{\Delta f}{f_0} = \frac{1}{Q}$$

where $f_0 = \omega_0/(2\pi) = 100/(2\pi) = 15.9$ Hz.

$$\Delta f = \frac{f_0}{Q} = \frac{15.9}{50} = 0.318 \text{ Hz}$$ ✓

**Answer:** $\Delta\omega = 2$ rad/s, $\Delta f \approx 0.32$ Hz

### Example 5: Transient and Steady-State

An underdamped oscillator ($\omega_0 = 5$ rad/s, $\gamma = 0.5$ s⁻¹) is driven at $\omega = 5$ rad/s starting from rest. Describe the motion.

**Solution:**

**Transient:** Homogeneous solution decays as $e^{-\gamma t}$:
$$x_h(t) = e^{-0.5t}[C\cos(\omega_d t) + D\sin(\omega_d t)]$$

where $\omega_d = \sqrt{25-0.25} = 4.97$ rad/s $\approx 5$ rad/s.

Time scale for transient decay: $\tau = 1/\gamma = 2$ s.

**Steady-state:** At resonance ($\omega = \omega_0$):
$$x_{ss}(t) = A\cos(5t - 90°) = A\sin(5t)$$

**Total motion:** For $t < \tau$: both transient and steady-state present, resulting in "beats" or amplitude modulation.

For $t \gg \tau$: pure sinusoidal steady-state at driving frequency.

**Answer:** Transient decays in $\sim 2$ s, then steady oscillation at driving frequency with $90°$ phase lag
