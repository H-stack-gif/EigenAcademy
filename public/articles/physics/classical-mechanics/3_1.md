# Article 3.1: Work and Kinetic Energy

The concept of energy transforms classical mechanics from a purely force-based description to a powerful scalar formulation. Where Newton's second law requires tracking vector forces and accelerations, energy methods reduce many problems to scalar equations. The work-energy theorem connects the integral of force along a path to the change in kinetic energy, providing an alternative route to solving dynamics problems. This is particularly valuable when forces are conservative, where energy conservation replaces differential equations with algebraic constraints.

The mathematical foundation of work rests on line integrals from vector calculus. Work is not simply force times distance—it's the integral $W = \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ along the actual path taken. This integral structure immediately raises questions: Does the path matter? When can we evaluate the integral using only endpoints? The answers lead to the distinction between conservative and non-conservative forces, one of the most fundamental classifications in physics.

This article develops work and kinetic energy with mathematical rigor. We derive the work-energy theorem from Newton's second law, explore work calculations in multiple coordinate systems, and establish the relationship between power and force. The techniques developed here—parametrizing paths, evaluating line integrals, expressing work in generalized coordinates—prepare for Lagrangian mechanics, where energy takes center stage as the fundamental quantity from which equations of motion are derived.

## Work as a Line Integral

For a particle moving along path $C$ from point A to point B under force $\mathbf{F}$, the work done is:
$$W = \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$$

Parametrizing the path by $\mathbf{r}(t)$ with $t \in [t_A, t_B]$:
$$W = \int_{t_A}^{t_B} \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) \cdot \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} dt = \int_{t_A}^{t_B} \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) \cdot \mathbf{v}(t) \, dt$$

In Cartesian coordinates:
$$W = \int_C (F_x dx + F_y dy + F_z dz)$$

**Physical interpretation:** Work measures the energy transferred to the particle by the force along its trajectory. Positive work increases kinetic energy; negative work decreases it.

**Units:** Joule (J) = N} \cdot \text {m = kg} \cdot \text {m²/s²

### Example 1: Work Along a Curved Path

A force $\mathbf{F} = (2xy)\hat{\mathbf{x}} + (x^2 + 2y)\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ acts on a particle moving along the parabola $y = x^2$ from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$. Calculate the work done.

**Solution:** Parametrize the path: $x = t$, $y = t^2$ with $t \in [0,1]$.

Then: $dx = dt$, $dy = 2t\,dt$

Substitute into work integral:
$$W = \int_0^1 [F_x dx + F_y dy] = \int_0^1 [(2xy)dx + (x^2 + 2y)dy]$$

$$= \int_0^1 [2t \cdot t^2 \cdot dt + (t^2 + 2t^2) \cdot 2t\,dt]$$

$$= \int_0^1 [2t^3 + 6t^3] dt = \int_0^1 8t^3 dt = 2t^4\Big|_0^1 = 2 \text{ J}$$

**Answer:** $W = 2$ J

## Kinetic Energy and the Work-Energy Theorem

Define **kinetic energy**:
$$K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2}m\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}$$

**Work-Energy Theorem:** The net work done on a particle equals the change in its kinetic energy:
$$W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K = K_f - K_i$$

**Derivation:** Start with Newton's second law:
$$\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a} = m\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}$$

Take dot product with velocity:
$$\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v} = m\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} \cdot \mathbf{v}$$

Note that:
$$\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}m\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}\right) = m\mathbf{v} \cdot \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}$$

Therefore:
$$\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \frac{dK}{dt}$$

Integrate from $t_A$ to $t_B$:
$$\int_{t_A}^{t_B} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v} \, dt = \int_{K_i}^{K_f} dK = K_f - K_i$$

But the left side is precisely the work integral:
$$W = \int_{t_A}^{t_B} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v} \, dt = \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$$

Thus: $W = \Delta K$ ∎

### Example 2: Projectile Energy Analysis

A projectile of mass $m = 2$ kg is launched at angle $\theta = 45°$ with initial speed $v_0 = 20$ m/s. Find the kinetic energy at the highest point using the work-energy theorem.

**Solution:** 

Initial kinetic energy:
$$K_i = \frac{1}{2}mv_0^2 = \frac{1}{2}(2)(20)^2 = 400 \text{ J}$$

At highest point, only horizontal component of velocity remains:
$$v_x = v_0\cos\theta = 20\cos(45°) = 20/\sqrt{2} = 14.14 \text{ m/s}$$

Kinetic energy at top:
$$K_f = \frac{1}{2}m v_x^2 = \frac{1}{2}(2)(14.14)^2 = 200 \text{ J}$$

**Alternatively, using work-energy theorem:**

The only force doing work is gravity (air resistance neglected). Maximum height:
$$h = \frac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta}{2g} = \frac{400 \cdot 0.5}{19.6} = 10.2 \text{ m}$$

Work by gravity:
$$W_g = -mgh = -2(9.8)(10.2) = -200 \text{ J}$$

By work-energy theorem:
$$K_f = K_i + W_g = 400 - 200 = 200 \text{ J}$$ ✓

**Answer:** $K_f = 200$ J

## Power: Rate of Energy Transfer

**Power** is the rate at which work is done:
$$P = \frac{dW}{dt} = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v}$$

**Units:** Watt (W) = J/s = kg} \cdot \text {m²/s³

For constant power $P$, the work done in time $\Delta t$ is:
$$W = P \Delta t$$

For variable power:
$$W = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} P(t) \, dt = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v} \, dt$$

### Example 3: Power Delivered by Engine

A car of mass $m = 1500$ kg accelerates from rest with constant power $P = 30$ kW. Find velocity as a function of time.

**Solution:** From $P = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v} = Fv$ (one dimension):
$$P = m\frac{dv}{dt} \cdot v = mv\frac{dv}{dt}$$

Separate variables:
$$P \, dt = mv \, dv$$

Integrate:
$$Pt = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - 0$$

$$v(t) = \sqrt{\frac{2Pt}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(30000)t}{1500}} = \sqrt{40t}$$

At $t = 10$ s:
$$v = \sqrt{400} = 20 \text{ m/s} = 72 \text{ km/h}$$

**Answer:** $v(t) = \sqrt{2Pt/m} = \sqrt{40t}$ m/s

## Work in Curvilinear Coordinates

### Polar Coordinates

In polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$, the displacement is:
$$d\mathbf{r} = dr\hat{\mathbf{r}} + r d\theta\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$$

Force components: $\mathbf{F} = F_r\hat{\mathbf{r}} + F_\theta\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$

Work element:
$$dW = \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = F_r dr + F_\theta r d\theta$$

Total work:
$$W = \int (F_r dr + F_\theta r d\theta)$$

### Example 4: Work in Polar Coordinates

A particle moves in a spiral path $r = a\theta$ (where $a = 1$ m/rad) from $\theta = 0$ to $\theta = 2\pi$ under force $\mathbf{F} = (k/r^2)\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ with $k = 10$ N} \cdot \text {m². Calculate work done.

**Solution:** Since $F_\theta = 0$ and $F_r = k/r^2$:
$$W = \int_0^{2\pi} F_r dr = \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{k}{r^2} dr$$

From $r = a\theta$: $dr = a d\theta$, and when $\theta$ goes from 0 to $2\pi$, $r$ goes from 0 to $2\pi a$.

$$W = \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{k}{(a\theta)^2} a d\theta = \frac{k}{a} \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{d\theta}{\theta^2}$$

This integral diverges at $\theta = 0$! The force is too singular near the origin.

**Better approach:** Start from $\theta = \theta_0$ (small but nonzero):
$$W = \frac{k}{a} \int_{\theta_0}^{2\pi} \frac{d\theta}{\theta^2} = \frac{k}{a}\left[-\frac{1}{\theta}\right]_{\theta_0}^{2\pi} = \frac{k}{a}\left(-\frac{1}{2\pi} + \frac{1}{\theta_0}\right)$$

For $\theta_0 = \pi/6$: 
$$W = \frac{10}{1}\left(-\frac{1}{2\pi} + \frac{6}{\pi}\right) = 10\left(\frac{6}{\pi} - \frac{1}{2\pi}\right) = 10 \cdot \frac{11}{2\pi} = \frac{55}{\pi} \approx 17.5 \text{ J}$$

**Answer:** $W = \frac{k}{a}\left(\frac{1}{\theta_0} - \frac{1}{2\pi}\right)$ (depends on starting point)

### Example 5: Gravitational Work in Spherical Coordinates

A satellite of mass $m = 1000$ kg moves from $r_1 = 2R_E$ to $r_2 = 3R_E$ (where $R_E = 6.37 \times 10^6$ m). Calculate work by gravity.

**Solution:** Gravitational force: $F_r = -\frac{GMm}{r^2}$

Work:

$For motion from $r_1$ to $r_2$ with $r_2 > r_1$, and $F_r < 0$ (attractive), the work is negative:
$$W = -GMm\left(\frac{1}{r_2} - \frac{1}{r_1}\right) = -1.04 \times 10^{10} \text{ J}$$

**Answer:** $W = -1.04 \times 10^{10}$ J (gravity does negative work)

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**

$$W = \int_0^2 F_x dx = \int_0^2 3x^2 dx = x^3\Big|_0^2 = 8 \text{ J}$$

---

**Problem 2: B**

Initial kinetic energy: $K_i = \frac{1}{2}(5)(10)^2 = 250$ J

Final kinetic energy: $K_f = 0$

Work: $W = \Delta K = 0 - 250 = -250$ J

---

**Problem 3: B**

$W = P\Delta t = 20000 \cdot 5 = 100000$ J $= 100$ kJ

---

**Problem 4: C**

Centripetal force is perpendicular to velocity, so $\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$ always, giving $W = 0$.

---

**Problem 5: B**

$$dW = F_\theta r d\theta \implies W = F_\theta r \Delta\theta$$

(assuming $r$ is constant or using average $r$)
