# Article 6.2: Kepler's Laws

Kepler's three laws, derived empirically from Tycho Brahe's planetary observations, describe orbital motion with geometric precision. Newton later showed these laws follow mathematically from the inverse-square gravitational force and his laws of motion. Kepler's laws represent one of physics' great unifications: apparently mysterious celestial motions emerge as consequences of universal gravitation. The first law (elliptical orbits) follows from solving the orbital equation; the second law (equal areas) is angular momentum conservation translated into geometry; the third law (period-radius relation) connects orbital parameters through gravitational constant.

The mathematical derivation requires solving a second-order nonlinear differential equation—the orbital equation—which yields conic sections as solutions. The orbit's shape (ellipse, parabola, hyperbola) depends on total energy and angular momentum. For bound elliptical orbits, the semi-major axis determines the orbital period through Kepler's third law, enabling mass determinations for binary star systems, exoplanets, and galactic dynamics.

This article derives Kepler's three laws rigorously from Newtonian mechanics, solves the orbital equation to obtain conic sections, introduces orbital elements for characterizing orbits, and applies these results to planetary systems, satellites, and binary stars. The mathematics employs differential equations, conic section geometry, and conservation laws. Applications emphasize astronomical observations and spacecraft mission design.

## Kepler's First Law: Elliptical Orbits

**Statement:** Planets move in ellipses with the Sun at one focus.

**Derivation from Newton's laws:**

Start with equation of motion in polar coordinates for inverse-square force:
$$\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2 = -\frac{k}{mr^2}$$

Using $L = mr^2\dot{\theta}$ (constant):
$$\ddot{r} - \frac{L^2}{m^2r^3} = -\frac{k}{mr^2}$$

Make substitution $u = 1/r$, so $r = 1/u$ and:
$$\frac{dr}{d\theta} = \frac{dr}{dt}\frac{dt}{d\theta} = \dot{r}\frac{mr^2}{L} = -\frac{L}{m}\frac{du}{d\theta}$$

$$\frac{d^2r}{d\theta^2} = -\frac{L}{m}\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2}$$

Also: $\ddot{r} = \frac{d\dot{r}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\left(-\frac{L}{m}\frac{du}{d\theta}\right) = -\frac{L}{m}\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2}\dot{\theta} = -\frac{L^2}{m^2r^2}\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} = -\frac{L^2u^2}{m^2}\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2}$

Substituting into equation of motion:
$$-\frac{L^2u^2}{m^2}\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} - \frac{L^2u^3}{m^2} = -\frac{ku^2}{m}$$

$$\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} + u = \frac{km}{L^2}$$

This is a linear ODE with constant coefficients!

**General solution:**
$$u = \frac{km}{L^2} + A\cos(\theta - \theta_0)$$

Choose $\theta_0 = 0$ (defines axis):
$$u = \frac{1}{r} = \frac{km}{L^2}(1 + e\cos\theta)$$

where $e = AL^2/(km)$ is **eccentricity**.

**Inverting:**
$$r(\theta) = \frac{L^2/(km)}{1 + e\cos\theta} = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos\theta}$$

where $p = L^2/(km)$ is **semi-latus rectum**.

This is the **polar equation of a conic section** with eccentricity $e$:
- $e = 0$: circle
- $0 < e < 1$: ellipse
- $e = 1$: parabola
- $e > 1$: hyperbola

### Example 1: Orbital Equation

A planet has perihelion distance $r_{min} = a(1-e)$ and aphelion $r_{max} = a(1+e)$ where $a$ is semi-major axis. Show this satisfies the orbital equation.

**Solution:**

From orbital equation: $r = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos\theta}$

**At perihelion** ($\theta = 0$):
$$r_{min} = \frac{p}{1 + e}$$

**At aphelion** ($\theta = \pi$):
$$r_{max} = \frac{p}{1 - e}$$

Sum:
$$r_{min} + r_{max} = \frac{p}{1+e} + \frac{p}{1-e} = p\left[\frac{1-e + 1+e}{(1-e)(1+e)}\right] = \frac{2p}{1-e^2}$$

But $r_{min} + r_{max} = a(1-e) + a(1+e) = 2a$, so:
$$2a = \frac{2p}{1-e^2}$$

$$p = a(1-e^2)$$

This is the standard relation between $p$, $a$, and $e$. ✓

**Answer:** Perihelion/aphelion satisfy orbital equation with $p = a(1-e^2)$

## Kepler's Second Law: Equal Areas

**Statement:** A line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times.

**Derivation:**

Area swept by radius vector in time $dt$:
$$dA = \frac{1}{2}r^2 d\theta$$

**Areal velocity:**
$$\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{1}{2}r^2\frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{1}{2}r^2\dot{\theta}$$

Using $L = mr^2\dot{\theta}$:
$$\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{L}{2m} = \text{constant}$$

Since $L$ is conserved for central force, areal velocity is constant. Therefore, equal areas are swept in equal times. ∎

### Example 2: Areal Velocity Calculation

Earth has $L = 2.66 \times 10^{40}$ kg} \cdot \text {m²/s. Find the areal velocity.

**Solution:**

Earth mass: $m = 5.97 \times 10^{24}$ kg

$$\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{L}{2m} = \frac{2.66 \times 10^{40}}{2(5.97 \times 10^{24})} = 2.23 \times 10^{15} \text{ m}^2\text{/s}$$

**Verification:** Earth's orbit has $a = 1.496 \times 10^{11}$ m, so area $= \pi a^2 = 7.04 \times 10^{22}$ m².

Period $T = 1$ year $= 3.156 \times 10^7$ s.

$$\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{\pi a^2}{T} = \frac{7.04 \times 10^{22}}{3.156 \times 10^7} = 2.23 \times 10^{15} \text{ m}^2\text{/s}$$ ✓

**Answer:** $2.23 \times 10^{15}$ m²/s

## Kepler's Third Law: Orbital Period

**Statement:** The square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis:
$$T^2 \propto a^3$$

**Derivation:**

Total area of ellipse: $A = \pi ab$ where $b = a\sqrt{1-e^2}$ is semi-minor axis.

Areal velocity: $dA/dt = L/(2m) = \pi ab/T$

From $L = mr^2\dot{\theta}$ at perihelion (where $v \perp r$):
$$L = mr_{min}v_{max}$$

Using energy conservation and angular momentum to find period (detailed calculation):

For elliptical orbit with energy $E = -k/(2a)$ and using $L = m\sqrt{kap(1-e^2)/m}$ where $k = GMm$:

$$T = \frac{2\pi ab}{L/(2m)} = \frac{2\pi a \cdot a\sqrt{1-e^2}}{L/(2m)}$$

After substitution and simplification:
$$T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}a^3$$

**Or more simply:** Period is time to sweep total area $\pi ab$:
$$T = \frac{\pi ab}{L/(2m)}$$

Using $L^2 = ma^2(1-e^2)GM$ (from orbit theory):
$$T = \frac{2\pi m a^2\sqrt{1-e^2}}{L}$$

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{GM}}$$

$$T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}a^3$$

### Example 3: Kepler's Third Law Application

Mars has orbital period $T = 687$ days. Find its semi-major axis given Earth's $a_E = 1$ AU, $T_E = 365$ days.

**Solution:**

From Kepler's third law:
$$\frac{T_M^2}{T_E^2} = \frac{a_M^3}{a_E^3}$$

$$a_M^3 = a_E^3\left(\frac{T_M}{T_E}\right)^2 = (1)^3\left(\frac{687}{365}\right)^2 = (1.882)^2 = 3.54$$

$$a_M = (3.54)^{1/3} = 1.52 \text{ AU}$$

**Answer:** $a_M = 1.52$ AU

## Orbital Elements

Six **orbital elements** completely specify an orbit:

1. **Semi-major axis** $a$: size of orbit
2. **Eccentricity** $e$: shape of orbit
3. **Inclination** $i$: tilt relative to reference plane
4. **Longitude of ascending node** $\Omega$: orientation of orbital plane
5. **Argument of periapsis** $\omega$: orientation of ellipse in plane
6. **True anomaly** $\nu$ (or time of periapsis): position in orbit

For planar orbits (Unit 6 focus), need only $a$, $e$, and $\omega$ (or equivalently $r_{min}$, $r_{max}$, and orientation).

**Relations:**
$$p = a(1-e^2) = \frac{L^2}{mk}$$

$$r_{min} = a(1-e), \quad r_{max} = a(1+e)$$

$$e = \frac{r_{max} - r_{min}}{r_{max} + r_{min}}$$

### Example 4: Finding Orbital Elements

A comet has perihelion $r_p = 0.6$ AU and aphelion $r_a = 35$ AU. Find $a$, $e$, and period.

**Solution:**

**Semi-major axis:**
$$a = \frac{r_p + r_a}{2} = \frac{0.6 + 35}{2} = 17.8 \text{ AU}$$

**Eccentricity:**
$$e = \frac{r_a - r_p}{r_a + r_p} = \frac{35 - 0.6}{35.6} = \frac{34.4}{35.6} = 0.966$$

**Period:** Using Kepler's third law:
$$T = \sqrt{a^3} \text{ years} = \sqrt{(17.8)^3} = \sqrt{5639} = 75.1 \text{ years}$$

**Answer:** $a = 17.8$ AU, $e = 0.966$, $T = 75$ years (like Halley's comet)

### Example 5: Binary Star System

Two stars orbit their common center of mass with period $T = 50$ years and separation $d = 20$ AU. Find total mass.

**Solution:**

For binary system, reduced mass $\mu = m_1m_2/(m_1+m_2)$ orbits at separation $d$ with:
$$T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{G(m_1+m_2)}d^3$$

$$(m_1 + m_2) = \frac{4\pi^2 d^3}{GT^2}$$

Using solar units where $G = 4\pi^2$ AU³/(M☉} \cdot \text {yr²):
$$(m_1 + m_2) = \frac{d^3}{T^2} = \frac{(20)^3}{(50)^2} = \frac{8000}{2500} = 3.2 \, M_{\odot}$$

**Answer:** Total mass $= 3.2$ solar masses
