# Article 2.1: Newton's Laws of Motion

The entire edifice of classical mechanics rests on three deceptively simple statements formulated by Isaac Newton in his *Principia Mathematica* (1687). These laws represent a profound insight: motion doesn't require explanation—*changes* in motion do. A body left alone continues in uniform motion; a force is precisely that which changes motion; and forces come in pairs arising from interactions. These principles, combined with specific force laws, allow us to predict the motion of everything from falling apples to orbiting planets.

Yet Newton's laws are not absolute truths but rather axioms that define a framework. They hold exactly in inertial reference frames—frames that are not accelerating. The identification of inertial frames becomes both a conceptual challenge and a practical approximation. Is the Earth's surface an inertial frame? Not quite, due to rotation. Is a frame fixed to the Sun? Closer, but the Sun orbits the galaxy. In practice, we identify "approximately inertial" frames sufficient for our calculations and add corrections (fictitious forces) when working in non-inertial frames.

This article establishes Newton's laws rigorously, explores their implications and limitations, and develops the methodology of force analysis through free-body diagrams. We emphasize the vector nature of forces, the importance of choosing appropriate coordinate systems, and the systematic approach to setting up equations of motion. These foundational skills enable the more complex applications—constrained systems, non-inertial frames, and Lagrangian mechanics—that follow in later units.

## Newton's First Law: The Law of Inertia

**Statement:** A body at rest remains at rest, and a body in motion continues in motion with constant velocity (constant speed and direction) unless acted upon by a net external force.

Mathematically: If $\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$, then $\mathbf{v} = \text{constant}$ (or equivalently, $\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}$).

This law defines **inertial reference frames**—frames where the law holds. Once we identify one inertial frame, any frame moving at constant velocity relative to it is also inertial (Galilean relativity).

The first law establishes the concept of **inertia**: a body's resistance to changes in motion. In the absence of forces, motion persists. This directly contradicts Aristotelian physics, which held that continued motion requires continued force.

**Physical Significance:** The first law is not merely a special case of the second law ($\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$ with $\mathbf{F} = 0$). Rather, it defines the class of reference frames—inertial frames—in which the second law takes its simple form without fictitious forces.

## Newton's Second Law: The Equation of Motion

**Statement:** The acceleration of an object is proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass.

$$\sum \mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$$

or in component form:
$$\sum F_x = ma_x, \quad \sum F_y = ma_y, \quad \sum F_z = ma_z$$

The mass $m$ is the **inertial mass**—a measure of resistance to acceleration. The sum $\sum \mathbf{F}$ includes *all* forces acting on the body.

Since $\mathbf{a} = d\mathbf{v}/dt = d^2\mathbf{r}/dt^2$, the second law becomes:
$$\sum \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{v}, t) = m\frac{d^2\mathbf{r}}{dt^2}$$

This is a second-order vector differential equation. Given forces as functions of position, velocity, and time, we can solve for the trajectory $\mathbf{r}(t)$ subject to initial conditions $\mathbf{r}(0)$ and $\mathbf{v}(0)$.

**Important:** The second law applies to *individual particles* or to the center of mass of extended objects (when treating them as point masses). For systems of particles, we sum over all particles or use center-of-mass dynamics.

### Example 1: One-Dimensional Motion with Constant Force

A particle of mass $m = 2$ kg experiences a constant force $F = 10$ N in the positive $x$-direction. If it starts from rest at $x = 0$, find $x(t)$ and $v(t)$.

**Solution:** Apply Newton's second law:
$$F = ma \implies 10 = 2a \implies a = 5 \text{ m/s}^2$$

Since $a$ is constant:
$$v(t) = v_0 + at = 0 + 5t = 5t \text{ m/s}$$

$$x(t) = x_0 + v_0 t + \frac{1}{2}at^2 = 0 + 0 + \frac{1}{2}(5)t^2 = 2.5t^2 \text{ m}$$

At $t = 2$ s: $v = 10$ m/s, $x = 10$ m.

**Answer:** $x(t) = 2.5t^2$ m, $v(t) = 5t$ m/s

## Newton's Third Law: Action and Reaction

**Statement:** For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. More precisely: If object A exerts a force $\mathbf{F}_{AB}$ on object B, then object B exerts a force $\mathbf{F}_{BA}$ on object A, where:
$$\mathbf{F}_{BA} = -\mathbf{F}_{AB}$$

These forces:
1. Are equal in magnitude
2. Are opposite in direction
3. Act on *different* objects
4. Are of the same type (both gravitational, both electromagnetic, etc.)

The third law ensures **conservation of momentum**: for an isolated system, the total momentum remains constant because internal forces cancel in pairs.

**Common misconception:** The action-reaction pair does *not* cancel to produce zero net force, because they act on different objects. Each object experiences only one of the pair.

### Example 2: Action-Reaction Pair

A book of mass $m = 1.5$ kg rests on a table. Identify all forces and their action-reaction pairs.

**Solution:** 

**Forces on the book:**
1. Gravitational force (weight): $\mathbf{F}_g = -mg\hat{\mathbf{z}} = -14.7\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ N (Earth pulls book downward)
2. Normal force: $\mathbf{N} = +14.7\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ N (table pushes book upward)

**Action-reaction pairs:**
- **Pair 1:** Earth pulls book down ($\mathbf{F}_g$ on book) ↔ Book pulls Earth up (equal magnitude, acts on Earth)
- **Pair 2:** Table pushes book up ($\mathbf{N}$ on book) ↔ Book pushes table down (equal magnitude, acts on table)

Note: $\mathbf{F}_g$ and $\mathbf{N}$ are *not* action-reaction pairs—they're both acting on the book. They happen to be equal in magnitude here because the book is in equilibrium ($a = 0$), but this is due to the second law, not the third.

**Answer:** Action-reaction pairs involve forces on different objects; $\mathbf{F}_g$ and $\mathbf{N}$ both act on book

## Free-Body Diagrams

A **free-body diagram (FBD)** is a sketch showing:
1. The object of interest (often represented as a point or simple shape)
2. All forces acting *on* that object (shown as vectors with labels)
3. A coordinate system

**Procedure:**
1. Isolate the object
2. Identify all forces acting on it (ignore forces it exerts on other objects)
3. Draw force vectors from the object's center
4. Label each force clearly ($\mathbf{F}_g$, $\mathbf{N}$, $\mathbf{T}$, etc.)
5. Choose convenient coordinates
6. Resolve forces into components
7. Apply $\sum \mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$ in each direction

### Example 3: Free-Body Diagram for Block on Incline

A block of mass $m = 5$ kg rests on a frictionless incline at angle $\theta = 30°$. Draw the free-body diagram and find the acceleration.

**Solution:** 

**Forces on block:**
1. Weight: $\mathbf{F}_g = -mg\hat{\mathbf{z}} = -49$ N (vertical downward)
2. Normal force: $\mathbf{N}$ (perpendicular to incline surface)

**Coordinate system:** Take $x$ along incline (positive downward) and $y$ perpendicular to incline.

**Resolve weight:**
- Component along incline: $F_{g,x} = mg\sin\theta = 49\sin(30°) = 24.5$ N
- Component perpendicular: $F_{g,y} = -mg\cos\theta = -49\cos(30°) = -42.4$ N

**Apply Newton's second law:**

In $y$-direction (perpendicular to incline): $a_y = 0$ (no motion perpendicular to surface)
$$\sum F_y = 0: \quad N - mg\cos\theta = 0 \implies N = mg\cos\theta = 42.4 \text{ N}$$

In $x$-direction (along incline):
$$\sum F_x = ma_x: \quad mg\sin\theta = ma \implies a = g\sin\theta = 9.8\sin(30°) = 4.9 \text{ m/s}^2$$

**Answer:** $a = g\sin\theta = 4.9$ m/s² down the incline

## Inertial Reference Frames

An **inertial frame** is one where Newton's first law holds: a free particle moves with constant velocity. Equivalently, it's a frame that is not accelerating.

**Galilean transformation:** If frame $S'$ moves with constant velocity $\mathbf{V}$ relative to inertial frame $S$:
$$\mathbf{r}' = \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{V}t$$
$$\mathbf{v}' = \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{V}$$
$$\mathbf{a}' = \mathbf{a}$$

Since acceleration is the same in both frames, $\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a} = m\mathbf{a}'$ holds in both. Thus $S'$ is also inertial.

**Non-inertial frames:** If frame $S'$ accelerates with $\mathbf{A}$ relative to inertial frame $S$:
$$\mathbf{a}' = \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{A}$$

In frame $S'$, Newton's second law requires a **fictitious force**:
$$\mathbf{F}_{\text{real}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{fictitious}} = m\mathbf{a}'$$
where $\mathbf{F}_{\text{fictitious}} = -m\mathbf{A}$.

### Example 4: Elevator Accelerating Upward

A person of mass $m = 70$ kg stands in an elevator accelerating upward at $a = 2$ m/s². What normal force does the floor exert on the person?

**Solution:** 

**Method 1 (Inertial frame):** In the inertial frame (outside elevator):

Forces on person:
- Weight: $F_g = -mg = -686$ N (downward)
- Normal force: $N$ (upward)

Apply $\sum F = ma$ in vertical direction:
$$N - mg = ma$$
$$N = m(g + a) = 70(9.8 + 2) = 70(11.8) = 826 \text{ N}$$

**Method 2 (Non-inertial frame, inside elevator):** In the elevator frame (accelerating at $a$ upward), there's a fictitious force $F_{\text{fict}} = -ma = -140$ N (downward).

For equilibrium in this frame ($a' = 0$):
$$N - mg - ma = 0 \implies N = m(g + a) = 826 \text{ N}$$

Both methods give the same result.

**Answer:** $N = 826$ N (person feels heavier)

### Example 5: System of Two Masses

Two masses $m_1 = 2$ kg and $m_2 = 3$ kg are connected by a massless string. A force $F = 20$ N pulls $m_1$ horizontally on a frictionless surface. Find the acceleration and tension.

**Solution:** 

Treat as system: Total mass $M = m_1 + m_2 = 5$ kg.

System acceleration:
$$F = (m_1 + m_2)a \implies a = \frac{F}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{20}{5} = 4 \text{ m/s}^2$$

For $m_2$ alone: Only tension $T$ acts (horizontally).
$$T = m_2 a = 3(4) = 12 \text{ N}$$

Check with $m_1$: Forces are $F$ (applied) and $-T$ (tension pulling back).
$$F - T = m_1 a \implies 20 - 12 = 2(4) = 8$$ ✓

**Answer:** $a = 4$ m/s², $T = 12$ N

B) $2\sqrt{2}$ m/s²

C) $4$ m/s²

**Problem 4:** In an elevator accelerating downward at $3$ m/s², a 50 kg person experiences an apparent weight of:

A) $640$ N

B) $490$ N

C) $340$ N

D) $150$ N

E) $0$ N

---

**Problem 5:** Two blocks ($m_1 = 4$ kg, $m_2 = 6$ kg) are pulled by force $F = 30$ N. The tension in the connecting string is:

A) $30$ N

B) $18$ N

C) $15$ N

D) $12$ N

E) $20$ N

---

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**

Net force: $\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = (30-10)\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 20\hat{\mathbf{y}} = 20\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 20\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ N

Magnitude: $|\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}}| = \sqrt{400 + 400} = 20\sqrt{2}$ N

Acceleration: $a = F_{\text{net}}/m = 20\sqrt{2}/10 = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83$ m/s²


---

**Problem 2: D**

Action-reaction forces always act on *different* objects. They don't cancel because they don't act on the same body.

---

**Problem 3: B**

Acceleration down incline: $a = g\sin(45°) = 9.8(1/\sqrt{2}) = 9.8(0.707) \approx 6.9$ m/s²

---

**Problem 4: C**

Apparent weight: $N = m(g - a) = 50(9.8 - 3) = 50(6.8) = 340$ N

The person feels lighter when accelerating downward.

---

**Problem 5: B**

System acceleration: $a = F/(m_1 + m_2) = 30/10 = 3$ m/s²

Tension (from $m_2$): $T = m_2 a = 6(3) = 18$ N
