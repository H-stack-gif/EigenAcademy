# Article 5.2: Damped Oscillations

Real oscillators experience dissipative forces—air resistance, friction, internal material losses—that convert mechanical energy to heat. Damping transforms the pure sinusoidal motion of SHM into exponentially decaying oscillations (underdamped), slow exponential relaxation (overdamped), or rapid non-oscillatory return to equilibrium (critically damped). The mathematical description introduces a first-derivative term in the differential equation, fundamentally changing the solution structure and requiring complex analysis techniques.

The damping parameter $\gamma$ characterizes energy loss rate, while the quality factor $Q$ measures how many oscillations occur before substantial energy dissipation. High-$Q$ systems—quartz crystals, optical cavities, atomic transitions—maintain coherent oscillations; low-$Q$ systems—car shock absorbers, door closers—return quickly to equilibrium without overshooting. The phase space trajectory for damped oscillations spirals inward toward the origin, with pitch determined by damping strength.

This article develops damped oscillators systematically, solving the differential equation for all three damping regimes, introducing the quality factor and logarithmic decrement as experimental observables, analyzing energy decay rates, and visualizing dynamics in phase space. The mathematics employs characteristic equations with complex roots, exponential functions, and decay envelopes. Physical examples range from mechanical oscillators to RLC circuits.

## The Damped Oscillator Equation

For a mass-spring system with damping force $F_d = -b\dot{x}$:
$$m\ddot{x} = -kx - b\dot{x}$$

Rearranging:
$$\ddot{x} + 2\gamma\dot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = 0$$

where:
- $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$ is the natural frequency (undamped)
- $\gamma = b/(2m)$ is the **damping coefficient**

**Characteristic equation:** Assume $x = e^{rt}$:
$$r^2 + 2\gamma r + \omega_0^2 = 0$$

**Solutions:**
$$r = -\gamma \pm \sqrt{\gamma^2 - \omega_0^2}$$

**Three cases depend on** $\gamma^2 - \omega_0^2$:
1. **Underdamped** ($\gamma < \omega_0$): Oscillatory decay
2. **Critically damped** ($\gamma = \omega_0$): Fastest non-oscillatory return
3. **Overdamped** ($\gamma > \omega_0$): Slow exponential decay

## Underdamped Oscillations ($\gamma < \omega_0$)

Roots are complex: $r = -\gamma \pm i\omega_d$ where
$$\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2}$$

is the **damped frequency**.

**General solution:**
$$x(t) = Ae^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega_d t + \phi)$$

**Amplitude envelope:** $A(t) = Ae^{-\gamma t}$ decays exponentially.

**Period:** $T_d = 2\pi/\omega_d > T_0 = 2\pi/\omega_0$ (damping increases period slightly)

### Example 1: Underdamped Oscillator

A mass $m = 1$ kg on spring with $k = 100$ N/m has damping coefficient $b = 6$ kg/s. Find: (a) $\omega_d$, (b) period, (c) time for amplitude to decrease by $50\%$.

**Solution:**

**(a) Natural frequency:**
$$\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{100/1} = 10 \text{ rad/s}$$

Damping coefficient:
$$\gamma = b/(2m) = 6/(2 \cdot 1) = 3 \text{ s}^{-1}$$

Since $\gamma = 3 < \omega_0 = 10$, system is underdamped.

Damped frequency:
$$\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2} = \sqrt{100 - 9} = \sqrt{91} = 9.54 \text{ rad/s}$$

**(b) Period:**
$$T_d = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_d} = \frac{2\pi}{9.54} = 0.658 \text{ s}$$

Compare to undamped: $T_0 = 2\pi/10 = 0.628$ s (damping increases period by 5%)

**(c) Half-life of amplitude:**

Amplitude envelope: $A(t) = A_0 e^{-\gamma t}$

When $A(t) = A_0/2$:
$$e^{-\gamma t_{1/2}} = 1/2$$
$$t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\gamma} = \frac{0.693}{3} = 0.231 \text{ s}$$

**Answer:** (a) $\omega_d = 9.54$ rad/s, (b) $T_d = 0.658$ s, (c) $t_{1/2} = 0.231$ s

## Critically Damped ($\gamma = \omega_0$)

Characteristic equation has repeated root $r = -\gamma$.

**General solution:**
$$x(t) = (A + Bt)e^{-\gamma t}$$

**Properties:**
- Returns to equilibrium in shortest time without overshooting
- Ideal for shock absorbers, door closers
- Boundary between oscillatory and non-oscillatory behavior

### Example 2: Critical Damping Design

Design a shock absorber for mass $m = 50$ kg on spring with $k = 2000$ N/m. What damping coefficient $b$ gives critical damping?

**Solution:**

Natural frequency:
$$\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{2000/50} = \sqrt{40} = 6.32 \text{ rad/s}$$

For critical damping: $\gamma = \omega_0$
$$\frac{b}{2m} = \omega_0$$
$$b = 2m\omega_0 = 2(50)(6.32) = 632 \text{ kg/s}$$

**Answer:** $b = 632$ kg/s

## Overdamped ($\gamma > \omega_0$)

Roots are real and negative: $r = -\gamma \pm \sqrt{\gamma^2 - \omega_0^2}$

**General solution:**
$$x(t) = Ae^{r_1 t} + Be^{r_2 t}$$

where $r_1, r_2 < 0$ are both negative.

**Properties:**
- No oscillations
- Slower return to equilibrium than critical damping
- Two exponential time constants

### Example 3: Overdamped Motion

For $\ddot{x} + 10\dot{x} + 16x = 0$ with $x(0) = 1$ m, $\dot{x}(0) = 0$, find $x(t)$.

**Solution:**

From equation: $2\gamma = 10$, so $\gamma = 5$ s⁻¹; $\omega_0^2 = 16$, so $\omega_0 = 4$ rad/s.

Since $\gamma = 5 > \omega_0 = 4$, system is overdamped.

Characteristic roots:
$$r = -5 \pm \sqrt{25-16} = -5 \pm 3$$
$$r_1 = -2, \quad r_2 = -8$$

General solution:
$$x(t) = Ae^{-2t} + Be^{-8t}$$

**Initial conditions:**
$$x(0) = A + B = 1$$
$$\dot{x}(0) = -2A - 8B = 0$$

From second equation: $A = -4B$

Substitute into first: $-4B + B = 1$, so $B = -1/3$, thus $A = 4/3$.

**Solution:**
$$x(t) = \frac{4}{3}e^{-2t} - \frac{1}{3}e^{-8t} \text{ m}$$

**Answer:** $x(t) = \frac{4}{3}e^{-2t} - \frac{1}{3}e^{-8t}$ m

## Quality Factor Q

The **quality factor** measures oscillation persistence:
$$Q = \frac{\omega_0}{2\gamma}$$

**Interpretation:**
- High $Q$ (e.g., $Q > 100$): Many oscillations before significant decay
- Low $Q$ (e.g., $Q < 1$): Heavily damped, possibly overdamped

**Relation to energy decay:** After one period $T$, fractional energy loss is:
$$\frac{\Delta E}{E} = \frac{2\pi}{Q}$$

For high $Q$: $E(t) \approx E_0 e^{-\omega_0 t/Q}$

**Number of oscillations for amplitude to decay by $1/e$:**
$$N = \frac{Q}{\pi}$$

### Example 4: Quality Factor Calculation

An oscillator has $\omega_0 = 100$ rad/s and $\gamma = 1$ s⁻¹. Find $Q$ and the number of oscillations for amplitude to decrease by factor of $e$.

**Solution:**

Amplitude envelope is $e^{-\gamma t}$. Time for $A \to A_0/e$ is $t = 1/\gamma$.

In this time, number of oscillations is:
$$N = \frac{t}{T} = \frac{t \omega_d}{2\pi} \approx \frac{\omega_0}{2\pi\gamma} = \frac{Q}{\pi}$$ ✓

**Answer:** $Q = 50$, approximately $16$ oscillations

## Logarithmic Decrement

For successive peaks at times $t_n$ and $t_{n+1} = t_n + T_d$:
$$\delta = \ln\frac{A(t_n)}{A(t_{n+1})} = \gamma T_d = \frac{2\pi\gamma}{\omega_d}$$

**Relation to Q:**
$$\delta = \frac{2\pi\gamma}{\sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2}} \approx \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0/2\gamma} = \frac{\pi}{Q}$$

(for weak damping: $\gamma \ll \omega_0$)

**Experimental use:** Measure consecutive amplitude maxima to determine $\gamma$ or $Q$.

### Example 5: Determining Damping from Measurement

An oscillator has successive peak amplitudes of $10$ cm and $8$ cm. The period is $T = 1.5$ s. Find $\gamma$ and $Q$.

**Solution:**

Logarithmic decrement:
$$\delta = \ln\frac{10}{8} = \ln(1.25) = 0.223$$

$$\delta = \gamma T_d = \gamma(1.5)$$
$$\gamma = \frac{0.223}{1.5} = 0.149 \text{ s}^{-1}$$

Angular frequency (damped):
$$\omega_d = \frac{2\pi}{T_d} = \frac{2\pi}{1.5} = 4.19 \text{ rad/s}$$

For weak damping: $\omega_d \approx \omega_0$, so $\omega_0 \approx 4.19$ rad/s.

$$Q = \frac{\omega_0}{2\gamma} = \frac{4.19}{2(0.149)} = 14.1$$

**Answer:** $\gamma = 0.149$ s⁻¹, $Q \approx 14$
