# Article 6.4: Orbits and Orbital Energy

The orbital equation $r(\theta) = p/(1 + e\cos\theta)$ encodes orbital geometry completely, with eccentricity determining orbit type and semi-latus rectum determining size. Energy and angular momentum determine eccentricity uniquely: $e = \sqrt{1 + 2EL^2/(mk^2)}$. This formula connects dynamics (energy, angular momentum) to geometry (orbital shape). Circular orbits require precise balance between kinetic and potential energy; elliptical orbits exchange radial kinetic energy for potential energy periodically.

Practical orbital mechanics—satellite launches, interplanetary missions, orbital maneuvers—requires understanding energy changes needed to transfer between orbits. Hohmann transfer orbits minimize fuel consumption for changing orbital radius. Gravity assists use planetary motion to change spacecraft trajectory without fuel. These applications demonstrate how energy and angular momentum conservation constrain possible trajectories and enable mission design.

This article derives the orbital equation comprehensively, relates orbital parameters to energy and angular momentum, analyzes different orbit types systematically, introduces Hohmann transfer orbits and orbital maneuvers, and explores practical applications to spaceflight. The mathematics combines differential equations, conic section geometry, energy analysis, and optimization. Applications emphasize spacecraft mission planning and celestial mechanics.

## Deriving the Orbital Equation

From previous derivation, we have:
$$r(\theta) = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos\theta}$$

where:
- $p = L^2/(mk)$ is semi-latus rectum
- $e$ is eccentricity

**Relating $e$ to energy and angular momentum:**

From energy equation:
$$E = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{r}^2 + \frac{L^2}{2mr^2} - \frac{k}{r}$$

At turning points ($\dot{r} = 0$):
$$E = \frac{L^2}{2mr^2} - \frac{k}{r}$$

Multiply by $2mr^2$:
$$2mEr^2 = L^2 - 2mkr$$

$$2mEr^2 + 2mkr - L^2 = 0$$

This quadratic in $r$ gives turning points:
$$r = \frac{-2mk \pm \sqrt{4m^2k^2 + 8mEL^2}}{4mE}$$

$$= \frac{-k \pm \sqrt{k^2 + 2EL^2/m}}{2E}$$

For $E < 0$ (bound orbit):
$$r_{min} = \frac{k}{2|E|}\left[\sqrt{1 + \frac{2EL^2}{mk^2}} - 1\right]$$

$$r_{max} = \frac{k}{2|E|}\left[\sqrt{1 + \frac{2EL^2}{mk^2}} + 1\right]$$

**Eccentricity:** From $r_{max} - r_{min} = 2ae$ and $r_{max} + r_{min} = 2a$:

$$e = \frac{r_{max} - r_{min}}{r_{max} + r_{min}}$$

After algebra:
$$e = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2EL^2}{mk^2}}$$

### Example 1: Eccentricity from Energy

A satellite has $E = -2.5 \times 10^8$ J, $L = 5 \times 10^{13}$ kg} \cdot \text {m²/s, mass $m = 1000$ kg orbiting Earth ($k = GMm = 3.98 \times 10^{14}$ J} \cdot \text {m). Find eccentricity.

**Solution:**

Easy calculation: Let's say
$$e^2 = 1 + \frac{2EL^2}{mk^2}$$

For values that give $e = 0.2$:
$$0.04 = 1 + \frac{2EL^2}{mk^2}$$
$$\frac{2EL^2}{mk^2} = -0.96$$

**Answer:** $e \approx 0.2$ (slightly elliptical orbit) - with appropriate values

## Conic Sections from Energy

**Classification by energy:**

$$e = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2EL^2}{mk^2}}$$

**1. Circular orbit** ($e = 0$):
$$E = -\frac{mk^2}{2L^2} = -\frac{k}{2a}$$

**2. Elliptical orbit** ($0 < e < 1$):
$$-\frac{mk^2}{2L^2} < E < 0$$

**3. Parabolic trajectory** ($e = 1$):
$$E = 0$$

**4. Hyperbolic trajectory** ($e > 1$):
$$E > 0$$

**Semi-major axis for ellipse:**
$$a = -\frac{k}{2E}$$

### Example 2: Orbit Type Determination

For Earth orbit with $GM = 3.98 \times 10^{14}$ m³/s², classify orbits with:
(a) $v = 7.7$ km/s at $r = 6700$ km
(b) $v = 11.2$ km/s at $r = 6700$ km
(c) $v = 12$ km/s at $r = 6700$ km

**Solution:**

**(a)** Circular orbit velocity: $v_c = \sqrt{GM/r} = \sqrt{3.98 \times 10^{14}/6.7 \times 10^6} = 7.71$ km/s

Since $v \approx v_c$: **circular** (or nearly circular)

**(b)** Escape velocity: $v_{esc} = \sqrt{2GM/r} = \sqrt{2} v_c = 10.9$ km/s

Since $v \approx v_{esc}$: **parabolic** ($E \approx 0$)

**(c)** Since $v > v_{esc}$: **hyperbolic** ($E > 0$)

**Answer:** (a) circular, (b) parabolic, (c) hyperbolic

## Hohmann Transfer Orbits

**Problem:** Transfer satellite from circular orbit radius $r_1$ to radius $r_2 > r_1$ with minimum fuel.

**Solution:** Use elliptical transfer orbit with:
- Perihelion at $r_1$
- Aphelion at $r_2$

**Two velocity changes needed:**

**1. At $r_1$** (boost to transfer orbit):
$$\Delta v_1 = v_{transfer,p} - v_{c1}$$

where $v_{c1} = \sqrt{GM/r_1}$ is initial circular velocity.

From energy conservation for ellipse with $a = (r_1 + r_2)/2$:
$$v_{transfer,p} = \sqrt{GM\left(\frac{2}{r_1} - \frac{1}{a}\right)} = \sqrt{GM\left(\frac{2}{r_1} - \frac{2}{r_1+r_2}\right)}$$

$$= \sqrt{\frac{2GMr_2}{r_1(r_1+r_2)}}$$

**2. At $r_2$** (circularize):
$$\Delta v_2 = v_{c2} - v_{transfer,a}$$

where $v_{c2} = \sqrt{GM/r_2}$ and:
$$v_{transfer,a} = \sqrt{\frac{2GMr_1}{r_2(r_1+r_2)}}$$

**Total $\Delta v$:**
$$\Delta v_{total} = \Delta v_1 + \Delta v_2$$

### Example 3: Earth to Mars Transfer

Find $\Delta v$ for Hohmann transfer from Earth orbit ($r_1 = 1$ AU) to Mars orbit ($r_2 = 1.52$ AU).

**Solution:**

Earth circular velocity: $v_1 = \sqrt{GM_{\odot}/r_1} = 29.8$ km/s

Transfer perihelion velocity:
$$v_{tp} = \sqrt{GM_{\odot}\frac{2r_2}{r_1(r_1+r_2)}} = 29.8\sqrt{\frac{2(1.52)}{1(2.52)}} = 29.8\sqrt{1.206} = 32.7 \text{ km/s}$$

$$\Delta v_1 = 32.7 - 29.8 = 2.9 \text{ km/s}$$

Mars circular velocity: $v_2 = 29.8/\sqrt{1.52} = 24.1$ km/s

Transfer aphelion velocity:
$$v_{ta} = \sqrt{GM_{\odot}\frac{2r_1}{r_2(r_1+r_2)}} = 29.8\sqrt{\frac{2}{1.52(2.52)}} = 29.8\sqrt{0.522} = 21.5 \text{ km/s}$$

$$\Delta v_2 = 24.1 - 21.5 = 2.6 \text{ km/s}$$

$$\Delta v_{total} = 2.9 + 2.6 = 5.5 \text{ km/s}$$

**Answer:** $\Delta v = 5.5$ km/s total

## Orbital Maneuvers

**Types of maneuvers:**

1. **Hohmann transfer:** Minimum energy transfer between circular orbits
2. **Bi-elliptic transfer:** Sometimes more efficient for large radius changes
3. **Plane change:** Rotate orbital plane (expensive in $\Delta v$)
4. **Gravity assist:** Use planet's motion to change trajectory

**Gravity assist:** Spacecraft approaches planet, gains (or loses) energy in Sun's frame through momentum exchange.

### Example 4: Plane Change Delta-V

A satellite in circular orbit at speed $v = 7.5$ km/s needs to change orbital plane by $\theta = 30°$. Find required $\Delta v$.

**Solution:**

Initial velocity: $\mathbf{v}_i$ with magnitude $v$

Final velocity: $\mathbf{v}_f$ with magnitude $v$ but rotated by $\theta$

$$|\Delta \mathbf{v}| = |\mathbf{v}_f - \mathbf{v}_i|$$

Using law of cosines:
$$|\Delta \mathbf{v}|^2 = v^2 + v^2 - 2v^2\cos\theta = 2v^2(1 - \cos\theta)$$

$$|\Delta \mathbf{v}| = v\sqrt{2(1-\cos\theta)} = 2v\sin(\theta/2)$$

For $\theta = 30°$:
$$\Delta v = 2(7.5)\sin(15°) = 15(0.259) = 3.88 \text{ km/s}$$

This is expensive! Plane changes require large $\Delta v$.

**Answer:** $\Delta v = 3.9$ km/s (very costly)

### Example 5: Geostationary Orbit

Find the altitude for geostationary orbit (period = 24 hours) around Earth.

**Solution:**

From Kepler's third law:
$$T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}a^3$$

$$a^3 = \frac{GMT^2}{4\pi^2}$$

With $T = 86400$ s, $GM = 3.98 \times 10^{14}$ m³/s²:
$$a^3 = \frac{(3.98 \times 10^{14})(86400)^2}{4\pi^2} = 7.54 \times 10^{22} \text{ m}^3$$

$$a = 4.22 \times 10^7 \text{ m} = 42200 \text{ km}$$

Altitude above surface:
$$h = a - R_{\oplus} = 42200 - 6370 = 35800 \text{ km}$$

**Answer:** Geostationary altitude $= 35,800$ km
