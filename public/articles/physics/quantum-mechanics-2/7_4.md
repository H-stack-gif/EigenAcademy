# Quantum Mechanics Topic 7.4: Identical Particles in Scattering

When identical particles scatter, quantum indistinguishability fundamentally alters the process. You cannot label which particle emerged at angle $\theta$ and which at $\pi - \theta$—both outcomes are the same quantum state. For bosons, the wavefunction must be symmetric under particle exchange; for fermions, antisymmetric. This symmetrization modifies scattering amplitudes: instead of just $f(\theta)$, we have $f(\theta) \pm f(\pi-\theta)$ depending on statistics. The resulting **exchange interference** creates distinctive angular distributions—bosons show enhanced forward-backward symmetry, while fermions exhibit suppression at $90°$ (Pauli exclusion).

Exchange effects dominate low-energy scattering where spatial wavefunctions overlap significantly. For electrons scattering at low energy, the antisymmetry requirement (Pauli exclusion) forbids scattering into certain states, dramatically reducing cross sections. Conversely, boson-boson scattering (like $^4$He-$^4$He) shows constructive interference. These quantum statistics effects have no classical analog—they arise purely from indistinguishability. Understanding exchange scattering is essential for many-body quantum systems: electron-electron interactions in atoms and solids, nuclear forces between identical nucleons, and collisions in ultracold atomic gases.

This article explores identical particle scattering systematically. We'll derive exchange-modified amplitudes, understand forward-backward symmetry for bosons and Pauli blocking for fermions, treat spin effects for electrons and neutrons, and see how indistinguishability affects cross section measurements. Exchange scattering beautifully demonstrates the fundamental role of quantum statistics in nature.

## Exchange-Modified Scattering Amplitude

For identical particles, the probability amplitude must account for both direct and exchange processes.

### Example 1: Symmetrized Amplitude

Two identical particles scatter with direct amplitude $f(\theta)$ (particle 1 at $\theta$, particle 2 at $\pi-\theta$) and exchange amplitude $f(\pi-\theta)$ (swapped).

For **bosons** (symmetric): Show total amplitude is:

$$f_B(\theta) = f(\theta) + f(\pi-\theta)$$

For **fermions** (antisymmetric):

$$f_F(\theta) = f(\theta) - f(\pi-\theta)$$

**Solution:**

State with particle 1 at $\mathbf{k}_1$ and particle 2 at $\mathbf{k}_2$ must be (anti)symmetrized:

**Bosons:**
$$|\psi_B\rangle \propto |\mathbf{k}_1\rangle|\mathbf{k}_2\rangle + |\mathbf{k}_2\rangle|\mathbf{k}_1\rangle$$

**Fermions:**
$$|\psi_F\rangle \propto |\mathbf{k}_1\rangle|\mathbf{k}_2\rangle - |\mathbf{k}_2\rangle|\mathbf{k}_1\rangle$$

Scattering amplitude:
- Direct: Particle 1 scatters to $\theta$, particle 2 to $\pi-\theta$ → amplitude $f(\theta)$
- Exchange: Particles swapped → amplitude $f(\pi-\theta)$

Total amplitude:
$$f_B = f(\theta) + f(\pi-\theta)$$
$$f_F = f(\theta) - f(\pi-\theta)$$

**Physical meaning:** Quantum interference between indistinguishable processes.

**Answer:** Bosons add amplitudes; fermions subtract (exchange interference)

### Example 2: Differential Cross Section

The differential cross section for identical particles is:

$$\left(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\right)_{\text{ident}} = |f(\theta) \pm f(\pi-\theta)|^2$$

Expand this and interpret each term.

**Solution:**

$$|f \pm g|^2 = |f|^2 + |g|^2 \pm 2\text{Re}[f^*g]$$

$$\left(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\right)_{\text{ident}} = |f(\theta)|^2 + |f(\pi-\theta)|^2 \pm 2\text{Re}[f^*(\theta)f(\pi-\theta)]$$

**Terms:**
1. $|f(\theta)|^2$: Direct scattering to $\theta$
2. $|f(\pi-\theta)|^2$: Direct scattering to $\pi-\theta$ (equivalent by symmetry)
3. $\pm 2\text{Re}[f^*(\theta)f(\pi-\theta)]$: **Exchange interference**

**Bosons** (+): Constructive interference
**Fermions** (−): Destructive interference

**Factor of 2:** Must account for both particles (no factor 1/2 because particles identical).

**Answer:** Cross section = direct + exchange + interference; interference has opposite signs

The interference term can be as large as the direct terms, completely changing angular distributions.

### Example 3: Forward-Backward Symmetry

For bosons, show that $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}$ is symmetric: $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}(\theta) = \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}(\pi-\theta)$.

**Solution:**

$$\left(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\right)_B(\theta) = |f(\theta) + f(\pi-\theta)|^2$$

At angle $\pi-\theta$:

$$\left(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\right)_B(\pi-\theta) = |f(\pi-\theta) + f(\theta)|^2$$

Since addition is commutative:

$$= |f(\theta) + f(\pi-\theta)|^2 = \left(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\right)_B(\theta)$$

**Physical meaning:** Cannot distinguish forward from backward scattering for identical bosons.

Detectors at $\theta$ and $\pi-\theta$ see equal rates.

**Experimental signature:** Boson-boson collisions show perfect forward-backward symmetry.

**Answer:** Bosons have $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}(\theta) = \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}(\pi-\theta)$ (forward-backward symmetry)

## Fermion Scattering and Pauli Blocking

For fermions (electrons, protons, neutrons, $^3$He), antisymmetry has dramatic consequences.

### Example 4: Ninety-Degree Suppression

For identical fermions, show that $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}(90°) = 0$.

**Solution:**

At $\theta = 90°$: $\pi - \theta = 90°$ also

$$f_F(90°) = f(90°) - f(\pi - 90°) = f(90°) - f(90°) = 0$$

**Cross section:** $|f_F|^2 = 0$

**Physical interpretation:** At 90°, exchange process is identical to direct process. Antisymmetry requires wavefunction vanish → **Pauli exclusion**.

Cannot have two identical fermions in same state (both at 90°).

**Experimental signature:** Dip in cross section at 90° for electron-electron, proton-proton, etc.

**Answer:** Fermion scattering forbidden at $90°$ by antisymmetry (Pauli blocking)

This is a purely quantum effect—classical particles have no such restriction. The 90° dip provides clear experimental signature of Fermi statistics.

### Example 5: Low-Energy Fermion Scattering

At very low energy, only $s$-wave scatters. For $s$-wave, $f(\theta) = f_0$ (isotropic).

What is fermion cross section?

**Solution:**

$s$-wave amplitude: $f(\theta) = f_0$ (constant)

Exchange amplitude: $f(\pi-\theta) = f_0$ (same)

Fermion amplitude:
$$f_F = f_0 - f_0 = 0$$

**Total cross section:** $\sigma = 0$!

**Physical meaning:** Symmetric spatial wavefunction ($s$-wave) requires antisymmetric spin state (spin singlet).

For spin-polarized fermions (same spin state): spatial wavefunction must be antisymmetric → no $s$-wave scattering!

**Pauli blocking:** Identical fermions in same spin state cannot scatter at low energy.

**Applications:** Ultracold fermionic atoms (polarized gas collisionless; unpolarized has $s$-wave).

**Answer:** $\sigma = 0$ for spin-polarized fermions at low energy (Pauli blocking)

### Example 6: Spin Effects in Electron Scattering

For electrons (spin-1/2), must combine spatial and spin symmetry.

Total wavefunction: $\Psi = \psi(\mathbf{r})\chi(\text{spin})$

For two electrons, overall antisymmetric:

- **Spin singlet** (antisymmetric): Spatial must be **symmetric** → use $f_B$
- **Spin triplet** (symmetric): Spatial must be **antisymmetric** → use $f_F$

If spins unpolarized, what is cross section?

**Solution:**

Four spin states total: 1 singlet, 3 triplet

**Singlet** (probability 1/4): $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = |f + f_{\text{ex}}|^2$

**Triplet** (probability 3/4): $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = |f - f_{\text{ex}}|^2$

**Average:**

$$\left(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\right)_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{4}|f+f_{\text{ex}}|^2 + \frac{3}{4}|f-f_{\text{ex}}|^2$$

Expanding:

$$= \frac{1}{4}[|f|^2 + |f_{\text{ex}}|^2 + 2\text{Re}(f^*f_{\text{ex}})] + \frac{3}{4}[|f|^2 + |f_{\text{ex}}|^2 - 2\text{Re}(f^*f_{\text{ex}})]$$

$$= |f|^2 + |f_{\text{ex}}|^2 - \frac{1}{2}\text{Re}(f^*f_{\text{ex}})$$

**Answer:** Unpolarized electron scattering weighted average of singlet/triplet

### Example 7: Spin-Polarized vs Unpolarized

Compare electron-electron scattering cross sections at 90° for:
(a) Parallel spins (polarized)
(b) Unpolarized

**Solution:**

**(a) Parallel spins:** Must be in triplet → spatial antisymmetric

At $90°$: $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = |f(90°) - f(90°)|^2 = 0$ (Pauli blocking)

**(b) Unpolarized:** Average over spin states

At $90°$: $f(\theta) = f(\pi-\theta) = f(90°)$

$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \frac{1}{4}|2f|^2 + \frac{3}{4}|0|^2 = |f(90°)|^2$$

Only singlet contributes (triplet blocked).

**Factor 1/4:** Statistical weight of singlet.

**Physical meaning:** Unpolarized has 1/4 the cross section compared to distinguishable particles (vs zero for polarized).

**Answer:** (a) Zero (Pauli); (b) $|f(90°)|^2$ (1/4 of distinguishable)

## Partial Wave Analysis for Identical Particles

In partial wave expansion, exchange effects modify amplitudes.

### Example 8: Partial Waves and Exchange

For $s$-wave scattering of identical bosons:

$$f(\theta) = f_0 = \frac{1}{k}e^{i\delta_0}\sin\delta_0$$

Show that cross section is enhanced by factor 4 compared to distinguishable particles.

**Solution:**

Boson amplitude: $f_B = f_0 + f_0 = 2f_0$

Cross section:

$$\left(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\right)_B = |2f_0|^2 = 4|f_0|^2$$

$$= 4\left(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\right)_{\text{dist}}$$

Total cross section:

$$\sigma_B = 4\pi(4|f_0|^2) = 16\pi|f_0|^2$$

For distinguishable: $\sigma_{\text{dist}} = 4\pi|f_0|^2$

**Enhancement factor:** 4 (comes from $|2|^2 = 4$)

**Physical meaning:** Bosonic bunching—enhanced scattering.

**Answer:** Boson $s$-wave cross section 4× larger than distinguishable particles

### Example 9: Fermion $p$-Wave Dominance

For identical fermions at low energy, $s$-wave blocked. Next contribution is $p$-wave.

If $\delta_0 \neq 0$ but $\delta_1 = 0$ (small $p$-wave scattering), what is cross section?

**Solution:**

Fermion spatial wavefunction must be antisymmetric (for triplet).

$s$-wave symmetric → blocked

$p$-wave antisymmetric → **allowed**

Even with small $\delta_1$, $p$-wave dominates because $s$-wave = 0.

Cross section:

$$\sigma \approx \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2\cdot 1 + 1)\sin^2\delta_1 = \frac{12\pi}{k^2}\sin^2\delta_1$$

For small $\delta_1 \propto k^3$ (threshold behavior):

$$\sigma \propto k^4$$ (very strong energy dependence)

**Answer:** Fermions scatter via $p$-wave; $\sigma \propto k^4$ at low energy

This strong energy dependence distinguishes fermion-fermion from boson-boson scattering experimentally.

### Example 10: Helium-3 vs Helium-4

Compare low-temperature scattering of $^3$He-$^3$He (fermions) vs $^4$He-$^4$He (bosons).

**Solution:**

**$^4$He (bosons):**
- $s$-wave allowed and enhanced
- $\sigma \approx 16\pi a_s^2$ (factor 4 enhancement)
- Temperature-independent at low $T$

**$^3$He (fermions):**
- $s$-wave blocked for spin-polarized
- $p$-wave scattering dominates
- $\sigma \propto T^2$ (since $k^2 \propto T$, and $\sigma \propto k^4$)

At ultralow temperatures: $^3$He becomes essentially **collisionless** (suppressed scattering).

This enables:
- $^3$He superfluidity via different mechanism (Cooper pairing)
- Dilution refrigeration (collisionless $^3$He)

**Answer:** Bosons have large, T-independent $\sigma$; fermions have small, $T^2$-dependent $\sigma$

### Example 11: Experimental Observation

In electron-electron scattering experiments, how do you observe exchange effects?

**Solution:**

**Challenge:** Cannot "label" electrons to distinguish direct from exchange.

**Observable:** Angular distribution shape

**Signature:**
- Dip at 90° (Pauli blocking for parallel spins)
- Forward-backward asymmetry modified

**Technique:**
- Measure $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}(\theta)$
- Compare with theoretical prediction including exchange
- Extract phase shifts

**Mott scattering:** Electrons from atoms (not free) show exchange when atomic electrons involved.

**Solid state:** Exchange integral affects electronic structure (Hartree-Fock).

**Answer:** Measure angular distribution; compare with exchange-modified predictions

## Practice Problems

**Problem 1:** For identical bosons, scattering amplitude is:

A) $f(\theta)$
B) $f(\theta) - f(\pi-\theta)$
C) $f(\theta) + f(\pi-\theta)$
D) $|f(\theta)|^2$
E) Zero

**Problem 2:** At 90°, fermion-fermion scattering cross section is:

A) Maximum
B) Minimum (zero)
C) Same as other angles
D) Infinite
E) Negative

**Problem 3:** Low-energy identical fermion scattering is dominated by:

A) $s$-wave
B) $p$-wave
C) $d$-wave
D) All waves equally
E) No scattering

**Problem 4:** For unpolarized electron scattering, spin singlet has statistical weight:

A) 0
B) 1/4
C) 1/2
D) 3/4
E) 1

**Problem 5:** Boson $s$-wave cross section compared to distinguishable particles is:

A) Same
B) Half
C) Double
D) Four times larger
E) Zero

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
Identical bosons require symmetric wavefunction. Scattering amplitude includes direct ($f(\theta)$) and exchange ($f(\pi-\theta)$) processes with **constructive interference**: $f_B = f(\theta) + f(\pi-\theta)$. Cross section $|f_B|^2$ shows enhancement from interference term. Forward-backward symmetry: $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}(\theta) = \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}(\pi-\theta)$. Examples: $^4$He-$^4$He, photon-photon, $\alpha$-$\alpha$. Enhancement factor 2 in amplitude → factor 4 in cross section for isotropic scattering.

**Problem 2: B**
At $\theta = 90°$: exchange angle $\pi - 90° = 90°$ (same). Fermion amplitude $f_F = f(90°) - f(90°) = 0$ by antisymmetry. Cross section $|f_F|^2 = 0$. **Pauli exclusion:** Cannot have two identical fermions in same state. Experimental signature: dip at 90° in electron-electron, proton-proton scattering. Purely quantum effect—no classical analog. For unpolarized electrons: reduced but non-zero (singlet contributes). Spin-polarized: exactly zero.

**Problem 3: B**
Identical fermions require antisymmetric spatial wavefunction (for spin triplet). $s$-wave (even parity) is symmetric → **blocked** by Pauli exclusion. $p$-wave (odd parity) is antisymmetric → **allowed**. At low energy where higher waves negligible: $p$-wave dominates despite small phase shift. Cross section $\sigma \propto k^4$ (vs $\sigma \propto k^0$ for bosons). Example: $^3$He-$^3$He scattering. Enables collisionless regime at ultralow $T$ (superfluidity).

**Problem 4: B**
Two spin-1/2 particles: 4 states total = 1 singlet (antisymmetric) + 3 triplet (symmetric). Singlet: $|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle - |\downarrow\uparrow\rangle$ (1 state). Triplet: $|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle, |\downarrow\downarrow\rangle, |\uparrow\downarrow\rangle + |\downarrow\uparrow\rangle$ (3 states). For unpolarized (random spins): equal probability → singlet weight = 1/4, triplet weight = 3/4. Cross section: $\sigma = \frac{1}{4}\sigma_{\text{singlet}} + \frac{3}{4}\sigma_{\text{triplet}}$. Singlet uses symmetric spatial ($f+f_{\text{ex}}$); triplet uses antisymmetric ($f-f_{\text{ex}}$).

**Problem 5: D**
Boson amplitude $f_B = f + f = 2f$ (isotropic $s$-wave). Cross section $\sigma_B = \int|f_B|^2 d\Omega = 4\pi|2f|^2 = 4(4\pi|f|^2) = 4\sigma_{\text{dist}}$. Factor 4 from $|2|^2$. Enhancement from bosonic bunching (constructive quantum interference). For distinguishable particles: $\sigma = 4\pi|f|^2$. Examples: Identical boson scattering always enhanced. Observable in ultracold $^{87}$Rb-$^{87}$Rb collisions. Related to stimulated emission, BEC formation.
