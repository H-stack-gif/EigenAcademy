# Quantum Mechanics Topic 10.3: Spontaneous Emission and Lamb Shift

In classical electrodynamics, an excited atom should radiate forever—the electron accelerates in the Coulomb field, and accelerating charges emit electromagnetic waves. But experimentally, excited atoms decay with characteristic lifetimes, emitting discrete photons. How can we understand this from first principles? The key insight is that even in "empty space," the electromagnetic field has quantum fluctuations. The **vacuum isn't empty**—it seethes with virtual photons appearing and disappearing. These vacuum fluctuations perturb the electron, inducing transitions from excited states to lower energy states with photon emission. This is **spontaneous emission**, impossible to derive from classical physics or even from treating the electromagnetic field classically while quantizing the atom.

But vacuum fluctuations do more than cause spontaneous emission. They also shift energy levels themselves—the **Lamb shift**, a tiny (~1 GHz) splitting between the $2S_{1/2}$ and $2P_{1/2}$ states of hydrogen that should be degenerate according to the Dirac equation. Willis Lamb's 1947 measurement of this shift forced physicists to take quantum electrodynamics seriously. The shift arises from two effects: the electron's self-energy (interacting with its own radiation field) and vacuum polarization (virtual electron-positron pairs screening the nuclear charge). Calculating these requires renormalization—subtracting infinities—but the finite result agrees with experiment to incredible precision.

This article explores how quantizing the electromagnetic field explains spontaneous emission and radiative corrections to energy levels. We'll derive the Einstein A coefficient from QED, understand the role of vacuum fluctuations, calculate decay rates using Fermi's golden rule, examine the Lamb shift's origin in self-energy and vacuum polarization, and see how these QED effects provide precision tests of quantum field theory.

## Spontaneous Emission from Field Quantization

### Example 1: Interaction Hamiltonian

Write the atom-field interaction Hamiltonian in second quantization.

**Solution:**

**Minimal coupling**: $\mathbf{p} \to \mathbf{p} - e\mathbf{A}$

Hamiltonian:
$$\hat{H} = \frac{(\hat{\mathbf{p}} - e\hat{\mathbf{A}})^2}{2m} + V(\mathbf{r})$$

Expanding:
$$\hat{H} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^2}{2m} + V(\mathbf{r}) - \frac{e}{m}\hat{\mathbf{A}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{p}} + \frac{e^2}{2m}\hat{\mathbf{A}}^2$$

**Perturbation** (to first order in $e$):
$$\hat{H}_{int} = -\frac{e}{m}\hat{\mathbf{A}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{p}}$$

Using $\hat{\mathbf{p}} = im\omega_{fi}\mathbf{r}$ (dipole approximation):
$$\hat{H}_{int} \approx -e\hat{\mathbf{E}} \cdot \mathbf{r} = -\hat{\mathbf{d}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{E}}$$

where $\hat{\mathbf{d}} = -e\mathbf{r}$ is electric dipole operator.

**Answer:** Interaction: $\hat{H}_{int} = -\hat{\mathbf{d}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{E}}$ (electric dipole coupling).

### Example 2: Transition Rate Calculation

Derive the spontaneous emission rate using Fermi's golden rule.

**Solution:**

Initial state: $|i\rangle = |e\rangle \otimes |0_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\rangle$ (excited atom, no photons)

Final state: $|f\rangle = |g\rangle \otimes |1_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\rangle$ (ground state, one photon)

Matrix element:
$$\langle f|\hat{H}_{int}|i\rangle = -\langle g|\hat{\mathbf{d}}|e\rangle \cdot \langle 1_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}|\hat{\mathbf{E}}|0\rangle$$

$$\hat{\mathbf{E}} = i\sum_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega_k}{2\epsilon_0 V}}[\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}e^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r}} - \text{h.c.}]$$

Only $\hat{a}^\dagger$ term contributes (emission):
$$\langle 1_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}|\hat{\mathbf{E}}|0\rangle = -i\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega}{2\epsilon_0 V}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}e^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r}_0}$$

where $\mathbf{r}_0$ is atomic position (set to 0).

**Fermi's golden rule**:
$$\Gamma = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}|\langle f|\hat{H}_{int}|i\rangle|^2\delta(E_e - E_g - \hbar\omega_k)$$

**Answer:** Transition rate requires sum over final photon states (modes $\mathbf{k}, \lambda$).

### Example 3: Density of States

Calculate the density of photon states.

**Solution:**

Number of modes in volume $V$ with $|\mathbf{k}| < k$:
$$N(k) = 2 \cdot \frac{V}{(2\pi)^3} \cdot \frac{4\pi k^3}{3}$$

(factor 2 from polarizations)

Density of states:
$$\rho(\omega) = \frac{dN}{d\omega} = 2 \cdot \frac{V}{(2\pi)^3} \cdot 4\pi k^2 \frac{dk}{d\omega}$$

Using $\omega = ck$: $dk/d\omega = 1/c$

$$\rho(\omega) = \frac{V\omega^2}{\pi^2 c^3}$$

Per unit volume: $\rho(\omega)/V = \frac{\omega^2}{\pi^2 c^3}$

**Answer:** Density of states $\rho(\omega) = \frac{V\omega^2}{\pi^2 c^3}$ (grows as $\omega^2$).

### Example 4: Einstein A Coefficient

Derive the Einstein A coefficient for spontaneous emission.

**Solution:**

Combining matrix element and density of states:

$$\Gamma = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar} \cdot \frac{\hbar\omega}{2\epsilon_0 V}|\mathbf{d}_{ge}|^2 \cdot \frac{V\omega^2}{\pi^2 c^3}$$

Averaging over polarizations ($|\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \cdot \mathbf{d}|^2 \to |\mathbf{d}|^2/3$):

$$A = \frac{\omega^3|\mathbf{d}_{ge}|^2}{3\pi\epsilon_0\hbar c^3}$$

Using $\omega = (E_e - E_g)/\hbar$ and $\mathbf{d}_{ge} = \langle g|\mathbf{d}|e\rangle = -e\langle g|\mathbf{r}|e\rangle$:

**Einstein A coefficient**: Spontaneous emission rate.

Lifetime: $\tau = 1/A$

**Answer:** $A = \frac{\omega^3|\mathbf{d}_{ge}|^2}{3\pi\epsilon_0\hbar c^3}$ (spontaneous emission rate, depends on $\omega^3$).

### Example 5: Hydrogen 2P → 1S Transition

Calculate the lifetime of hydrogen 2P state.

**Solution:**

Transition: $2P \to 1S + \gamma$

Energy: $\hbar\omega = E_{2P} - E_{1S} = \frac{3}{8} \cdot 13.6\text{ eV} = 10.2\text{ eV}$

Dipole matrix element (calculated):
$$|\langle 1S|\mathbf{r}|2P\rangle| = \frac{128\sqrt{2}}{243}a_0$$

where $a_0 = 0.529$ Å (Bohr radius).

$$A = \frac{\omega^3}{3\pi\epsilon_0\hbar c^3}\left(\frac{128\sqrt{2}}{243}ea_0\right)^2$$

Numerically: $A \approx 6.3 \times 10^8$ s$^{-1}$

Lifetime: $\tau = 1/A \approx 1.6$ ns

Agrees with experiment!

**Answer:** 2P lifetime $\tau \approx 1.6$ ns from spontaneous emission (QED prediction matches experiment).

### Example 6: Selection Rules

Explain how selection rules arise in spontaneous emission.

**Solution:**

Electric dipole matrix element: $\mathbf{d}_{fi} = -e\langle f|\mathbf{r}|i\rangle$

**Selection rules** from symmetry:

1. **Parity**: $\mathbf{r}$ is odd, so $\langle f|\mathbf{r}|i\rangle = 0$ unless states have opposite parity.
   - Allowed: $s \leftrightarrow p$, $p \leftrightarrow d$
   - Forbidden: $s \leftrightarrow s$, $s \leftrightarrow d$

2. **Angular momentum**: Photon carries spin 1, so $\Delta l = \pm 1$

3. **Magnetic quantum number**: $\Delta m = 0, \pm 1$ (from polarization)

**Forbidden transitions**: Can occur via higher multipoles (magnetic dipole, electric quadrupole) but much slower ($\sim 10^6$ slower).

Metastable states: Long-lived because only forbidden transitions available.

**Answer:** Selection rules: $\Delta l = \pm 1$, $\Delta m = 0, \pm 1$, opposite parity (from dipole symmetry).

## Vacuum Fluctuations and Physical Effects

### Example 7: Vacuum Field Fluctuations

Calculate the RMS electric field in vacuum.

**Solution:**

Electric field operator:
$$\hat{\mathbf{E}} = i\sum_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega_k}{2\epsilon_0 V}}[\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}e^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r}} - \text{h.c.}]$$

Vacuum expectation:
$$\langle 0|\hat{E}^2|0\rangle = \sum_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\frac{\hbar\omega_k}{2\epsilon_0 V}\langle 0|(\hat{a} - \hat{a}^\dagger)(\hat{a} - \hat{a}^\dagger)^\dagger|0\rangle$$

$$= \sum_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\frac{\hbar\omega_k}{2\epsilon_0 V}$$

This diverges (sum over all frequencies)!

Need cutoff $\omega_{max}$ (atomic physics: $\sim mc^2/\hbar$):

$$E_{rms}^2 \sim \frac{\hbar}{\epsilon_0 V}\int_0^{\omega_{max}} \omega \cdot \frac{V\omega^2}{\pi^2 c^3}d\omega \sim \frac{\hbar\omega_{max}^4}{\epsilon_0\pi^2 c^3}$$

**Answer:** Vacuum fluctuations: $\langle E^2 \rangle$ diverges, needs cutoff; finite at any given frequency.

### Example 8: Lamb Shift Overview

Describe the physical origin of the Lamb shift.

**Solution:**

**Dirac equation**: $2S_{1/2}$ and $2P_{1/2}$ degenerate (same $n$, $j$).

**Experiment** (Lamb & Retherford, 1947): $2S_{1/2}$ is higher by $\Delta E \approx 1057$ MHz.

**QED corrections**:

1. **Self-energy**: Electron emits and reabsorbs virtual photons
   - Modifies electron mass and charge (renormalization)
   - Different effect on $s$ vs $p$ states ($|\psi(0)|^2$ differs)

2. **Vacuum polarization**: Virtual $e^+e^-$ pairs screen nuclear charge
   - Effective potential $V(r) \to V(r) + \delta V(r)$
   - Larger effect on $s$-states (penetrate close to nucleus)

**Result**: $2S_{1/2}$ shifted up relative to $2P_{1/2}$.

**Answer:** Lamb shift: 1057 MHz splitting from QED (self-energy + vacuum polarization).

### Example 9: Self-Energy Contribution

Estimate the self-energy contribution to Lamb shift.

**Solution:**

**Physical picture**: Electron position "jitters" from virtual photon emission/absorption.

Mean square displacement:
$$\langle\delta r^2\rangle \sim \frac{e^2}{\epsilon_0 mc^2} \sim \alpha a_0$$

where $\alpha = 1/137$ (fine structure constant).

Energy shift from averaging potential:
$$\Delta E \sim \langle\nabla^2 V\rangle \langle\delta r^2\rangle \sim |\psi(0)|^2 \cdot \frac{e^2}{\epsilon_0} \cdot \alpha a_0$$

For $s$-states: $|\psi(0)|^2 = 1/(\pi a_0^3)$

$$\Delta E_s \sim \frac{\alpha}{\pi a_0^2} \cdot \frac{e^2}{\epsilon_0} \sim \frac{e^2\alpha}{4\pi\epsilon_0 a_0^2}$$

$$\sim \frac{mc^2\alpha^3}{\pi} \sim \alpha^3 \cdot 13.6\text{ eV} \approx 0.2\text{ eV} \times \ln(\Lambda/m)$$

Logarithmic divergence requires regularization, but order of magnitude correct.

**Answer:** Self-energy: $\Delta E \sim \alpha^3 mc^2 \ln(\Lambda)$ (logarithmically divergent, requires renormalization).

### Example 10: Vacuum Polarization

Describe vacuum polarization contribution.

**Solution:**

**Virtual pairs**: Vacuum fluctuations create temporary $e^+e^-$ pairs.

**Screening**: Pairs polarize around nucleus, screening charge.

**Effective charge**: At distance $r$:
$$e_{eff}(r) = e\left[1 + \frac{\alpha}{3\pi}\ln\frac{\Lambda}{m}\right]$$

where $\Lambda$ is cutoff (running coupling).

**Potential modification**:
$$\delta V(r) \sim -\frac{\alpha^2}{\pi r} \cdot \delta^3(\mathbf{r})$$

(Most important at $r = 0$)

**Energy shift**: Proportional to $|\psi(0)|^2$ (only $s$-states affected).

Combined with self-energy: Agrees with observed Lamb shift to high precision.

**Answer:** Vacuum polarization: Screening modifies potential at short distances, shifts $s$-states.

### Example 11: QED Precision Tests

State the precision of QED predictions.

**Solution:**

**Lamb shift**: 
- Theory: $1057.8446(29)$ MHz
- Experiment: $1057.8440(11)$ MHz
- Agreement: 13 significant figures!

**Anomalous magnetic moment** (electron):
$$g - 2 = \frac{\alpha}{2\pi} - 0.328478\left(\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\right)^2 + \cdots$$

- Theory: $g = 2.00231930436256(35)$
- Experiment: $g = 2.00231930436153(27)$
- Agreement: parts in $10^{12}$!

**Most precisely tested theory in physics.**

**Answer:** QED: Most precise theory, agreement with experiment to parts in $10^{12}$ (electron $g$-factor).

### Example 12: Beyond Lamb Shift

What other QED effects exist in atoms?

**Solution:**

**Hyperfine splitting**: QED corrections to hyperfine structure
- Nuclear size effects
- Vacuum polarization
- Used in atomic clocks (Cs-133 standard)

**Fine structure corrections**: Higher-order QED to fine structure
- Two-loop diagrams
- Test QED at next order in $\alpha$

**Positronium**: $e^+e^-$ bound state
- Pure QED system (no nuclear complications)
- Decay rates test annihilation processes

**Muonic atoms**: Muon orbiting nucleus
- Larger Lamb shift ($\propto m_\mu^3$)
- Sensitive to nuclear structure

**Answer:** Other QED effects: Hyperfine corrections, positronium, muonic atoms—all test QED.
