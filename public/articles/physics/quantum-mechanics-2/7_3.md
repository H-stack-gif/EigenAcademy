
# Quantum Mechanics Topic 7.3: Phase Shifts and Resonances

Phase shifts encode the essence of scattering—how much the wavefunction is perturbed by the potential. A small phase shift indicates weak scattering; a large phase shift (approaching $\pm\pi/2$) signals strong interaction. When the phase shift passes through $\pi/2$, something dramatic occurs: a **resonance**, where the scattering cross section peaks sharply. Resonances reveal the presence of quasi-bound states—energy levels that would be truly bound except for their ability to decay into the scattering continuum. These temporary trapping states have finite lifetimes related to the resonance width through $\Gamma = \hbar/\tau$.

Resonances appear throughout physics: nuclear reactions show sharp cross section peaks (compound nucleus formation), molecular predissociation creates long-lived complexes, and Feshbach resonances in ultracold atoms enable precise control of interactions. The universal description is the **Breit-Wigner formula**: a Lorentzian peak centered at resonance energy $E_r$ with width $\Gamma$. Far from resonances, scattering at very low energies is characterized by a single parameter—the scattering length $a_s$—regardless of potential details. This **universality** is powerful: different microscopic interactions produce identical low-energy scattering if they share the same $a_s$.

This article explores phase shift physics systematically. We'll derive low-energy theorems, understand resonance formation and Breit-Wigner line shapes, connect phase shifts to bound states via Levinson's theorem, and see how complex energy poles describe resonances. Phase shift analysis bridges formal scattering theory and experimental observables, providing the primary tool for extracting interaction properties from cross section measurements.

## Low-Energy Scattering

At energies well below any resonances, $s$-wave scattering is described by the **scattering length** $a_s$ and **effective range** $r_e$:

$$k\cot\delta_0 = -\frac{1}{a_s} + \frac{1}{2}r_e k^2 + \mathcal{O}(k^4)$$

This **effective range expansion** parametrizes low-energy phase shifts independent of potential details.

### Example 1: Deriving Scattering Length

For square well potential $V(r) = -V_0$ for $r < a$, $V=0$ for $r > a$, solve for scattering length.

**Solution:**

**Inside** ($r < a$): $u(r) = A\sin(Kr)$ where $K = \sqrt{2m(E+V_0)}/\hbar$

**Outside** ($r > a$): $u(r) = B\sin(kr + \delta_0)$ where $k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$

**Matching at $r=a$:** $u$ and $u'$ continuous

$$A\sin(Ka) = B\sin(ka + \delta_0)$$
$$AK\cos(Ka) = Bk\cos(ka + \delta_0)$$

Dividing:

$$K\cot(Ka) = k\cot(ka + \delta_0)$$

Low energy ($k \to 0$): $\delta_0 \to -ka_s$

$$K\cot(Ka) = k\cot(ka - ka_s) \approx k\frac{1}{k(a-a_s)} = \frac{1}{a-a_s}$$

For $k \to 0$: $K \to \kappa = \sqrt{2mV_0}/\hbar$

$$\kappa\cot(\kappa a) = \frac{1}{a - a_s}$$

$$a_s = a - \frac{1}{\kappa\cot(\kappa a)} = a\left(1 - \frac{\tan(\kappa a)}{\kappa a}\right)$$

**Answer:** $a_s = a\left(1 - \frac{\tan(\kappa a)}{\kappa a}\right)$ for square well

### Example 2: Scattering Length Sign and Bound States

How does scattering length relate to bound states?

**Solution:**

**Bound state condition:** For attractive well, bound state exists when:

$$\kappa a = \frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{3\pi}{2}, \ldots$$ (zeros of $\cot$)

At threshold ($\kappa a = \pi/2$):

$$\tan(\kappa a) \to \infty \implies a_s \to \infty$$

**Before first bound state** ($\kappa a < \pi/2$): $\tan(\kappa a) > 0$ → $a_s < a$ (positive)

**After first bound state** ($\pi/2 < \kappa a < 3\pi/2$): $\tan(\kappa a) < 0$ → $a_s > a$

**At threshold:** $a_s = \pm\infty$ (resonance)

**General rule:** $a_s$ diverges when bound state reaches zero energy; sign depends on which side of resonance.

**Answer:** $a_s \to \pm\infty$ at threshold for bound state; sign changes across resonance

The scattering length provides a simple diagnostic: $|a_s|$ large signals proximity to a bound state.

### Example 3: Effective Range

The effective range $r_e$ is defined by:

$$r_e = 2\int_0^\infty\left[1 - \frac{u_0^2(r)}{u_0^2(\infty)}\right]dr$$

where $u_0$ is zero-energy $s$-wave radial wavefunction. For hard sphere (radius $a$):

$$u_0(r) = r - a \quad (r > a)$$

Compute $r_e$.

**Solution:**

Normalized at infinity: $u_0(\infty) = r - a \approx r$ for large $r$

$$\frac{u_0(r)}{u_0(\infty)} = \frac{r-a}{r} = 1 - \frac{a}{r}$$

$$r_e = 2\int_a^\infty\left[1 - \left(1-\frac{a}{r}\right)^2\right]dr$$

$$= 2\int_a^\infty\left[\frac{2a}{r} - \frac{a^2}{r^2}\right]dr$$

$$= 2[2a\ln r + \frac{a^2}{r}]_a^\infty$$

The logarithm diverges! This is artifact of sharp cutoff. Properly regulated:

For hard sphere: $r_e = a$

**Physical meaning:** $r_e \sim$ range of potential; for hard sphere equals radius.

**Answer:** $r_e = a$ for hard sphere; general $r_e \sim$ potential range

## Resonances and Breit-Wigner Formula

A resonance occurs when a quasi-bound state (virtual level) lies near scattering energy. Phase shift passes rapidly through $\pi/2$.

### Example 4: Resonant Phase Shift

Near resonance energy $E_r$, the phase shift behaves as:

$$\delta_l(E) \approx \delta_{bg} + \arctan\left(\frac{\Gamma/2}{E - E_r}\right)$$

where $\Gamma$ is resonance width and $\delta_{bg}$ is background phase shift. Show this gives $\delta_l(E_r) = \delta_{bg} + \pi/2$.

**Solution:**

At resonance ($E = E_r$):

$$\delta_l(E_r) = \delta_{bg} + \arctan\left(\frac{\Gamma/2}{0}\right)$$

$$= \delta_{bg} + \arctan(\infty) = \delta_{bg} + \frac{\pi}{2}$$

**Away from resonance:**
- $E \ll E_r$: $\arctan \to -\pi/2$ → $\delta_l = \delta_{bg} - \pi/2$
- $E \gg E_r$: $\arctan \to +\pi/2$ → $\delta_l = \delta_{bg} + \pi/2$

Phase shift **increases by $\pi$** through resonance region (Levinson's theorem signature).

**Width $\Gamma$:** Energy range over which phase changes significantly.

At $E = E_r \pm \Gamma/2$: $\arctan(\pm 1) = \pm\pi/4$ → phase changed by $\pm\pi/4$ from resonance.

**Answer:** $\delta_l(E_r) = \delta_{bg} + \pi/2$; $\Gamma$ sets energy width of resonance

### Example 5: Breit-Wigner Cross Section

Substituting resonant phase shift into cross section formula:

$$\sigma_l(E) = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l$$

Show this gives Breit-Wigner form near resonance.

**Solution:**

For simplicity, take $\delta_{bg} = 0$:

$$\delta_l = \arctan\left(\frac{\Gamma/2}{E-E_r}\right)$$

$$\sin\delta_l = \frac{\Gamma/2}{\sqrt{(E-E_r)^2 + (\Gamma/2)^2}}$$

$$\sin^2\delta_l = \frac{(\Gamma/2)^2}{(E-E_r)^2 + (\Gamma/2)^2}$$

$$\sigma_l(E) = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)\frac{(\Gamma/2)^2}{(E-E_r)^2 + (\Gamma/2)^2}$$

**Breit-Wigner form:** Lorentzian peak centered at $E_r$ with FWHM $= \Gamma$.

Maximum at resonance: $\sigma_l(E_r) = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)$ (unitarity limit!)

**Answer:** $\sigma_l = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)\frac{\Gamma^2/4}{(E-E_r)^2 + \Gamma^2/4}$ (Breit-Wigner)

### Example 6: Resonance Lifetime

The width $\Gamma$ relates to lifetime of quasi-bound state: $\tau = \hbar/\Gamma$.

If a nuclear resonance has width $\Gamma = 1$ keV, what is its lifetime?

**Solution:**

$$\tau = \frac{\hbar}{\Gamma} = \frac{6.58 \times 10^{-16} \text{ eV·s}}{10^3 \text{ eV}}$$

$$= 6.58 \times 10^{-19} \text{ s}$$

**Interpretation:** State lives $\sim 10^{-18}$ s before decaying into continuum.

**Narrow resonance** ($\Gamma$ small): Long lifetime, well-defined energy

**Broad resonance** ($\Gamma$ large): Short lifetime, poorly-defined energy

Energy-time uncertainty: $\Delta E \cdot \Delta t \sim \hbar$ → $\Gamma \cdot \tau \sim \hbar$ ✓

**Answer:** $\tau \approx 6.6 \times 10^{-19}$ s (sub-femtosecond lifetime)

Resonance widths span enormous range: narrow nuclear resonances ($\Gamma \sim$ eV, $\tau \sim 10^{-15}$ s) to broad hadronic resonances ($\Gamma \sim$ GeV, $\tau \sim 10^{-24}$ s).

### Example 7: Complex Energy and Poles

Resonances correspond to poles of $S$-matrix in complex energy plane. For resonance at $E_r$ with width $\Gamma$:

$$E_{pole} = E_r - i\frac{\Gamma}{2}$$

Show this reproduces Breit-Wigner near the pole.

**Solution:**

Scattering amplitude near pole:

$$f_l(E) \sim \frac{1}{k}\frac{1}{E - E_{pole}} = \frac{1}{k}\frac{1}{E - E_r + i\Gamma/2}$$

Cross section:

$$\sigma_l \propto |f_l|^2 = \frac{1}{k^2}\frac{1}{(E-E_r)^2 + (\Gamma/2)^2}$$

Matches Breit-Wigner! ✓

**Physical interpretation:**
- Real part of pole: Resonance energy
- Imaginary part: Decay rate ($-i\Gamma/2$ gives exponential decay $e^{-\Gamma t/(2\hbar)}$)

**Bound state limit:** $\Gamma \to 0$ → pole moves to real axis → stable bound state

**Answer:** Pole at $E = E_r - i\Gamma/2$ gives Breit-Wigner cross section

### Example 8: Levinson's Theorem

Levinson's theorem states: For potential with $n$ bound states in $l$-channel,

$$\delta_l(k=0) - \delta_l(k=\infty) = n\pi$$

For $s$-wave with one bound state, what is $\delta_0(0)$?

**Solution:**

Assume $\delta_0(\infty) = 0$ (asymptotic free particle)

One bound state: $n = 1$

$$\delta_0(0) - 0 = \pi$$

$$\delta_0(0) = \pi$$

**Physical meaning:** Phase shift starts at $\pi$ (zero energy) and decreases to 0 (high energy).

**Scattering length:** At $k=0$ with $\delta_0 = \pi$:

$$k\cot\delta_0 = k\cot\pi = k \cdot \infty \to -\frac{1}{a_s}$$

Near $k=0$: $\delta_0 \approx \pi - ka_s'$ for some $a_s' > 0$

**Answer:** $\delta_0(0) = \pi$ for one $s$-wave bound state (Levinson's theorem)

## Applications and Examples

### Example 9: Feshbach Resonances

In ultracold atomic physics, Feshbach resonances occur when molecular bound state crosses atomic threshold as magnetic field varies.

Near resonance, scattering length:

$$a_s(B) = a_{bg}\left(1 - \frac{\Delta B}{B - B_0}\right)$$

where $B_0$ is resonance position and $\Delta B$ is width. What happens at $B = B_0$?

**Solution:**

At $B = B_0$: denominator vanishes → $a_s \to \infty$

This is a **resonance** (bound state at threshold).

**Tuning:** By varying $B$, can tune $a_s$ from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$.

**Applications:**
- BEC interactions controllable
- Efimov physics (three-body bound states)
- Precision spectroscopy

For $^{85}$Rb: Feshbach resonance at 155 G; $\Delta B \approx 11$ G

Achievable: $|a_s| > 10^4 a_0$ (Bohr radius)

**Answer:** $a_s \to \infty$ at $B=B_0$; allows precise tuning of interactions

### Example 10: Ramsauer-Townsend Effect

In electron scattering from noble gases (Ar, Kr), total cross section has **minimum** near $\sim 0.7$ eV.

This occurs when $s$-wave phase shift $\delta_0 = \pi$ (Levinson condition for bound state, but no actual bound state!).

Explain this paradox.

**Solution:**

For attractive well with specific depth/range, $\delta_0(k) = \pi$ at certain energy even without bound state.

This is **accidental zero** of scattering amplitude:

$$f_0 = \frac{1}{k}e^{i\pi}\sin\pi = 0$$

Cross section vanishes! (perfectly transmitted)

**Physical interpretation:** Partial cancelation of scattered waves; wave "fits" inside potential.

**Quantum tunneling:** At this energy, electron wavelength matches potential size → resonant transmission.

Similar to anti-reflection coating (optical analog).

**Answer:** Phase shift $= \pi$ gives $f_0 = 0$ → cross section minimum (Ramsauer-Townsend)

### Example 11: Nuclear Compound Nucleus

Neutron scattering from heavy nucleus shows many sharp resonances (compound nucleus formation).

Typical: $\Gamma \sim 0.1$ eV, spacing $\sim 10$ eV at MeV neutron energy.

Estimate compound nucleus lifetime.

**Solution:**

Lifetime: $\tau = \hbar/\Gamma$

$$\tau = \frac{6.58 \times 10^{-16} \text{ eV·s}}{0.1 \text{ eV}} = 6.58 \times 10^{-15} \text{ s}$$

**Physical meaning:** Neutron captured, shares energy with nucleons, forms quasi-stable compound nucleus for $\sim 10$ femtoseconds before decaying.

**Resonance spacing:** Level density in compound nucleus $\rho \sim 1/(10 \text{ eV})$

Statistical nuclear theory (Bohr) explains distribution.

**Answer:** $\tau \approx 6.6$ fs (compound nucleus lifetime)

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Scattering length defined by low-energy expansion: $k\cot\delta_0 = -1/a_s + \frac{1}{2}r_e k^2 + \cdots$ as $k \to 0$. This is **definition**, not approximation. For small $k$: $\delta_0 \approx -ka_s$ (approximate). Low-energy cross section: $\sigma_0 = 4\pi a_s^2$ (factor 4, not $\pi$). Scattering length universal parameter—different potentials with same $a_s$ give identical low-energy scattering. Can exceed potential range near resonance: $|a_s| \gg a$ when bound state near threshold.

**Problem 2: C**
Resonance occurs when $\delta_l(E_r) = \pi/2$ (modulo $\pi$, accounting for background). This maximizes $\sin\delta_l = 1$ → unitarity limit. Breit-Wigner formula: $\delta_l = \delta_{bg} + \arctan\frac{\Gamma/2}{E-E_r}$; at $E=E_r$, $\arctan(\infty) = \pi/2$. Physical: quasi-bound state at this energy. Phase increases by $\pi$ through resonance (Levinson). Not $0$ or $\pi$ which give $\sin\delta=0$ (zero scattering). Quarter-wave condition $\pi/4$ non-resonant.

**Problem 3: C**
Energy-time uncertainty: $\Delta E \cdot \Delta t \sim \hbar$. Resonance width $\Gamma$ is energy uncertainty; lifetime $\tau$ is time uncertainty: $\tau = \hbar/\Gamma$. Narrow resonance (small $\Gamma$) → long-lived ($\tau$ large). Broad resonance → short-lived. Examples: atomic excited states ($\Gamma \sim$ MHz, $\tau \sim$ ns); nuclear resonances ($\Gamma \sim$ eV, $\tau \sim$ fs); hadronic resonances ($\Gamma \sim$ GeV, $\tau \sim 10^{-24}$ s). Natural linewidth of spectral line determined by lifetime.

**Problem 4: B**
Levinson's theorem: $\delta_l(0) - \delta_l(\infty) = n_l \pi$ where $n_l$ = number of bound states in $l$-channel. Each bound state contributes $\pi$ to zero-energy phase shift. For $s$-wave with no bound states: $\delta_0(0) = 0$; one bound state: $\delta_0(0) = \pi$; etc. Topological result—robust to potential details. Provides count of bound states from scattering data. Related to Friedel sum rule (condensed matter). Not applicable to Coulomb (long-range).

**Problem 5: B**
Feshbach resonance: Magnetic (or optical) field tunes molecular bound state energy relative to atomic threshold. At resonance ($E_{bound} = 0$): scattering length $a_s \to \pm\infty$. Formula: $a_s(B) = a_{bg}(1 - \Delta B/(B-B_0))$. Enables precise control of interactions in ultracold atoms: tune from weakly to strongly interacting, explore BCS-BEC crossover, create Efimov states. Magnetic field shifts hyperfine states → changes binding energy. Optical Feshbach uses laser detuning. Revolutionary for quantum gas experiments.
