# Quantum Mechanics Topic 7.1: Cross Sections and Born Approximation

When particles collide, quantum mechanics replaces classical trajectories with probability amplitudes. A beam of particles scatters off a target, and detectors measure how many particles emerge at various angles. The fundamental observable is the **differential cross section** $d\sigma/d\Omega$—the effective area for scattering into solid angle $d\Omega$. Cross sections connect quantum mechanics to experiment: Rutherford's gold foil experiment revealed the atomic nucleus through angular distributions, electron scattering maps charge distributions in nuclei, and X-ray crystallography determines molecular structure by analyzing scattering patterns.

The theoretical challenge is computing the scattering amplitude $f(\theta)$ from which all cross sections follow. For weak potentials or high energies, the **Born approximation** provides a systematic perturbative approach. The incident wave $e^{ikz}$ is weakly perturbed by the potential, producing a scattered wave $f(\theta)e^{ikr}/r$ at large distances. Remarkably, the Born approximation gives the scattering amplitude as the **Fourier transform of the potential**—high-energy scattering probes short distances because large momentum transfer samples the potential's fine structure. This article develops scattering theory from first principles, deriving cross sections, the Born approximation, and the optical theorem that connects total cross section to forward scattering.

## Scattering Amplitude and Cross Sections

A plane wave $e^{ikz}$ (momentum $\hbar k$ along $z$) scatters off a localized potential $V(\mathbf{r})$. At large distances $r \to \infty$, the wavefunction has the asymptotic form:

$$\psi(\mathbf{r}) \sim e^{ikz} + f(\theta,\phi)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}$$

The **scattering amplitude** $f(\theta,\phi)$ contains all information about the scattering process.

### Example 1: Defining Differential Cross Section

The differential cross section relates scattering amplitude to observable quantities. Derive $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = |f(\theta,\phi)|^2$.

**Solution:**

**Incident flux:** $j_{inc} = \frac{\hbar k}{m}$ (particles per unit area per unit time)

**Scattered current:** From $\psi_{sc} = f(\theta)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}$:

$$j_{sc} = \frac{\hbar}{m}\text{Im}[\psi_{sc}^*\nabla\psi_{sc}] = \frac{\hbar k}{m}\frac{|f(\theta)|^2}{r^2}\hat{r}$$

**Particles scattered into $d\Omega$:** Through area $r^2d\Omega$ at distance $r$:

$$\frac{dN}{dt} = j_{sc} \cdot r^2d\Omega = \frac{\hbar k}{m}|f(\theta)|^2d\Omega$$

**Definition of cross section:** Effective area times incident flux equals scattered rate:

$$j_{inc} \cdot d\sigma = \frac{dN}{dt}$$

$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \frac{|f(\theta)|^2j_{inc}}{j_{inc}} = |f(\theta,\phi)|^2$$

**Physical interpretation:** $d\sigma$ is the effective area that produces scattering into solid angle $d\Omega$.

**Answer:** $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = |f(\theta,\phi)|^2$ (squared amplitude gives cross section)

### Example 2: Total Cross Section

The total cross section integrates over all angles. For spherically symmetric scattering (independent of $\phi$):

$$\sigma_{tot} = \int \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}d\Omega = 2\pi\int_0^\pi |f(\theta)|^2\sin\theta d\theta$$

If $f(\theta) = f_0$ (constant, isotropic scattering), compute $\sigma_{tot}$.

**Solution:**

$$\sigma_{tot} = 2\pi|f_0|^2\int_0^\pi\sin\theta d\theta$$

$$= 2\pi|f_0|^2[-\cos\theta]_0^\pi = 2\pi|f_0|^2(1-(-1))$$

$$= 4\pi|f_0|^2$$

**Physical meaning:** Total effective area for scattering in any direction.

For weak $s$-wave scattering at low energy: $f(\theta) \approx -a_s$ where $a_s$ is scattering length.

$$\sigma_{tot} = 4\pi a_s^2$$

**Answer:** $\sigma_{tot} = 4\pi|f_0|^2$ for isotropic; general case requires angular integration

Total cross section has dimensions of area (m² or more commonly barns, where 1 barn = 10⁻²⁸ m²). Nuclear cross sections are typically femtobarns to millibarns, while atomic cross sections are $\sim$Å² $\sim 10^{-20}$ m².

### Example 3: Rutherford Scattering (Classical Limit)

For Coulomb potential $V(r) = \frac{Z_1Z_2e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}$, the exact scattering amplitude is:

$$f(\theta) = -\frac{Z_1Z_2e^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0E}\frac{1}{\sin^2(\theta/2)}$$

where $E = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}$ is incident energy. Verify this gives Rutherford's classical formula.

**Solution:**

Differential cross section:

$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = |f(\theta)|^2 = \left(\frac{Z_1Z_2e^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0E}\right)^2\frac{1}{\sin^4(\theta/2)}$$

This is the **Rutherford formula**, originally derived classically.

**Low angle** ($\theta \to 0$): Cross section diverges—forward scattering dominates.

**High angle** ($\theta \to \pi$): Backscattering suppressed as $\sim 1/E^2$.

**Quantum vs classical:** Quantum derivation gives same result (Coulomb is special case where Born approximation is exact).

**Historical:** Rutherford's 1911 experiment with alpha particles scattered by gold foil confirmed $1/\sin^4(\theta/2)$ dependence, establishing nuclear model.

**Answer:** Rutherford formula recovered; forward scattering diverges for pure Coulomb

## The Born Approximation

For weak potentials, treat scattering perturbatively. The **Lippmann-Schwinger equation** relates exact and free solutions:

$$|\psi\rangle = |\phi\rangle + \hat{G}_0^+\hat{V}|\psi\rangle$$

where $|\phi\rangle = e^{ikz}$ is incident plane wave and $\hat{G}_0^+ = \frac{1}{E - \hat{H}_0 + i\epsilon}$ is the free Green's function.

### Example 4: First Born Approximation

Replace $|\psi\rangle$ on right side by $|\phi\rangle$ (assume weak scattering):

$$|\psi\rangle \approx |\phi\rangle + \hat{G}_0^+\hat{V}|\phi\rangle$$

Show this gives:

$$f(\theta) = -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int V(\mathbf{r})e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}d^3r$$

where $\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{k}_i - \mathbf{k}_f$ is momentum transfer.

**Solution:**

Green's function in position space:

$$G_0^+(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}') = -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\frac{e^{ik|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|}}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|}$$

For $r \to \infty$ and $\mathbf{r}' \sim 0$ (localized potential):

$$\psi(\mathbf{r}) \approx e^{i\mathbf{k}_i\cdot\mathbf{r}} - \frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int V(\mathbf{r}')e^{ik|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|}e^{i\mathbf{k}_i\cdot\mathbf{r}'}d^3r'$$

Asymptotically: $|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'| \approx r - \hat{r}\cdot\mathbf{r}'$ where $\mathbf{k}_f = k\hat{r}$

$$\psi \approx e^{i\mathbf{k}_i\cdot\mathbf{r}} - \frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}\int V(\mathbf{r}')e^{i(\mathbf{k}_i - \mathbf{k}_f)\cdot\mathbf{r}'}d^3r'$$

Comparing with $\psi \sim e^{ikz} + f(\theta)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}$:

$$f(\theta) = -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int V(\mathbf{r})e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}d^3r$$

**Physical interpretation:** Scattering amplitude is **Fourier transform** of potential!

**Answer:** Born gives $f(\theta) = -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\tilde{V}(\mathbf{q})$ where $\tilde{V}$ is Fourier transform

### Example 5: Momentum Transfer

The momentum transfer $\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{k}_i - \mathbf{k}_f$ has magnitude:

$$|\mathbf{q}| = 2k\sin(\theta/2)$$

Explain why large angle scattering probes short distances.

**Solution:**

Born approximation: $f(\theta) \propto \tilde{V}(\mathbf{q})$ (Fourier transform at wavevector $\mathbf{q}$)

**Small angle** ($\theta \to 0$): $|\mathbf{q}| \to 0$ → samples potential at long wavelengths (large distances)

**Large angle** ($\theta \to \pi$): $|\mathbf{q}| \to 2k$ → samples short wavelengths (small distances)

For high energy ($k$ large): $|\mathbf{q}|$ can be large even at moderate angles.

$$\lambda_q = \frac{2\pi}{|\mathbf{q}|} = \frac{\pi}{k\sin(\theta/2)}$$

**Physical meaning:** Scattering resolves potential features on scale $\sim \lambda_q$.

**Application:** High-energy electron scattering probes nuclear structure (GeV electrons resolve quarks).

**Answer:** $|\mathbf{q}| = 2k\sin(\theta/2)$; large $|\mathbf{q}|$ probes small distances via Fourier analysis

The Born approximation reveals a beautiful connection: scattering is essentially spectroscopy in momentum space, with different angles probing different Fourier components of the potential.

### Example 6: Yukawa Potential Scattering

For Yukawa potential $V(r) = V_0\frac{e^{-\mu r}}{r}$ (models nuclear forces), compute Born scattering amplitude.

**Solution:**

Need Fourier transform:

$$\tilde{V}(\mathbf{q}) = \int V_0\frac{e^{-\mu r}}{r}e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}d^3r$$

In spherical coordinates with $\mathbf{q}$ along $z$:

$$= V_0\int_0^\infty\int_0^\pi\frac{e^{-\mu r}}{r}e^{iqr\cos\theta}r^2\sin\theta dr d\theta$$

$$= 2\pi V_0\int_0^\infty re^{-\mu r}\left[\frac{e^{iqr} - e^{-iqr}}{iqr}\right]dr$$

$$= \frac{2\pi V_0}{iq}\int_0^\infty[e^{-(\mu-iq)r} - e^{-(\mu+iq)r}]dr$$

$$= \frac{2\pi V_0}{iq}\left[\frac{1}{\mu-iq} - \frac{1}{\mu+iq}\right] = \frac{4\pi V_0}{\mu^2 + q^2}$$

Born amplitude:

$$f(\theta) = -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2} \cdot \frac{4\pi V_0}{\mu^2 + 4k^2\sin^2(\theta/2)}$$

$$= -\frac{2mV_0}{\hbar^2(\mu^2 + 4k^2\sin^2(\theta/2))}$$

**Limit $\mu \to 0$:** Recovers Coulomb result (Yukawa → Coulomb as range → ∞)

**Answer:** $f(\theta) = -\frac{2mV_0}{\hbar^2(\mu^2 + 4k^2\sin^2(\theta/2))}$; forward scattering finite (vs Coulomb)

### Example 7: Validity of Born Approximation

Born approximation assumes perturbation is weak. Quantitative criterion:

$$\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\int_0^\infty |V(r)|r dr \ll 1$$

For square well $V(r) = -V_0$ for $r < a$, when is Born valid?

**Solution:**

Integral: $\int_0^a V_0 r dr = V_0\frac{a^2}{2}$

Criterion:

$$\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}V_0\frac{a^2}{2} \ll 1$$

$$\frac{mV_0a^2}{\hbar^2} \ll 1$$

Define dimensionless parameter: $\xi = \frac{mV_0a^2}{\hbar^2}$

**Born valid:** $\xi \ll 1$

**Physical interpretation:**
- Weak potential: $V_0$ small
- Short range: $a$ small
- High energy: $k$ large (enters through improved criterion $ka \gg 1$ for validity)

**Alternative form:** $V_0 \ll \frac{\hbar^2}{ma^2}$ (potential weak compared to kinetic energy scale)

**Answer:** Born valid when $\frac{mV_0a^2}{\hbar^2} \ll 1$ (weak coupling)

## The Optical Theorem

A remarkable connection exists between total cross section and forward scattering amplitude.

### Example 8: Deriving Optical Theorem

Using probability current conservation, show that:

$$\sigma_{tot} = \frac{4\pi}{k}\text{Im}[f(0)]$$

**Solution:**

Total wavefunction: $\psi = e^{ikz} + f(\theta)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}$

Probability current: $\mathbf{j} = \frac{\hbar}{m}\text{Im}[\psi^*\nabla\psi]$

**Conservation:** Flux through large sphere equals total scattering rate.

Detailed calculation (subtracting incident and scattered currents, using unitarity):

$$\int\mathbf{j}\cdot d\mathbf{A} = 0$$

This yields:

$$\sigma_{tot} = \frac{4\pi}{k}\text{Im}[f(0)]$$

**Physical interpretation:** Forward scattering amplitude's imaginary part measures total probability removed from incident beam.

**Unitarity:** Optical theorem is consequence of probability conservation.

**Answer:** $\sigma_{tot} = \frac{4\pi}{k}\text{Im}[f(0)]$ (total cross section from forward amplitude)

### Example 9: Optical Theorem Application

For hard sphere scattering (impenetrable sphere radius $a$), the exact forward amplitude at high energy is:

$$f(0) = ia$$

Compute total cross section.

**Solution:**

For hard sphere:

$$f(0) = -a + i\mathcal{O}(ka)$$

At high energy $(ka \gg 1)$:

$$\text{Im}[f(0)] \approx ka$$

$$\sigma_{tot} = \frac{4\pi}{k} \cdot ka = 4\pi a^2$$

Geometric cross section is $\pi a^2$. Factor of 4 comes from:
- Factor 2: Absorption removes particles
- Factor 2: Diffraction shadow (wave nature)

**Answer:** $\sigma_{tot} = 4\pi a^2$ (twice geometric due to diffraction)

The factor of 2 beyond geometric cross section is a purely quantum effect—classical hard sphere would give $\pi a^2$.

### Example 10: Born Approximation and Optical Theorem

Check that Born approximation satisfies optical theorem for real potential.

**Solution:**

Born amplitude: $f(\theta) = -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int V(\mathbf{r})e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}d^3r$

At $\theta = 0$: $\mathbf{q} = 0$

$$f(0) = -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int V(\mathbf{r})d^3r$$

For **real** potential: $f(0)$ is **real** → $\text{Im}[f(0)] = 0$

Optical theorem: $\sigma_{tot} = 0$ (no absorption!)

**Physical meaning:** Born approximation is first-order perturbation theory. At first order, elastic scattering redistributes flux but doesn't remove it. Total cross section comes from higher orders (accounting for multiple scattering).

**Answer:** Born gives real $f(0)$ → $\sigma_{tot} = 0$ at first order (flux conservation)

### Example 11: Complex Potential and Absorption

For complex potential $V(r) = V_0(r) + iW(r)$ where $W > 0$ represents absorption:

$$f(0) = -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int [V_0(\mathbf{r}) + iW(\mathbf{r})]d^3r$$

The imaginary part:

$$\text{Im}[f(0)] = -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int W(\mathbf{r})d^3r > 0$$

gives **absorption cross section** via optical theorem.

**Answer:** Complex potential → absorptive scattering; optical theorem relates to absorption

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Differential cross section defined by $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = |f(\theta,\phi)|^2$ where $f$ is scattering amplitude. Physical: probability flux into solid angle $d\Omega$ equals incident flux times effective area $d\sigma$. Must be real, positive → requires absolute value squared. Units: $[f] = $ length, $[d\sigma/d\Omega] = $ area/steradian. Forward scattering: $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\bigg|_{\theta=0}$ often largest. Total: $\sigma_{tot} = \int |f|^2 d\Omega$. Complex $f$ contains both magnitude (cross section) and phase (interference).

**Problem 2: B**
Born approximation: $f(\theta) = -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int V(\mathbf{r})e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}d^3r = -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\tilde{V}(\mathbf{q})$ where $\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{k}_i - \mathbf{k}_f$ is momentum transfer. Scattering probes Fourier transform—different angles sample different $\mathbf{q}$ values. High-energy/large-angle: large $|\mathbf{q}|$ → short-distance structure. Low-energy/small-angle: small $|\mathbf{q}|$ → long-distance. Beautiful connection: scattering is Fourier spectroscopy. X-ray crystallography uses this: crystal structure from diffraction pattern.

**Problem 3: B**
Momentum transfer: $\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{k}_i - \mathbf{k}_f$ where $|\mathbf{k}_i| = |\mathbf{k}_f| = k$ (elastic). Geometry: angle $\theta$ between $\mathbf{k}_i$ and $\mathbf{k}_f$ gives $|\mathbf{q}|^2 = 2k^2(1-\cos\theta) = 4k^2\sin^2(\theta/2)$. Therefore $|\mathbf{q}| = 2k\sin(\theta/2)$. Forward ($\theta=0$): $|\mathbf{q}|=0$; backward ($\theta=\pi$): $|\mathbf{q}|=2k$ (maximum). Determines resolution: $\lambda_q = 2\pi/|\mathbf{q}|$ is probed length scale.

**Problem 4: B**
Optical theorem: $\sigma_{tot} = \frac{4\pi}{k}\text{Im}[f(0)]$ where $f(0)$ is forward scattering amplitude. Consequence of unitarity (probability conservation). Imaginary part of forward amplitude measures total flux removed from incident beam. Real part gives forward elastic scattering. For absorptive potential (complex $V$): $\text{Im}[f(0)] > 0$ gives absorption cross section. First-order Born (real potential): $f(0)$ real → $\sigma_{tot}=0$ (flux redistributed but conserved). Fundamental check on scattering calculations.

**Problem 5: B**
High energy → large momentum $k$ → momentum transfer $|\mathbf{q}| = 2k\sin(\theta/2)$ can be large. Scattering amplitude $f \propto \tilde{V}(\mathbf{q})$ samples Fourier components at wavevector $\mathbf{q}$. Large $|\mathbf{q}|$ → short wavelength $\lambda_q = 2\pi/|\mathbf{q}|$ → resolves small distances. Example: GeV electron scattering ($k \sim 10^{-15}$ m$^{-1}$) probes nucleon substructure (quarks). Rutherford experiment: alpha particles ($\sim$ MeV) resolved nucleus ($\sim$ fm). Higher energy → better microscope.
