# Quantum Mechanics Topic 6.2: Absorption and Emission of Radiation

Atoms interact with light through their charged constituents—primarily electrons. When electromagnetic radiation illuminates an atom, the oscillating electric field can induce transitions between energy levels. **Absorption** occurs when a photon is destroyed and the atom jumps to a higher energy state. **Stimulated emission** happens when a photon triggers the atom to drop to a lower state, emitting an identical photon. **Spontaneous emission** is the decay of an excited state even without external radiation—a purely quantum phenomenon requiring field quantization. These three processes govern all light-matter interaction, from spectroscopy to lasers to the cosmic microwave background.

The theory begins classically: treat the electromagnetic field as an external perturbation $\hat{V}(t) = -\mathbf{d} \cdot \mathbf{E}(t)$ where $\mathbf{d} = -e\mathbf{r}$ is the electric dipole moment. For monochromatic radiation $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{E}_0\cos(\omega t)$, time-dependent perturbation theory gives transition rates. Absorption and stimulated emission have equal rates—Einstein's famous result predicting stimulated emission before its experimental observation. Spontaneous emission cannot be derived from classical fields; it emerges from quantum field theory when we quantize the electromagnetic field and account for vacuum fluctuations. The spontaneous emission rate involves the **Einstein A coefficient**, determining atomic lifetimes and spectral linewidths.

This article develops the theory of radiative transitions systematically. We'll derive absorption and emission rates using the electric dipole approximation, understand the Einstein A and B coefficients and their relationships, compute transition probabilities and lifetimes, and explore selection rules that determine which transitions are allowed. The formalism applies to atoms, molecules, quantum dots—any quantum system coupled to electromagnetic radiation.

## Electric Dipole Interaction

The interaction of an atom with electromagnetic field in the **dipole approximation** (wavelength $\lambda \gg$ atomic size) is:

$$\hat{V}(t) = -\mathbf{d} \cdot \mathbf{E}(t)$$

where $\mathbf{d} = -e\mathbf{r}$ is the electric dipole operator. For monochromatic field $\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r},t) = \mathbf{E}_0\cos(\omega t)$:

$$\hat{V}(t) = -\mathbf{d} \cdot \mathbf{E}_0\cos(\omega t)$$

### Example 1: Dipole Approximation Validity

For hydrogen atom with Bohr radius $a_0 = 0.53$ Å and visible light $\lambda = 500$ nm, verify the dipole approximation is valid.

**Solution:**

Dipole approximation requires: wavelength $\gg$ atomic size

$$\frac{\lambda}{a_0} = \frac{5 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}}{5.3 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m}} \approx 10^4$$

Wavelength is $\sim 10,000$ times larger than atom.

Over atomic dimensions, field is essentially uniform—spatial variation negligible.

This justifies treating $\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r},t) \approx \mathbf{E}(0,t)$ as constant in space.

**Corrections:** Higher multipoles (magnetic dipole, electric quadrupole) suppressed by factors $\sim (a_0/\lambda)^2 \approx 10^{-8}$.

For X-rays ($\lambda \sim a_0$): dipole approximation breaks down, need full treatment.

**Answer:** $\lambda/a_0 \sim 10^4 \gg 1$; dipole approximation excellent for optical transitions

### Example 2: Matrix Element Calculation

For hydrogen $2p \to 1s$ transition, the dipole matrix element along $z$ is:

$$d_z = -e\langle 1s|z|2p_0\rangle$$

Given $\langle 1s|r\cos\theta|2p_0\rangle = \frac{128\sqrt{2}}{243}a_0$, compute $|d_z|$.

**Solution:**

Using $z = r\cos\theta$:

$$d_z = -e \cdot \frac{128\sqrt{2}}{243}a_0$$

$$|d_z| = e \cdot \frac{128\sqrt{2}}{243}a_0 \approx 0.75 ea_0$$

This is order of magnitude estimate: $|d| \sim ea_0$ typical for allowed transitions.

**Oscillator strength:** Dimensionless measure of transition strength, $\sim 1$ for strong transitions.

**Forbidden transitions:** $d_{fi} = 0$ due to selection rules (parity, angular momentum).

**Answer:** $|d_z| \approx 0.75ea_0$; typical atomic dipole moment scale

## Absorption and Stimulated Emission

For radiation field $\mathbf{E}(t) = \mathbf{E}_0\cos(\omega t)$, Fermi's golden rule (continuous spectrum of photon modes) gives transition rate.

### Example 3: Absorption Rate

An atom in state $|i\rangle$ absorbs photon of frequency $\omega$ and transitions to $|f\rangle$. Using $\hat{V}(t) = -\mathbf{d} \cdot \mathbf{E}_0\cos(\omega t)$ and Fermi's golden rule, derive the absorption rate.

**Solution:**

Write $\cos(\omega t) = \frac{1}{2}(e^{i\omega t} + e^{-i\omega t})$.

Two terms: one at $+\omega$ (emission), one at $-\omega$ (absorption).

For absorption ($E_f > E_i$), resonance when $\omega = \omega_{fi} = (E_f - E_i)/\hbar$.

Matrix element: $V_{fi} = -\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{d}_{fi} \cdot \mathbf{E}_0$

$$\Gamma_{abs} = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\left|\frac{\mathbf{d}_{fi} \cdot \mathbf{E}_0}{2}\right|^2 \rho(\omega)$$

For electromagnetic field, "density of states" related to energy density.

Energy density: $u(\omega) = \frac{\epsilon_0|\mathbf{E}_0|^2}{2}$ (time-averaged)

$$\Gamma_{abs} = \frac{\pi}{2\hbar^2\epsilon_0}|\mathbf{d}_{fi}|^2 u(\omega)$$

**Answer:** $\Gamma_{abs} = \frac{\pi|\mathbf{d}_{fi}|^2}{2\hbar^2\epsilon_0}u(\omega)$ (proportional to field intensity)

### Example 4: Stimulated Emission

Stimulated emission occurs when atom in $|f\rangle$ (excited) drops to $|i\rangle$ (lower) triggered by photon of frequency $\omega = \omega_{fi}$.

Show that stimulated emission rate equals absorption rate.

**Solution:**

For stimulated emission: same perturbation, opposite transition direction.

Matrix element: $V_{if} = -\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{d}_{if} \cdot \mathbf{E}_0$

But $\mathbf{d}_{if} = \langle i|\mathbf{d}|f\rangle = \langle f|\mathbf{d}^\dagger|i\rangle^* = \mathbf{d}_{fi}^*$

For real operator $\mathbf{d}$: $\mathbf{d}_{if} = \mathbf{d}_{fi}^*$

$$|V_{if}|^2 = |V_{fi}|^2$$

Therefore:
$$\Gamma_{stim} = \Gamma_{abs}$$

**Einstein's result:** Stimulated emission rate equals absorption rate (assumes same field).

This predicted lasers before they were built (1917 vs. 1960)!

**Answer:** $\Gamma_{stim} = \Gamma_{abs}$ (equal matrix elements)

### Example 5: Einstein B Coefficient

Define Einstein B coefficient by $\Gamma_{abs} = B_{if}u(\omega)$. Express $B_{if}$ in terms of dipole matrix element.

**Solution:**

From Example 3:

$$\Gamma_{abs} = \frac{\pi|\mathbf{d}_{fi}|^2}{2\hbar^2\epsilon_0}u(\omega)$$

Defining $B_{if}$:

$$B_{if} = \frac{\pi|\mathbf{d}_{fi}|^2}{2\hbar^2\epsilon_0}$$

For unpolarized light (average over orientations):

$$B_{if} = \frac{\pi|\mathbf{d}_{fi}|^2}{6\hbar^2\epsilon_0}$$

(Factor 1/3 from angular average: $\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle = 1/3$)

**Units:** $B$ has dimensions $[\text{energy}^{-1}\text{volume}^{-1}\text{time}^{-1}]$

**Physical meaning:** Proportionality constant between rate and energy density.

**Answer:** $B_{if} = \frac{\pi|\mathbf{d}_{fi}|^2}{6\hbar^2\epsilon_0}$ (unpolarized radiation)

## Spontaneous Emission

Spontaneous emission cannot be derived from classical perturbation theory—requires quantum field theory where vacuum has fluctuations.

### Example 6: Einstein A Coefficient

The spontaneous emission rate is the **Einstein A coefficient**:

$$A_{fi} = \frac{\omega_{fi}^3|\mathbf{d}_{fi}|^2}{3\pi\epsilon_0\hbar c^3}$$

For hydrogen $2p \to 1s$ with $|\mathbf{d}_{fi}| \approx 0.75ea_0$ and $\hbar\omega_{fi} = 10.2$ eV, compute $A$ and lifetime $\tau = 1/A$.

**Solution:**

$$\omega_{fi} = \frac{10.2 \text{ eV}}{\hbar} = \frac{10.2 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}}{1.055 \times 10^{-34}} = 1.55 \times 10^{16} \text{ rad/s}$$

$$|\mathbf{d}_{fi}|^2 = (0.75e)^2a_0^2 = 0.56 e^2 a_0^2$$

$$A = \frac{(1.55 \times 10^{16})^3 \times 0.56 \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19})^2 \times (5.3 \times 10^{-11})^2}{3\pi \times 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \times 1.055 \times 10^{-34} \times (3 \times 10^8)^3}$$

Order of magnitude: $A \sim 10^8$ s$^{-1}$

Lifetime: $\tau = 1/A \sim 1.6$ ns

This matches experimental value for hydrogen $2p$ state!

**Answer:** $A \approx 6 \times 10^8$ s$^{-1}$; $\tau \approx 1.6$ ns

### Example 7: Scaling of Spontaneous Emission

The Einstein A coefficient scales as $\omega^3$. Why do optical transitions have much shorter lifetimes than radio transitions?

**Solution:**

Compare optical ($\lambda = 500$ nm, $\omega \sim 4 \times 10^{15}$ rad/s) to radio ($\lambda = 21$ cm, $\omega \sim 9 \times 10^9$ rad/s):

$$\frac{A_{optical}}{A_{radio}} \sim \left(\frac{\omega_{optical}}{\omega_{radio}}\right)^3 = \left(\frac{4 \times 10^{15}}{9 \times 10^9}\right)^3 \sim 10^{17}$$

**Optical:** $\tau \sim$ ns

**Radio (21-cm):** $\tau \sim 10^7$ years!

**Physical reason:** Spontaneous emission from vacuum fluctuations; higher frequency → more vacuum modes → faster emission.

Hydrogen 21-cm line metastable—survives long enough to observe in space.

**Answer:** $A \propto \omega^3$; optical $\sim 10^{17}$ times faster than radio

### Example 8: Einstein Relations

Einstein derived relations between A and B coefficients from thermodynamic equilibrium (detailed balance):

$$A_{fi} = \frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2 c^3}B_{fi}$$

Verify this matches our expressions.

**Solution:**

From Examples 5 and 6:

$$B_{fi} = \frac{\pi|\mathbf{d}_{fi}|^2}{6\hbar^2\epsilon_0}$$

$$A_{fi} = \frac{\omega^3|\mathbf{d}_{fi}|^2}{3\pi\epsilon_0\hbar c^3}$$

Taking ratio:

$$\frac{A_{fi}}{B_{fi}} = \frac{\omega^3|\mathbf{d}_{fi}|^2}{3\pi\epsilon_0\hbar c^3} \cdot \frac{6\hbar^2\epsilon_0}{\pi|\mathbf{d}_{fi}|^2}$$

$$= \frac{\omega^3 \cdot 6\hbar^2}{3\pi^2 c^3} = \frac{2\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2 c^3}$$

Close to Einstein relation (factors depend on conventions for $u(\omega)$ vs. $u(\nu)$).

**Physical meaning:** Ratio $A/B$ determined purely by photon phase space, independent of atomic details.

**Answer:** Einstein relation verified; ratio is universal function of $\omega$

## Transition Rates and Lifetimes

### Example 9: Lifetime and Branching Ratios

An excited state can decay to multiple lower states. If $A_{f_1 i}$ and $A_{f_2 i}$ are rates to two final states, what is total lifetime?

**Solution:**

Total decay rate: $\Gamma_{total} = A_{f_1 i} + A_{f_2 i}$

Lifetime: $\tau = \frac{1}{\Gamma_{total}}$

Branching ratio to state $f_1$:

$$BR_{f_1} = \frac{A_{f_1 i}}{\Gamma_{total}}$$

This is the fraction of decays going to $f_1$.

Conservation: $\sum_f BR_f = 1$

**Example:** If $A_1 = 10^8$ s$^{-1}$ and $A_2 = 3 \times 10^8$ s$^{-1}$:

$$\tau = \frac{1}{4 \times 10^8} = 2.5 \text{ ns}$$

$$BR_1 = 0.25, \quad BR_2 = 0.75$$

**Answer:** $\tau = 1/(A_1 + A_2)$; branching ratios give decay probabilities

### Example 10: Oscillator Strength

The **oscillator strength** is a dimensionless measure of transition strength:

$$f_{if} = \frac{2m\omega_{fi}}{3\hbar}|\mathbf{d}_{fi}|^2$$

For strong transitions, $f \sim 1$. For hydrogen $2p \to 1s$, estimate $f$.

**Solution:**

Using $|\mathbf{d}_{fi}| \sim ea_0$ and $\hbar\omega_{fi} = (3/4) \times 13.6$ eV $= 10.2$ eV:

$$f \sim \frac{2m \times (10.2 \text{ eV})}{3\hbar} \times (ea_0)^2$$

$$= \frac{2 \times 9.1 \times 10^{-31} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-18}}{3 \times 1.055 \times 10^{-34}} \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 5.3 \times 10^{-11})^2$$

Order of magnitude: $f \sim 0.4$

Strong allowed transition has $f \sim 0.1$ to $1$.

Forbidden transitions: $f \ll 1$ (can be $10^{-6}$ or smaller).

**Sum rule:** $\sum_f f_{if} = Z$ (number of electrons)—Thomas-Reiche-Kuhn sum rule.

**Answer:** $f_{2p\to1s} \approx 0.42$ (strong allowed transition)

### Example 11: Linewidth from Lifetime

An excited state with lifetime $\tau = 5$ ns has an energy uncertainty $\Delta E = \hbar/\tau$. What is the spectral linewidth $\Delta\nu$?

**Solution:**

Energy uncertainty: $\Delta E = \frac{\hbar}{\tau} = \frac{1.055 \times 10^{-34}}{5 \times 10^{-9}} = 2.1 \times 10^{-26}$ J

Frequency width:

$$\Delta\nu = \frac{\Delta E}{h} = \frac{1}{2\pi\tau}$$

$$= \frac{1}{2\pi \times 5 \times 10^{-9}} = 3.2 \times 10^7 \text{ Hz} = 32 \text{ MHz}$$

This is the **natural linewidth**—intrinsic quantum limit.

For visible light $\nu \sim 5 \times 10^{14}$ Hz:

$$\frac{\Delta\nu}{\nu} \sim 6 \times 10^{-8}$$

Extremely narrow relative width!

**Answer:** $\Delta\nu = 32$ MHz (natural linewidth); fractional width $\sim 10^{-8}$

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Dipole approximation valid when electromagnetic wavelength $\lambda \gg a_0$ (atomic size). Field approximately uniform over atom: $\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) \approx \mathbf{E}(0)$. For visible light: $\lambda \sim 500$ nm, $a_0 \sim 0.5$ Å → $\lambda/a_0 \sim 10^4$. Excellent approximation. Higher multipoles (magnetic dipole, electric quadrupole) suppressed by $(a_0/\lambda)^2 \sim 10^{-8}$. Breaks down for X-rays where $\lambda \sim a_0$. Justifies treating radiation-atom interaction as $\hat{V} = -\mathbf{d} \cdot \mathbf{E}(t)$ with constant field.

**Problem 2: C**
Einstein showed: $\Gamma_{stim} = \Gamma_{abs}$ (equal rates). Both involve same matrix element $|\mathbf{d}_{fi}|^2$ and same photon energy density. Difference: absorption goes $i \to f$ (upward), stimulated emission $f \to i$ (downward). Predicted lasers (1917) decades before invention (1960). In thermal equilibrium, absorption dominates at low energy (more atoms in ground state); stimulated emission can dominate if population inverted (laser condition). Key to laser operation: stimulated emission amplifies light coherently.

**Problem 3: C**
Spontaneous emission: $A_{fi} = \frac{\omega_{fi}^3|\mathbf{d}_{fi}|^2}{3\pi\epsilon_0\hbar c^3} \propto \omega^3$. Strong frequency dependence explains why optical transitions ($\omega \sim 10^{15}$ rad/s) have ns lifetimes while radio transitions ($\omega \sim 10^9$ rad/s) have $\sim 10^7$ year lifetimes. Physical origin: number of photon modes in vacuum scales as $\omega^2$ (phase space), and each mode couples with strength $\propto \omega$. Total: $\omega^3$. Makes optical spectroscopy possible; radio transitions metastable.

**Problem 4: C**
Natural linewidth from energy-time uncertainty: $\Delta E = \hbar\Gamma = \hbar/\tau$. In frequency: $\Delta\nu = \Gamma/(2\pi) = 1/(2\pi\tau)$. Fundamental quantum limit—cannot be reduced. For typical atomic transition ($\tau \sim$ ns): $\Delta\nu \sim 10$–$100$ MHz. Very narrow! Fractional width $\Delta\nu/\nu \sim 10^{-7}$ for optical. Additional broadening: Doppler (thermal motion $\sim$ GHz at room temp), pressure (collision broadening), power (strong field), instrumental. Natural width usually smallest except for very short-lived states.

**Problem 5: B**
Oscillator strength dimensionless: $f_{if} = \frac{2m\omega_{fi}}{3\hbar}|\mathbf{d}_{fi}|^2$. Strong allowed transitions: $f \sim 0.1$–$1$. Forbidden transitions: $f \ll 1$ (can be $10^{-6}$). Physical meaning: relates quantum transition to classical oscillator—$f=1$ means transition as strong as classical electron oscillating. Thomas-Reiche-Kuhn sum rule: $\sum_f f_{if} = Z$ (total electrons). Hydrogen $2p \to 1s$: $f \approx 0.4$ (strong). Selection rules determine whether $f = 0$ exactly (forbidden) or just small.
