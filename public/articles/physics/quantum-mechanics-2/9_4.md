# Quantum Mechanics Topic 9.4: Feynman Propagator

The journey from single-particle quantum mechanics to quantum field theory requires understanding how particles propagate—not just in space, but also how they can be created and destroyed. The **Feynman propagator** is the fundamental object that describes this: it's the amplitude for a fermion to travel from point $y$ to point $x$, including the possibility of creating and annihilating particle-antiparticle pairs along the way. Unlike the Schrödinger propagator (which only moves particles forward in time), the Feynman propagator can involve antiparticles moving backward in time—Feynman's revolutionary insight that simplified calculations enormously.

Mathematically, the Feynman propagator is the Green's function for the Dirac equation, the kernel that inverts the Dirac operator. In momentum space, it's simply $i/({\not}{p} - m + i\epsilon)$, where the crucial $i\epsilon$ prescription tells us how to handle the poles and ensures causality. This seemingly simple object encodes deep physics: the numerator structure contains information about spin, the denominator enforces the mass-shell condition for particles, and virtual particles (those appearing in intermediate states of Feynman diagrams) live in the complex plane between the poles.

This article develops the Feynman propagator from first principles, starting with the need for a Green's function, deriving it in momentum space, understanding the pole structure and its physical meaning, and seeing how it appears in Feynman diagrams. We'll work through explicit calculations, examine the role of time-ordering, and understand why the propagator's form is inevitable once we demand causality and Lorentz invariance.

## Green's Functions and the Dirac Equation

### Example 1: Defining the Green's Function

What differential equation should the Feynman propagator satisfy?

**Solution:**

The Dirac equation is:
$$(i{\not}{\partial} - m)\psi(x) = 0$$

For source term $\eta(x)$:
$$(i{\not}{\partial} - m)\psi(x) = \eta(x)$$

Define Green's function $S_F(x - y)$ such that:
$$(i{\not}{\partial}_x - m)S_F(x - y) = \delta^{(4)}(x - y) \cdot \mathbb{1}_4$$

where $\mathbb{1}_4$ is the $4 \times 4$ identity matrix.

Then the solution is:
$$\psi(x) = \int d^4y \, S_F(x - y)\eta(y)$$

**Answer:** Feynman propagator satisfies $(i{\not}{\partial}_x - m)S_F(x - y) = \delta^{(4)}(x - y)$ (Green's function for Dirac operator).

### Example 2: Momentum Space Propagator

Find the Feynman propagator in momentum space.

**Solution:**

Fourier transform:
$$S_F(x - y) = \int \frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4} e^{-ip \cdot (x-y)} \tilde{S}_F(p)$$

Apply Dirac operator:
$$(i{\not}{\partial}_x - m)S_F(x-y) = \int \frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4} e^{-ip \cdot (x-y)} ({\not}{p} - m)\tilde{S}_F(p)$$

For this to equal $\delta^{(4)}(x-y) = \int \frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4} e^{-ip \cdot (x-y)}$:

$$({\not}{p} - m)\tilde{S}_F(p) = \mathbb{1}_4$$

Therefore:
$$\tilde{S}_F(p) = ({\not}{p} - m)^{-1} = \frac{{\not}{p} + m}{p^2 - m^2}$$

using $({\not}{p} - m)({\not}{p} + m) = p^2 - m^2$ (from $\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu + \gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu = 2\eta^{\mu\nu}$).

**Answer:** Momentum space: $\tilde{S}_F(p) = \frac{{\not}{p} + m}{p^2 - m^2}$ (before $i\epsilon$ prescription).

### Example 3: The $i\epsilon$ Prescription

Why do we need the $i\epsilon$ term, and where does it go?

**Solution:**

The propagator has poles when $p^2 = m^2$:
$$p^0 = \pm\sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2 + m^2} = \pm E_{\mathbf{p}}$$

To define the Fourier integral, we need to specify how to go around these poles.

**Feynman prescription**: Shift poles slightly off real axis:
$$\tilde{S}_F(p) = \frac{i}{{\not}{p} - m + i\epsilon}$$

This moves poles:
- Positive energy pole: $p^0 = +E_{\mathbf{p}} - i\epsilon$ (below real axis)
- Negative energy pole: $p^0 = -E_{\mathbf{p}} + i\epsilon$ (above real axis)

Physical meaning:
- $p^0 > 0$: Particle propagating forward in time
- $p^0 < 0$: Antiparticle propagating backward in time (= particle backward)

**Answer:** $\tilde{S}_F(p) = \frac{i}{{\not}{p} - m + i\epsilon} = \frac{i({\not}{p} + m)}{p^2 - m^2 + i\epsilon}$ with poles at $p^0 = \pm E_{\mathbf{p}} \mp i\epsilon$.

## Calculating the Propagator

### Example 4: Explicit Form in Standard Representation

Write out the Feynman propagator matrix explicitly.

**Solution:**

$$\tilde{S}_F(p) = \frac{i({\not}{p} + m)}{p^2 - m^2 + i\epsilon}$$

where ${\not}{p} = \gamma^\mu p_\mu = \gamma^0 p^0 - \boldsymbol{\gamma} \cdot \mathbf{p}$.

In standard representation:
$$\gamma^0 = \begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ 0 & -I \end{pmatrix}, \quad \boldsymbol{\gamma} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \boldsymbol{\sigma} \\ -\boldsymbol{\sigma} & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

$${\not}{p} = \begin{pmatrix} p^0 I & -\boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf{p} \\ \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf{p} & -p^0 I \end{pmatrix}$$

$${\not}{p} + m = \begin{pmatrix} (p^0 + m)I & -\boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf{p} \\ \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf{p} & -(p^0 - m)I \end{pmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $\tilde{S}_F(p)$ is $4 \times 4$ matrix with structure encoding spin and particle/antiparticle.

### Example 5: Pole Decomposition

Express the propagator in terms of positive and negative energy projectors.

**Solution:**

Using completeness of Dirac spinors:
$$\sum_{s=1,2} u^{(s)}(p)\bar{u}^{(s)}(p) = {\not}{p} + m$$

$$\sum_{s=1,2} v^{(s)}(p)\bar{v}^{(s)}(p) = {\not}{p} - m$$

(evaluated at $p^0 = E_{\mathbf{p}}$ and $p^0 = -E_{\mathbf{p}}$ respectively)

Propagator can be written:
$$\tilde{S}_F(p) = \frac{i\sum_s u^{(s)}\bar{u}^{(s)}}{p^0 - E_{\mathbf{p}} + i\epsilon} - \frac{i\sum_s v^{(s)}\bar{v}^{(s)}}{p^0 + E_{\mathbf{p}} - i\epsilon}$$

First term: Particle poles (positive energy)
Second term: Antiparticle poles (negative energy)

**Answer:** $\tilde{S}_F(p) = i\sum_s \left[\frac{u^{(s)}\bar{u}^{(s)}}{p^0 - E_{\mathbf{p}} + i\epsilon} - \frac{v^{(s)}\bar{v}^{(s)}}{p^0 + E_{\mathbf{p}} - i\epsilon}\right]$

### Example 6: Residues and Physical States

Calculate the residue at the positive energy pole.

**Solution:**

Near $p^0 = E_{\mathbf{p}}$:
$$\tilde{S}_F(p) \sim \frac{i({\not}{p} + m)}{(p^0)^2 - E_{\mathbf{p}}^2}$$

$$(p^0)^2 - E_{\mathbf{p}}^2 = (p^0 - E_{\mathbf{p}})(p^0 + E_{\mathbf{p}}) \approx 2E_{\mathbf{p}}(p^0 - E_{\mathbf{p}})$$

Residue:
$$\text{Res}_{p^0 = E_{\mathbf{p}}} = \frac{i({\not}{p} + m)}{2E_{\mathbf{p}}}\bigg|_{p^0 = E_{\mathbf{p}}}$$

Using ${\not}{p}|_{p^0 = E_{\mathbf{p}}} + m = \sum_s u^{(s)}\bar{u}^{(s)}$:

$$= \frac{i}{2E_{\mathbf{p}}}\sum_s u^{(s)}\bar{u}^{(s)}$$

This projects onto positive energy spinor states.

**Answer:** Residue at $p^0 = E_{\mathbf{p}}$ is $\frac{i}{2E_{\mathbf{p}}}\sum_s u^{(s)}\bar{u}^{(s)}$ (projection onto particle states).

## Time-Ordering and Causality

### Example 7: Time-Ordered Product

Show that the Feynman propagator equals the time-ordered product of field operators.

**Solution:**

In quantum field theory:
$$S_F(x - y) = \langle 0 | T\{\psi(x)\bar{\psi}(y)\} | 0 \rangle$$

where $T$ is time-ordering operator:
$$T\{\psi(x)\bar{\psi}(y)\} = \begin{cases} \psi(x)\bar{\psi}(y) & t_x > t_y \\ -\bar{\psi}(y)\psi(x) & t_y > t_x \end{cases}$$

(minus sign from fermion anticommutation)

For $t_x > t_y$: particle created at $y$, destroyed at $x$ (propagates forward)

For $t_y > t_x$: antiparticle created at $x$, destroyed at $y$ (propagates backward = particle backward in time)

The $i\epsilon$ prescription implements this time-ordering in momentum space.

**Answer:** $S_F(x-y) = \langle 0 | T\psi(x)\bar{\psi}(y) | 0 \rangle$; time-ordering automatically implemented by pole prescription.

### Example 8: Causality Check

Verify that the propagator vanishes outside the light cone for free particles.

**Solution:**

For spacelike separation $(x - y)^2 < 0$, can choose frame where $t_x = t_y$.

In this frame, $\psi(x)$ and $\bar{\psi}(y)$ are at equal time.

Dirac field operators satisfy:
$$\{\psi_\alpha(x), \bar{\psi}_\beta(y)\} = 0 \quad \text{for spacelike separation}$$

Therefore:
$$T\{\psi(x)\bar{\psi}(y)\} = 0$$

(Each term cancels due to anticommutation)

Propagator vanishes: No causal influence outside light cone.

**Answer:** $S_F(x-y) = 0$ for spacelike $(x-y)^2 < 0$ (causality preserved).

### Example 9: Non-Relativistic Limit

Find the non-relativistic limit of the Feynman propagator.

**Solution:**

For non-relativistic particle: $E \approx m + \frac{\mathbf{p}^2}{2m}$ (small $|\mathbf{p}|$)

$$p^0 = E + i\epsilon \approx m + \frac{\mathbf{p}^2}{2m} + i\epsilon$$

$$p^2 - m^2 = (p^0)^2 - \mathbf{p}^2 - m^2 \approx 2m\left(\frac{\mathbf{p}^2}{2m}\right) = \mathbf{p}^2$$

Upper-left block of propagator (particle part):
$$\tilde{S}_F \approx \frac{i}{p^0 - m - \frac{\mathbf{p}^2}{2m} + i\epsilon}$$

This is the **Schrödinger propagator** in momentum space!

**Answer:** Non-relativistic limit recovers Schrödinger Green's function: $\frac{i}{E - \frac{\mathbf{p}^2}{2m} + i\epsilon}$.

## Feynman Diagrams and Applications

### Example 10: Propagator in Feynman Diagrams

Draw and interpret the Feynman diagram for a propagating electron.

**Solution:**

Feynman diagram:
```
y ----→---- x
```

- Straight line with arrow: Fermion propagator
- Arrow direction: Particle flow (from $y$ to $x$ if $t_x > t_y$)
- Mathematical: Factor of $\tilde{S}_F(p)$ in amplitude

For antiparticle (arrow reversed):
```
x ←---- y
```

Equivalent to particle going backward in time ($y$ to $x$ with $t_y > t_x$).

**Answer:** Straight line with arrow represents fermion propagator $\tilde{S}_F(p)$; arrow shows particle direction.

### Example 11: Virtual Particles

What does it mean for a particle to be "off mass shell" or "virtual"?

**Solution:**

Physical (on-shell) particles satisfy:
$$p^2 = m^2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2 + m^2$$

In propagator denominator: $p^2 - m^2 + i\epsilon$

**Virtual particles**: Internal lines in diagrams where $p^2 \neq m^2$ (off-shell)

These violate classical energy-momentum relation but appear in intermediate states.

Allowed by uncertainty principle: $\Delta E \Delta t \sim \hbar$

Virtual particles integrate over all momenta (not just on-shell).

Propagator gives amplitude for virtual states:
$$\frac{i}{p^2 - m^2} \quad \text{(no $i\epsilon$ needed for internal lines)}$$

**Answer:** Virtual particles: Off-shell ($p^2 \neq m^2$), appear in intermediate states, integrated over all momenta.

### Example 12: Electron Self-Energy

Describe the leading QED correction to the electron propagator.

**Solution:**

One-loop correction: Electron emits and reabsorbs virtual photon.

Diagram:
```
    ~~~~~
   /     \
--o-------o--
```

This modifies propagator:
$$\tilde{S}_F(p) \to \tilde{S}_F(p) + \tilde{S}_F(p) \cdot \Sigma(p) \cdot \tilde{S}_F(p) + \cdots$$

where $\Sigma(p)$ is the **self-energy**:
$$\Sigma(p) = -\frac{e^2}{4\pi^2\epsilon_0} \int \frac{d^4k}{(2\pi)^4} \frac{\gamma^\mu({\not}{k} + m)\gamma_\mu}{(k^2 - m^2)[(p-k)^2]}$$

This integral is **divergent**—requires renormalization (QED's foundational issue).

Physical effects: Lamb shift, anomalous magnetic moment.

**Answer:** Self-energy $\Sigma(p)$ from electron-photon loop; divergent, requires renormalization; predicts QED corrections.
