# Quantum Mechanics Topic 7.2: Partial Wave Analysis

When scattering potential is spherically symmetric, the scattering amplitude inherits this symmetry—it depends only on scattering angle $\theta$, not azimuthal angle $\phi$. This allows powerful decomposition into **partial waves**, each labeled by orbital angular momentum $l$. The incident plane wave, expanded in spherical waves of definite $l$, scatters independently in each angular momentum channel. Low-energy scattering is dominated by $s$-wave ($l=0$); higher energies require more partial waves. Each partial wave acquires a **phase shift** $\delta_l$ characterizing the scattering strength—a single number that contains all information about scattering in that channel.

Partial wave analysis converts a three-dimensional scattering problem into a sequence of one-dimensional radial equations, each including a centrifugal barrier. The phase shift $\delta_l$ measures how much the radial wavefunction is shifted relative to the free solution. At low energies, a single parameter—the **scattering length** $a_s$—characterizes all $s$-wave scattering, independent of potential details. This universality is crucial for ultracold atomic physics where different atoms can be modeled by their scattering lengths alone. Phase shifts also reveal **resonances**—sharp peaks in cross sections when a quasi-bound state nearly coincides with the scattering energy.

This article develops partial wave analysis systematically. We'll expand plane waves in Legendre polynomials, derive radial equations with centrifugal barriers, extract phase shifts from asymptotic behavior, compute cross sections from phase shifts, and understand the physical meaning of scattering lengths. Partial wave methods are essential for low-energy nuclear physics, atomic collisions, and ultracold quantum gases.

## Partial Wave Expansion

For spherically symmetric potential $V(r)$, expand scattering amplitude in Legendre polynomials:

$$f(\theta) = \sum_{l=0}^\infty (2l+1)f_l P_l(\cos\theta)$$

where $f_l$ is the **partial wave amplitude** for angular momentum $l$.

### Example 1: Expanding Plane Wave

The incident plane wave $e^{ikz}$ can be expanded in spherical Bessel functions:

$$e^{ikz} = \sum_{l=0}^\infty i^l(2l+1)j_l(kr)P_l(\cos\theta)$$

Asymptotically, spherical Bessel function: $j_l(kr) \to \frac{\sin(kr - l\pi/2)}{kr}$

Show how this connects to partial waves.

**Solution:**

For large $r$:

$$e^{ikz} \sim \sum_{l=0}^\infty i^l(2l+1)\frac{\sin(kr - l\pi/2)}{kr}P_l(\cos\theta)$$

$$= \sum_{l=0}^\infty(2l+1)\frac{e^{i(kr-l\pi/2)} - e^{-i(kr-l\pi/2)}}{2ikr}P_l(\cos\theta)$$

Each $l$ contributes an **incoming** and **outgoing** spherical wave.

**Scattering:** Potential modifies outgoing wave but not incoming wave (causality).

Modified form: Outgoing wave multiplied by $S_l = e^{2i\delta_l}$ (scattering matrix element)

$$\psi \sim \sum_{l=0}^\infty(2l+1)\frac{e^{i(kr-l\pi/2)}S_l - e^{-i(kr-l\pi/2)}}{2ikr}P_l$$

**Physical interpretation:** Each partial wave scatters independently; $S_l$ contains scattering information.

**Answer:** Plane wave is sum of incoming/outgoing spherical waves in each $l$-channel

### Example 2: Phase Shift Definition

Define phase shift $\delta_l$ through scattering matrix: $S_l = e^{2i\delta_l}$.

The asymptotic radial wavefunction is:

$$u_l(r) \sim A_l\sin(kr - l\pi/2 + \delta_l)$$

Explain why this form represents scattering.

**Solution:**

**Free particle** ($V=0$): $\delta_l = 0$ gives $u_l^{(0)} = A_l\sin(kr - l\pi/2)$

**With potential:** Phase shifted by $\delta_l$ relative to free solution.

$$u_l = A_l\sin(kr - l\pi/2 + \delta_l)$$

Using $\sin(x+\delta) = \sin x\cos\delta + \cos x\sin\delta$:

$$= A_l[\sin(kr-l\pi/2)\cos\delta_l + \cos(kr-l\pi/2)\sin\delta_l]$$

**Physical meaning:**
- $\delta_l = 0$: No scattering (free particle)
- $\delta_l > 0$: Attractive potential pulls wave inward (phase advanced)
- $\delta_l < 0$: Repulsive potential pushes wave outward (phase retarded)

**Answer:** $\delta_l$ measures phase shift relative to free solution; contains scattering info

Unitarity requires $|S_l| = 1$ (probability conservation), which is satisfied by $S_l = e^{2i\delta_l}$ with real $\delta_l$.

### Example 3: Partial Wave Amplitude

The partial wave amplitude relates to phase shift:

$$f_l = \frac{e^{2i\delta_l} - 1}{2ik} = \frac{1}{k}e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l$$

Show that total scattering amplitude is:

$$f(\theta) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{l=0}^\infty(2l+1)e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l P_l(\cos\theta)$$

**Solution:**

From scattering matrix: outgoing wave multiplied by $S_l = e^{2i\delta_l}$

Comparing modified wavefunction with $\psi \sim e^{ikz} + f(\theta)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}$:

Scattered part: $\psi_{sc} = \sum_l \frac{2l+1}{2ik}\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}(S_l - 1)P_l$

Identifying: $f(\theta) = \sum_l(2l+1)f_l P_l$ where

$$f_l = \frac{S_l - 1}{2ik} = \frac{e^{2i\delta_l} - 1}{2ik}$$

$$= \frac{e^{i\delta_l}(e^{i\delta_l} - e^{-i\delta_l})}{2ik} = \frac{e^{i\delta_l} \cdot 2i\sin\delta_l}{2ik}$$

$$= \frac{1}{k}e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l$$

**Answer:** $f_l = \frac{1}{k}e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l$; phase and magnitude determined by $\delta_l$

## Radial Schrödinger Equation

For spherically symmetric $V(r)$, separate variables: $\psi = R(r)Y_l^m(\theta,\phi)$. Defining $u(r) = rR(r)$:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2u_l}{dr^2} + \left[V(r) + \frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2mr^2}\right]u_l = Eu_l$$

The **effective potential** includes centrifugal barrier.

### Example 4: Centrifugal Barrier

The centrifugal term $\frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2mr^2}$ acts as repulsive barrier. For what energies can particles with $l > 0$ penetrate to small $r$?

**Solution:**

Effective potential: $V_{eff}(r) = V(r) + \frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2mr^2}$

Centrifugal barrier height at $r = a$ (characteristic potential range):

$$V_{cent}(a) = \frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2ma^2}$$

For $l=1$: $V_{cent} = \frac{\hbar^2}{ma^2}$

**Classical turning point:** $E = V_{eff}(r)$

For $E \ll V_{cent}$: Particle cannot reach small $r$ (barrier impenetrable)

**Low energy scattering:** Only $s$-wave ($l=0$) contributes because higher partial waves blocked by centrifugal barrier.

**Critical energy:** $E \sim \frac{\hbar^2l^2}{2ma^2}$ needed for $l$-wave to contribute.

**Answer:** $E \gtrsim \frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2ma^2}$ required for $l$-wave to penetrate barrier

### Example 5: Low-Energy s-Wave Dominance

At low energy $ka \ll 1$ where $a$ is potential range, show that only $s$-wave contributes significantly.

**Solution:**

For $l$-wave to contribute, must overcome centrifugal barrier to reach potential region.

**Penetration factor** through barrier of height $V_l \sim \hbar^2l^2/(ma^2)$ for energy $E = \hbar^2k^2/(2m)$:

$$P_l \sim \exp\left(-\int_a^{r_c}\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}V_l}dr\right) \sim \exp(-\text{const} \times l)$$

Also, angular momentum suppresses small-$r$ wavefunction: $R_l(r) \sim r^l$ near origin.

For $ka \ll 1$: $(ka)^{2l+1}$ suppression factor.

**Typical:** $\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_0} \sim (ka)^2$ (ratio of $p$-wave to $s$-wave)

For thermal neutrons ($E \sim 0.025$ eV, $\lambda \sim 2$ Å) scattering off nucleus ($a \sim 5$ fm):

$$ka \sim 10^{-2} \implies \sigma_1/\sigma_0 \sim 10^{-4}$$

**Answer:** $l$-wave suppressed by $(ka)^{2l}$ and centrifugal barrier; $s$-wave dominates at low $E$

### Example 6: Hard Sphere Phase Shift

For impenetrable sphere radius $a$ (infinite potential for $r < a$), the boundary condition is $u_l(a) = 0$.

For $s$-wave, this gives:

$$\delta_0 = -ka$$

Compute the $s$-wave cross section.

**Solution:**

Phase shift: $\delta_0 = -ka$ (from boundary condition)

Partial cross section:

$$\sigma_0 = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sin^2\delta_0 = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sin^2(ka)$$

**Low energy** $(ka \ll 1)$: $\sin(ka) \approx ka$

$$\sigma_0 \approx \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(ka)^2 = 4\pi a^2$$

**High energy** $(ka \gg 1)$: $\sin^2(ka)$ oscillates, average $= 1/2$

$$\sigma_0 \approx \frac{4\pi}{k^2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{2\pi}{k^2}$$

Total cross section (all partial waves): $\sigma_{tot} = 4\pi a^2$ (twice geometric)

**Answer:** $\delta_0 = -ka$; $\sigma_0 = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sin^2(ka)$ varying with energy

## Cross Sections from Phase Shifts

The total cross section is sum over partial wave contributions:

$$\sigma_{tot} = \sum_{l=0}^\infty\sigma_l = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sum_{l=0}^\infty(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l$$

Each partial wave has maximum cross section when $\delta_l = \pi/2$ (resonance).

### Example 7: Unitarity Limit

For given $l$, what is maximum possible partial cross section?

**Solution:**

$$\sigma_l = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l$$

Maximum when $\sin^2\delta_l = 1$ → $\delta_l = \pi/2$

$$\sigma_l^{max} = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)$$

**Physical interpretation:** Unitarity bound—cannot scatter more than incoming flux in that partial wave.

For $s$-wave ($l=0$):

$$\sigma_0^{max} = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}$$

At resonance: wavelength $\lambda = 2\pi/k$ gives $\sigma_0^{max} = 4\pi(\lambda/2\pi)^2 = \lambda^2/\pi$

**Answer:** $\sigma_l^{max} = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)$ at $\delta_l = \pi/2$ (unitarity limit)

### Example 8: Optical Theorem from Partial Waves

Show that partial wave expansion satisfies optical theorem.

**Solution:**

Forward scattering ($\theta = 0$): $P_l(1) = 1$ for all $l$

$$f(0) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{l=0}^\infty(2l+1)e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l$$

Imaginary part:

$$\text{Im}[f(0)] = \frac{1}{k}\sum_l(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l$$

Optical theorem: $\sigma_{tot} = \frac{4\pi}{k}\text{Im}[f(0)]$

$$= \frac{4\pi}{k} \cdot \frac{1}{k}\sum_l(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sum_l(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l$$ ✓

**Answer:** Partial wave formula consistent with optical theorem

The optical theorem's validity in partial wave form provides a powerful consistency check on phase shift calculations.

### Example 9: Scattering Length

At very low energy ($k \to 0$), the $s$-wave phase shift behaves as:

$$k\cot\delta_0 \to -\frac{1}{a_s}$$

where $a_s$ is the **scattering length**. Show that $\sigma_0 \to 4\pi a_s^2$.

**Solution:**

Low-energy: $\delta_0 \approx -ka_s$ (small)

$$\sigma_0 = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sin^2\delta_0 \approx \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(ka_s)^2 = 4\pi a_s^2$$

Independent of energy! (in zero-energy limit)

**Physical meaning:** $a_s$ is the universal low-energy scattering parameter.

- $a_s > 0$: Effective hard sphere radius (repulsive or weakly attractive)
- $a_s < 0$: No bound state, but attractive at short range
- $a_s \to \infty$: Threshold for bound state (resonance)

**Applications:** Ultracold atoms characterized by scattering length; Feshbach resonances tune $a_s$.

**Answer:** $\sigma_0 = 4\pi a_s^2$ at $k=0$; scattering length is low-energy universal parameter

### Example 10: Effective Range Expansion

Beyond leading term, the effective range expansion gives:

$$k\cot\delta_0 = -\frac{1}{a_s} + \frac{1}{2}r_e k^2 + \mathcal{O}(k^4)$$

where $r_e$ is **effective range**. For square well depth $V_0$ and range $a$:

$$a_s = a\left(1 - \frac{\tan\kappa a}{\kappa a}\right), \quad \kappa = \sqrt{\frac{2mV_0}{\hbar^2}}$$

What happens as $V_0$ increases?

**Solution:**

As $V_0$ increases: $\kappa a$ increases.

**Resonance:** When $\kappa a = \pi/2$ (first zero of $\tan$): denominator vanishes → $a_s \to \infty$!

This corresponds to a **bound state at threshold** ($E = 0$).

Beyond this: $a_s$ changes sign (goes through resonance).

**Physical meaning:** Scattering length diverges when bound state appears at zero energy.

**Feshbach resonance:** Tuning magnetic field changes $V_0$, scanning $a_s$ through resonance.

**Answer:** $a_s \to \pm\infty$ when bound state reaches threshold; sign changes across resonance

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Partial wave expansion exploits spherical symmetry: $f(\theta) = \sum_l(2l+1)f_l P_l(\cos\theta)$. Each angular momentum $l$ scatters independently. Reduces 3D problem to sequence of 1D radial equations. Phase shift $\delta_l$ characterizes scattering in $l$-channel. Essential for: low-energy nuclear/atomic scattering, quantum gases, neutron scattering. Not specific to energy regime—works at all energies, but low energy simplifies (few partial waves). Coulomb needs modification (long-range requires different treatment). For non-spherical: must use full $f(\theta,\phi)$.

**Problem 2: C**
Low energy: $ka \ll 1$ where $a$ is potential range. Centrifugal barrier $\sim \hbar^2l(l+1)/(2ma^2)$ blocks higher partial waves. Penetration factor $\sim (ka)^{2l}$ heavily suppresses $l>0$. Only $s$-wave ($l=0$) has no barrier—can reach small $r$ even at zero energy. Cross section ratio: $\sigma_l/\sigma_0 \sim (ka)^{2l}$. Thermal neutrons: $ka \sim 10^{-2}$ → $p$-wave suppressed by $10^{-4}$. Ultracold atoms ($k \to 0$): purely $s$-wave scattering. Higher $l$ contribute at higher energy.

**Problem 3: B**
Partial cross section: $\sigma_l = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l$. Maximum at $\sin^2\delta_l = 1$ (i.e., $\delta_l = \pi/2$, resonance). Gives $\sigma_l^{max} = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)$. This is **unitarity limit**—conservation of probability forbids larger cross section. For $s$-wave: $\sigma_0^{max} = 4\pi/k^2 = 4\pi\lambda^2/(4\pi^2) = \lambda^2/\pi$ where $\lambda = 2\pi/k$. Physical: cannot scatter more flux than incoming in that channel. Resonances saturate unitarity limit.

**Problem 4: C**
Scattering length defined by $k\cot\delta_0 \to -1/a_s$ as $k\to 0$. Can be positive (repulsive or weakly attractive) or negative (attractive but no bound state). Sign change at resonance (bound state at threshold). Examples: $a_s > 0$ for hard sphere; $a_s < 0$ for shallow attractive well; $a_s \to +\infty$ from below or $-\infty$ from above at resonance. Magnitude: $|a_s|$ can exceed potential range $a$ near resonance. Feshbach resonances tune $a_s$ continuously through zero.

**Problem 5: C**
Centrifugal potential: $V_{\text{cent}} = \frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2mr^2}$ from quantum angular momentum. Classical: $L^2/(2mr^2)$ with $L^2 = \hbar^2l(l+1)$. Not $l^2$ but $l(l+1)$ due to quantum commutation relations. Height at $r=a$: $\sim \hbar^2l(l+1)/(2ma^2)$. For $l \gg 1$: approximately $l^2$, but exact form is $l(l+1)$. Suppresses low-energy higher partial waves. Critical energy for $l$-wave: $E_l \sim \hbar^2l(l+1)/(2ma^2)$.
