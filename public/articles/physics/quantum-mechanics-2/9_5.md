# Quantum Mechanics Topic 9.5: Antiparticles and CPT

When Paul Dirac discovered his equation admitted negative energy solutions, he faced a profound puzzle. If electrons could have arbitrarily negative energy, what prevents them from radiating photons and cascading to $E = -\infty$? His ingenious solution—the "Dirac sea"—proposed that all negative energy states are already filled, and a hole in this infinite sea would appear as a positively charged particle. This prediction of the **positron** (discovered by Anderson in 1932) stands as one of theoretical physics' greatest triumphs. Modern quantum field theory reinterprets these ideas: both particles and antiparticles are excitations of the same quantum field, with creation operators $\hat{b}^\dagger$ producing antiparticles just as $\hat{a}^\dagger$ produces particles.

But antimatter is more than just "opposite charge"—it connects deeply to fundamental symmetries. The **CPT theorem** states that any local, Lorentz-invariant quantum field theory must be invariant under the combined operation of charge conjugation (C), parity (P), and time reversal (T). Individually, these symmetries can be violated (weak interactions violate P and CP), but their product CPT remains exact. This theorem has profound consequences: particles and antiparticles must have identical masses and lifetimes, and CPT violation would signal breakdown of local quantum field theory itself.

This article explores antiparticles from their origin in the Dirac equation through the CPT theorem. We'll see how charge conjugation transforms particles to antiparticles, understand pair creation and annihilation processes, examine the Dirac sea picture versus modern field theory, and prove the CPT theorem's essential features. The existence of antimatter—far from being a curious byproduct—is intimately connected to relativity, quantum mechanics, and the structure of spacetime.

## Charge Conjugation and Antiparticles

### Example 1: Charge Conjugation Operator

Define the charge conjugation operator for Dirac spinors.

**Solution:**

Charge conjugation $\hat{C}$ takes particle → antiparticle.

For Dirac equation:
$$(i{\not}{\partial} - m)\psi = 0$$

Charge conjugate should satisfy:
$$(i{\not}{\partial} - m)\psi^c = 0$$

Define: $\psi^c = C\bar{\psi}^T$ where $C$ is matrix satisfying:
$$C\gamma^\mu C^{-1} = -(\gamma^\mu)^T$$

In standard representation:
$$C = i\gamma^2\gamma^0 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & i\sigma^2 \\ -i\sigma^2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

Properties: $C^\dagger = C^{-1} = -C^T$ (unitary and antisymmetric)

**Answer:** Charge conjugation: $\psi^c = C\bar{\psi}^T$ where $C\gamma^\mu C^{-1} = -(\gamma^\mu)^T$.

### Example 2: Conjugating Positive Energy Spinor

Find the charge conjugate of a positive energy spinor $u(p)$.

**Solution:**

For positive energy: $({\not}{p} - m)u(p) = 0$

Charge conjugate: $u^c = C\bar{u}^T = C(u^\dagger\gamma^0)^T = C(\gamma^0)^T u^*$

Must satisfy: $({\not}{p} - m)u^c = 0$

Check:
$$({\not}{p} - m)C\bar{u}^T = C({\not}{p}^T - m)\bar{u}^T = C\gamma^0({\not}{p}^T - m)^T u^*$$

Using $C\gamma^\mu C^{-1} = -(\gamma^\mu)^T$:
$$= -C\gamma^0 C^{-1} C({\not}{p} - m)^T u^* = \gamma^0({\not}{p} - m)C u^* = 0$$

Result: $u^c$ is a negative energy spinor $v(-p)$ (antiparticle with opposite momentum).

**Answer:** Charge conjugation: $u^c = v(-p)$ (positive energy particle → negative energy antiparticle).

### Example 3: Electromagnetic Coupling Under C

Show how electromagnetic interaction transforms under charge conjugation.

**Solution:**

Dirac equation with EM field:
$$(i{\not}{D} - m)\psi = 0$$

where $D^\mu = \partial^\mu + ieA^\mu$ (minimal coupling)

Under charge conjugation: $\psi \to \psi^c$, $A^\mu \to -A^\mu$ (reverse EM field)

$$\left(i\gamma^\mu(\partial_\mu + ieA_\mu) - m\right)C\bar{\psi}^T = 0$$

Multiply by $C^{-1}$:
$$\left(iC^{-1}\gamma^\mu C(\partial_\mu + ieA_\mu) - m\right)\bar{\psi}^T = 0$$

Using $C^{-1}\gamma^\mu C = -(\gamma^\mu)^T$:
$$\left(-i(\gamma^\mu)^T(\partial_\mu + ieA_\mu) - m\right)\bar{\psi}^T = 0$$

Transpose:
$$\left(-i\gamma^\mu(\partial_\mu - ieA_\mu) - m\right)\psi = 0$$

This is Dirac equation with $e \to -e$ (opposite charge).

**Answer:** Under C: $e \to -e$ and $A^\mu \to -A^\mu$ (particles and antiparticles have opposite charge).

## Pair Creation and Annihilation

### Example 4: Threshold for Pair Production

Find the minimum photon energy to create an electron-positron pair.

**Solution:**

Initial: Single photon with energy $E_\gamma$, momentum $p_\gamma = E_\gamma/c$

Final: Electron and positron, each with mass $m_e$

Energy conservation: $E_\gamma = E_e + E_{e^+} \geq 2m_e c^2$ (threshold when both at rest in CM)

BUT: Cannot conserve both energy and momentum with single photon!

$$p_{initial} = E_\gamma/c, \quad p_{final} = 0$$

**Must have target** (nucleus for recoil):

With nuclear recoil (mass $M \gg m_e$):
$$E_\gamma^{threshold} = 2m_e c^2\left(1 + \frac{m_e}{M}\right) \approx 2m_e c^2 = 1.022 \text{ MeV}$$

(For electron-positron pair in practice)

**Answer:** Threshold: $E_\gamma \geq 2m_e c^2 = 1.022$ MeV (requires target for momentum conservation).

### Example 5: Pair Annihilation

Calculate the outcome of electron-positron annihilation at rest.

**Solution:**

Initial: $e^- + e^+$ at rest, total energy $= 2m_e c^2$, momentum $= 0$

Final: Must conserve energy and momentum

**Cannot be single photon**: Would have $p = E/c \neq 0$ (violates momentum conservation)

**Must be two (or more) photons**:

For two photons (most probable):
- Energy: Each carries $m_e c^2 = 0.511$ MeV
- Momentum: Equal and opposite to conserve $\mathbf{p}_{total} = 0$
- Angle: $180°$ apart (back-to-back)

$$e^- + e^+ \to \gamma + \gamma$$

Positron Emission Tomography (PET) uses this: Detect coincident $511$ keV photons.

**Answer:** Annihilation at rest: Two photons, each $511$ keV, emitted at $180°$ (conserves energy and momentum).

### Example 6: Lifetime and Decay Channels

Why is positronium unstable? What determines its lifetime?

**Solution:**

**Positronium**: Bound state of $e^+e^-$ (like hydrogen but with positron replacing proton)

Binding energy: $E_b \sim 6.8$ eV (half of hydrogen due to reduced mass $\mu = m_e/2$)

Unstable because $e^+$ and $e^-$ can annihilate.

**Lifetimes**:
- **Para-positronium** (singlet, $S = 0$): $\tau \sim 125$ ps → $2\gamma$ (C-even)
- **Ortho-positronium** (triplet, $S = 1$): $\tau \sim 142$ ns → $3\gamma$ (C-odd)

C-conservation: Photon system must match $e^+e^-$ charge parity.

Lifetime $\propto \alpha^{-n}$ where $n$ = number of photons (phase space suppression).

**Answer:** Positronium unstable to annihilation; para $\to 2\gamma$ (fast), ortho $\to 3\gamma$ (slow, C-conservation).

### Example 7: Creation from Vacuum

Describe vacuum pair production in strong electric field.

**Solution:**

In strong electric field $\mathcal{E}$:

Potential energy difference over Compton wavelength:
$$\Delta V = e\mathcal{E} \cdot \lambda_C = \frac{e\mathcal{E}\hbar}{m_e c}$$

For pair production: $\Delta V \sim 2m_e c^2$

Critical field:
$$\mathcal{E}_c = \frac{2m_e^2 c^3}{e\hbar} \approx 1.3 \times 10^{18} \text{ V/m}$$

**Schwinger mechanism**: Quantum tunneling through potential barrier creates pairs from vacuum.

Production rate:
$$\Gamma \sim e^{-\pi m_e^2 c^3/(e\hbar\mathcal{E})}$$

Exponentially suppressed for $\mathcal{E} \ll \mathcal{E}_c$, but measurable near critical.

Observable in ultra-intense lasers, heavy-ion collisions.

**Answer:** Critical field $\mathcal{E}_c \sim 10^{18}$ V/m; pairs tunnel from vacuum (Schwinger mechanism).

## The Dirac Sea Picture

### Example 8: Holes as Antiparticles

Explain how a hole in the Dirac sea behaves as a positron.

**Solution:**

**Dirac sea**: All negative energy states ($E < 0$) filled with electrons.

**Hole**: Missing electron in negative energy sea.

Properties:
1. **Energy**: Removing $-|E|$ electron → hole has energy $+|E|$ (positive!)
2. **Charge**: Missing $-e$ → hole has charge $+e$ (positive!)
3. **Momentum**: Removing $-\mathbf{p}$ → hole has momentum $+\mathbf{p}$

Hole behaves exactly like positron: positive energy, positive charge, same mass.

**Pair creation**: Photon excites electron from sea → electron in positive energy state + hole

**Pair annihilation**: Positive energy electron falls into hole → photons emitted

**Answer:** Hole in Dirac sea: positive energy, positive charge, same mass → behaves as positron.

### Example 9: Problems with Dirac Sea

What conceptual issues does the Dirac sea have?

**Solution:**

**Problems**:
1. **Infinite negative charge**: Filled negative energy states → infinite charge density
   - Requires "renormalization" to set vacuum charge to zero

2. **Bosons**: Picture only works for fermions (Pauli exclusion fills states)
   - Photons, pions, etc. cannot use Dirac sea

3. **Infinite energy**: Vacuum has infinite negative energy
   - Must subtract infinite constant (unnatural)

4. **Not manifestly Lorentz-invariant**: Filling states in one frame differs from another frame

5. **Formal issues**: Why is negative energy sea stable? What defines "filled"?

**Resolution**: Quantum field theory treats particles and antiparticles symmetrically as excitations.

**Answer:** Dirac sea: infinite energy/charge, only for fermions, frame-dependent; QFT resolves these issues.

### Example 10: Field Theory Reinterpretation

How does QFT reinterpret negative energy solutions?

**Solution:**

In **second quantization**:

Field operator:
$$\hat{\psi}(x) = \int \frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf{p}}}}\sum_s \left[\hat{a}_{\mathbf{p}}^s u^s(p)e^{-ip \cdot x} + \hat{b}_{\mathbf{p}}^{s\dagger} v^s(p)e^{+ip \cdot x}\right]$$

- $\hat{a}_{\mathbf{p}}^s$: Annihilates electron (positive energy)
- $\hat{b}_{\mathbf{p}}^{s\dagger}$: **Creates** positron (NOT negative energy electron)

Both terms contribute positive energy to Hamiltonian:
$$\hat{H} = \int d^3p \, E_{\mathbf{p}}\sum_s (\hat{a}_{\mathbf{p}}^{s\dagger}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{p}}^s + \hat{b}_{\mathbf{p}}^{s\dagger}\hat{b}_{\mathbf{p}}^s)$$

**Vacuum**: $\hat{a}|0\rangle = \hat{b}|0\rangle = 0$ (nothing, zero energy)

**Antiparticles**: Independent degrees of freedom, not "holes"

**Answer:** QFT: Antiparticles created by $\hat{b}^\dagger$, independent from particles; both have positive energy; vacuum is empty.

## CPT Theorem and Symmetries

### Example 11: CPT Transformation

Define the combined CPT operation and show its action on a Dirac spinor.

**Solution:**

**C (Charge conjugation)**: $\psi \to C\bar{\psi}^T$, $A^\mu \to -A^\mu$

**P (Parity)**: $\psi(t,\mathbf{r}) \to \gamma^0\psi(t,-\mathbf{r})$, $A^0 \to A^0$, $\mathbf{A} \to -\mathbf{A}$

**T (Time reversal)**: $\psi(t,\mathbf{r}) \to \mathcal{T}\psi(-t,\mathbf{r})$ where $\mathcal{T} = i\gamma^1\gamma^3 K$ ($K$ = complex conjugation)

**CPT combined**:
$$\psi(x) \xrightarrow{CPT} \eta_{CPT} \, C\gamma^0\mathcal{T}\psi^*(-x)$$

where $\eta_{CPT}$ is phase factor.

Transforms:
- Particle at $(t, \mathbf{r})$ → Antiparticle at $(-t, -\mathbf{r})$
- Flips all charges, spatial coordinates, and time

**Answer:** CPT: $\psi(x) \to C\gamma^0\mathcal{T}\psi^*(-x)$ (particle → antiparticle at spacetime-reversed point).

### Example 12: CPT Theorem Consequences

What physical predictions follow from CPT invariance?

**Solution:**

**CPT theorem** (Pauli, Lüders, Zumino): Any local, Lorentz-invariant QFT is CPT-invariant.

**Consequences**:
1. **Masses equal**: $m_{particle} = m_{antiparticle}$ (verified to $10^{-19}$ for $K^0/\bar{K}^0$)

2. **Lifetimes equal**: $\tau_{particle} = \tau_{antiparticle}$

3. **Magnetic moments opposite**: $\mu_{particle} = -\mu_{antiparticle}$

4. **Quantum numbers opposite**: Charge, baryon number, lepton number reversed

5. **Spectra identical**: Hydrogen and antihydrogen have identical spectra

Experimental tests:
- $m_p/m_{\bar{p}} = 1.00000000009(9)$ (equality to $10^{-10}$)
- Antihydrogen spectroscopy (ALPHA, BASE experiments)

**Answer:** CPT → particle/antiparticle have equal mass, lifetime; opposite charge, magnetic moment; same spectra.

### Example 13: CP Violation and CPT

If CP is violated (as in weak interactions), what does CPT theorem imply?

**Solution:**

**CPT invariance**: $(CPT)H(CPT)^{-1} = H$ (always true)

**CP violation**: $(CP)H(CP)^{-1} \neq H$ (observed in $K^0$, $B^0$ decays)

From CPT = (CP) · T:
$$CPT = CP \cdot T$$

If CP violated but CPT conserved, then **T must also be violated**.

Example: In $K^0 \to \pi^+\pi^-$ decay,
- CP violation: Different rates for $K^0$ vs $\bar{K}^0$
- T violation: Time-reversed process has different rate
- CPT: Combined transformation leaves physics invariant

**Direct T violation** observed (BaBar, 2012).

**Answer:** CPT conserved + CP violated → T must be violated; CP and T violations compensate.

**Problem 1: E**
Historically: Dirac sea (holes in negative energy states)
Modern QFT: Independent excitations (created by $\hat{b}^\dagger$)
Both interpretations give correct predictions
Dirac sea limited to fermions, infinite charge issues
QFT more general, elegant, manifestly covariant

**Problem 2: B**
Threshold: $2m_e c^2 = 1.022$ MeV
Energy to create both electron and positron
Plus: Need target for momentum conservation
Cannot produce pair from single photon in vacuum
Schwinger: Can create from vacuum in strong $\mathcal{E}$-field

**Problem 3: C**
CPT conserved (exact in all local, Lorentz-invariant QFT)
C, P, T individually can be violated (weak interactions)
CP violated (observed in $K^0$, $B^0$ decays)
T also violated (CPT = CP · T)
Poincaré (spacetime) symmetry required for CPT theorem

**Problem 4: C**
QFT: Antiparticles created by $\hat{b}^\dagger$ operators
Positive energy (contribute $+E$ to Hamiltonian)
NOT holes (that's Dirac sea interpretation)
Independent degrees of freedom
Causal (no faster-than-light propagation)

**Problem 5: B**
At rest: Two photons (minimum, most probable)
Each photon: $511$ keV, emitted at $180°$
Cannot be one (momentum conservation fails)
Three photons: Possible but suppressed ($\alpha$ factor)
Spin state determines channels (para vs. ortho)
