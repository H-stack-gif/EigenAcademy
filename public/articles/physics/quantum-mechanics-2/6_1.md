# Quantum Mechanics Topic 6.1: Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory and Fermi's Golden Rule

Time-independent perturbation theory handles static corrections to energy levels—but what happens when the perturbation itself changes with time? An atom in an oscillating electromagnetic field, a particle hit by a pulse, a quantum system coupled to a time-varying external potential—these require **time-dependent perturbation theory**. Instead of shifts in energy eigenvalues, we calculate **transition probabilities** between states. The perturbation induces quantum jumps, and we want to know: what is the probability of finding the system in a different state after time $t$?

The framework parallels time-independent theory but with crucial differences. We expand the time-dependent state $|\psi(t)\rangle$ in the basis of unperturbed energy eigenstates with time-dependent coefficients $c_n(t)$. The perturbation couples different states, causing the coefficients to evolve. First-order theory gives transition amplitudes proportional to the Fourier transform of the perturbation—revealing that **resonance** occurs when the perturbation frequency matches the energy gap. For transitions to a continuum of final states (like ionization or decay), we obtain **Fermi's golden rule**: the transition rate is proportional to the density of final states and the squared matrix element.

Fermi's golden rule is among the most practically important results in quantum mechanics. It governs radioactive decay, absorption spectra, scattering cross sections, and any process where a discrete state couples to a continuum. The rule's power lies in its simplicity: transition rate equals coupling strength squared times available phase space. This article develops time-dependent perturbation theory systematically, derives Fermi's golden rule, and explores the physics of resonance, energy conservation, and irreversibility in quantum transitions.

## Time-Dependent Framework

Consider Hamiltonian $\hat{H}(t) = \hat{H}_0 + \hat{V}(t)$ where $\hat{H}_0$ is time-independent with known eigenstates $\hat{H}_0|n\rangle = E_n|n\rangle$. The state evolves as:

$$|\psi(t)\rangle = \sum_n c_n(t)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle$$

The time-dependent Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi(t)\rangle = \hat{H}(t)|\psi(t)\rangle$ yields:

$$i\hbar\dot{c}_n(t) = \sum_m c_m(t)V_{nm}(t)e^{i\omega_{nm}t}$$

where $V_{nm}(t) = \langle n|\hat{V}(t)|m\rangle$ and $\omega_{nm} = (E_n - E_m)/\hbar$.

### Example 1: Setting Up Time-Dependent Problem

An electron initially in hydrogen ground state $|1s\rangle$ experiences perturbation $\hat{V}(t) = V_0\cos(\omega t)\hat{z}$ starting at $t=0$. Set up the equation for $c_{2p}(t)$.

**Solution:**

Initially: $c_{1s}(0) = 1$, all other $c_n(0) = 0$.

For $|2p\rangle$ state (specifically $|2,1,0\rangle$):

$$i\hbar\dot{c}_{2p}(t) = c_{1s}(t)\langle 2p|\hat{V}(t)|1s\rangle e^{i\omega_{2p,1s}t}$$

$$= c_{1s}(t) \cdot V_0\cos(\omega t)\langle 2p|z|1s\rangle e^{i\omega_0 t}$$

where $\omega_0 = (E_{2p} - E_{1s})/\hbar = E_1(3/4)/\hbar$.

Matrix element: $\langle 2p|z|1s\rangle$ is real (standard calculation gives specific value).

**Answer:** Coupled differential equation with driving frequency $\omega$ and natural frequency $\omega_0$

## First-Order Perturbation Theory

Assume perturbation is weak. To first order, $c_m(t) \approx \delta_{mi}$ (stays in initial state $|i\rangle$), giving:

$$c_f^{(1)}(t) = -\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^t V_{fi}(t')e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}dt'$$

The transition amplitude from $|i\rangle$ to $|f\rangle$ involves the Fourier transform of $V_{fi}(t)$.

### Example 2: Constant Perturbation

For constant perturbation $\hat{V}(t) = \hat{V}_0$ (turned on at $t=0$), compute $c_f^{(1)}(t)$.

**Solution:**

$$c_f^{(1)}(t) = -\frac{i}{\hbar}V_{fi}\int_0^t e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}dt'$$

$$= -\frac{i}{\hbar}V_{fi} \cdot \frac{e^{i\omega_{fi}t} - 1}{i\omega_{fi}}$$

$$= V_{fi}\frac{1 - e^{i\omega_{fi}t}}{\hbar\omega_{fi}}$$

Transition probability:

$$P_{i\to f}(t) = |c_f(t)|^2 = |V_{fi}|^2\frac{2(1 - \cos\omega_{fi}t)}{\hbar^2\omega_{fi}^2}$$

$$= |V_{fi}|^2\frac{4\sin^2(\omega_{fi}t/2)}{\hbar^2\omega_{fi}^2}$$

For $\omega_{fi} \neq 0$ (non-degenerate states), this **oscillates** in time—no permanent transition!

**Answer:** $P(t) = \frac{4|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2\omega_{fi}^2}\sin^2(\omega_{fi}t/2)$ (oscillatory, not irreversible)

### Example 3: Resonance Condition

For sinusoidal perturbation $\hat{V}(t) = 2\hat{V}_0\cos(\omega t)$, when does maximum transition occur?

**Solution:**

Write $\cos(\omega t) = \frac{1}{2}(e^{i\omega t} + e^{-i\omega t})$:

$$c_f^{(1)}(t) = -\frac{i}{\hbar}V_{fi}\int_0^t [e^{i(\omega_{fi} + \omega)t'} + e^{i(\omega_{fi} - \omega)t'}]dt'$$

Two terms with frequencies $\omega_{fi} + \omega$ and $\omega_{fi} - \omega$.

**Resonance**: When $\omega \approx \omega_{fi}$ (for $\omega_{fi} > 0$, absorption), the second term dominates:

$$c_f^{(1)}(t) \approx -\frac{i V_{fi}}{\hbar} \cdot \frac{e^{i(\omega_{fi} - \omega)t} - 1}{i(\omega_{fi} - \omega)}$$

For exact resonance $\omega = \omega_{fi}$: grows linearly with time!

$$c_f^{(1)}(t)\bigg|_{\text{resonance}} = -\frac{iV_{fi}t}{\hbar}$$

$$P(t) = \frac{|V_{fi}|^2 t^2}{\hbar^2}$$

**Physical interpretation:** Energy conservation $\hbar\omega = E_f - E_i$ maximizes transition.

**Answer:** Resonance at $\omega = \omega_{fi}$; probability grows as $t^2$ (until perturbation theory breaks down)

The resonance behavior demonstrates a fundamental principle: quantum transitions are most probable when energy is conserved. Off-resonance transitions are suppressed by energy-time uncertainty.

## Fermi's Golden Rule

For transitions to a **continuum** of final states (density of states $\rho(E_f)$), we sum over all accessible final states. Consider perturbation turned on for long time.

### Example 4: Deriving the Golden Rule

For constant perturbation coupling to continuum, the total transition rate is:

$$\Gamma = \sum_f P_{i\to f}/t$$

Use the identity: $\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\sin^2(\omega_{fi}t/2)}{(\omega_{fi}/2)^2 t} = \pi\delta(\omega_{fi})$

Show this leads to $\Gamma = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}|V_{fi}|^2\rho(E_f)$.

**Solution:**

Transition rate to states near energy $E_f$:

$$\Gamma = \int \frac{dP}{dt}\rho(E_f)dE_f$$

From Example 2, for large $t$:

$$\frac{dP}{dt} = \frac{4|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2\omega_{fi}^2}\sin^2(\omega_{fi}t/2) \cdot \frac{1}{t}$$

Using $\delta$-function limit and $\omega_{fi} = (E_f - E_i)/\hbar$:

$$\Gamma = \int |V_{fi}|^2 \cdot \frac{2\pi\hbar}{1}\delta(E_f - E_i)\rho(E_f)dE_f$$

Integrating over delta function:

$$\Gamma = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}|V_{fi}|^2\rho(E_f)\bigg|_{E_f = E_i}$$

**Fermi's golden rule**: Transition rate is proportional to coupling squared and density of states.

**Answer:** $\Gamma = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}|V_{fi}|^2\rho(E_f)$ with energy conservation $E_f = E_i$

### Example 5: Physical Meaning of Golden Rule

Explain each factor in Fermi's golden rule: $\Gamma = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}|V_{fi}|^2\rho(E_f)$.

**Solution:**

**Factor 1:** $|V_{fi}|^2$ — Coupling strength squared
- Larger matrix element → stronger coupling → faster transitions
- Selection rules determine if $V_{fi} = 0$ (forbidden transition)

**Factor 2:** $\rho(E_f)$ — Density of final states
- More available states → more "channels" for transition
- Phase space factor: higher dimension → larger $\rho$

**Factor 3:** $2\pi/\hbar$ — Dimensional constant
- Ensures correct units: $[\Gamma] = 1/\text{time}$

**Energy conservation:** Implicit through $\delta(E_f - E_i)$
- Only states with $E_f = E_i$ contribute

**Validity:** Long time ($t \gg \hbar/|V_{fi}|$), weak coupling, continuous spectrum

**Answer:** Rate = (coupling strength)² × (available phase space) × (dimensional constant)

### Example 6: Density of States

For free particle in 3D box of volume $V$, the density of states at energy $E$ is:

$$\rho(E) = \frac{V}{(2\pi\hbar)^3} \cdot 4\pi p^2 \frac{dp}{dE}$$

where $E = p^2/(2m)$. Compute $\rho(E)$ explicitly.

**Solution:**

From $E = p^2/(2m)$:

$$p = \sqrt{2mE}, \quad \frac{dp}{dE} = \frac{m}{p} = \sqrt{\frac{m}{2E}}$$

$$\rho(E) = \frac{V}{(2\pi\hbar)^3} \cdot 4\pi(2mE) \cdot \sqrt{\frac{m}{2E}}$$

$$= \frac{V}{(2\pi\hbar)^3} \cdot 4\pi \cdot 2mE \cdot \sqrt{\frac{m}{2E}}$$

$$= \frac{V}{(2\pi\hbar)^3} \cdot 4\pi m\sqrt{2mE}$$

$$= \frac{V(2m)^{3/2}}{2\pi^2\hbar^3}\sqrt{E}$$

**Physical interpretation:** Density increases as $\sqrt{E}$—more momentum states at higher energy.

**Answer:** $\rho(E) = \frac{Vm^{3/2}\sqrt{2E}}{\pi^2\hbar^3}$ (grows as $\sqrt{E}$)

## Applications and Examples

### Example 7: Radioactive Decay

An excited nuclear state $|i\rangle$ decays to continuum $|f\rangle$ via weak interaction with coupling $|V_{fi}|^2 \approx$ constant. If $\rho(E_f) = \rho_0$, what is the probability of remaining in initial state?

**Solution:**

Decay rate: $\Gamma = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}|V_{fi}|^2\rho_0$

For exponential decay: $P_i(t) = e^{-\Gamma t}$

This is **irreversible** because continuum has infinite number of states—system cannot return.

Lifetime: $\tau = 1/\Gamma$

Half-life: $t_{1/2} = \tau\ln 2$

**Validity:** Requires $\Gamma t \ll 1$ for perturbation theory, but golden rule gives correct exponential even for longer times.

**Answer:** $P_i(t) = e^{-\Gamma t}$ with $\Gamma = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}|V_{fi}|^2\rho_0$; irreversible decay

### Example 8: Ionization Rate

Hydrogen atom in ground state is irradiated by light of frequency $\omega > E_{ion}/\hbar = 13.6$ eV$/\hbar$. The ionization rate depends on density of continuum states.

For photoionization, $\rho(E) \propto \sqrt{E}$ (Example 6). How does ionization rate scale with photon energy above threshold?

**Solution:**

Energy of final state: $E_f = E_{1s} + \hbar\omega = -13.6\text{ eV} + \hbar\omega$

Photoelectron kinetic energy: $E_k = \hbar\omega - 13.6\text{ eV}$

$$\rho(E_f) \propto \sqrt{E_k} = \sqrt{\hbar\omega - E_{ion}}$$

Ionization rate:
$$\Gamma_{ion} \propto |V_{fi}|^2\sqrt{\hbar\omega - E_{ion}}$$

Near threshold ($\omega \approx E_{ion}/\hbar$): $\Gamma \to 0$ (Wigner threshold law).

Far above threshold: Rate increases but matrix element $|V_{fi}|^2$ may decrease.

**Answer:** $\Gamma_{ion} \propto \sqrt{\hbar\omega - E_{ion}}$ near threshold; vanishes at threshold

### Example 9: Two-Level System with Oscillating Field

A two-level system (ground $|g\rangle$ and excited $|e\rangle$ with gap $\hbar\omega_0$) is driven by $\hat{V}(t) = V_0\cos(\omega t)$. At resonance $\omega = \omega_0$, the probability oscillates as **Rabi oscillations**:

$$P_{g\to e}(t) = \sin^2(\Omega_R t/2)$$

where $\Omega_R = 2|V_{ge}|/\hbar$ is the Rabi frequency. Why doesn't this violate golden rule?

**Solution:**

Golden rule assumes **continuum** of final states, making transition irreversible.

Two-level system: **discrete** final state—system can return!

Result is **coherent oscillation** (Rabi flopping), not exponential decay.

$$P(t) = \sin^2\left(\frac{|V_{ge}|t}{\hbar}\right)$$

Population oscillates between states at Rabi frequency.

**When golden rule applies:** Coupling to many levels or continuum → phase randomization → irreversibility.

**Answer:** Golden rule requires continuum; discrete states give reversible Rabi oscillations

### Example 10: Width and Lifetime

An unstable state with decay rate $\Gamma$ has energy uncertainty $\Delta E \sim \hbar\Gamma$. This gives spectral line a **natural width**.

If $\Gamma = 10^8$ s$^{-1}$, what is the line width in Hz?

**Solution:**

Lifetime: $\tau = 1/\Gamma = 10^{-8}$ s

Energy uncertainty: $\Delta E \sim \hbar\Gamma$

Frequency width: $\Delta \nu = \Delta E/h = \Gamma/(2\pi)$

$$\Delta \nu = \frac{10^8}{2\pi} \approx 1.6 \times 10^7 \text{ Hz} = 16 \text{ MHz}$$

This is **natural linewidth**—fundamental limit from finite lifetime via energy-time uncertainty.

Additional broadening: Doppler, collision, etc.

**Answer:** $\Delta\nu \approx 16$ MHz (natural linewidth from lifetime)

### Example 11: Perturbation Theory Breakdown

First-order perturbation theory requires $|c_f(t)| \ll 1$. When does this break down for resonant transition?

**Solution:**

From Example 3, at resonance:

$$|c_f(t)| = \frac{|V_{fi}|t}{\hbar}$$

Perturbation theory valid when:

$$\frac{|V_{fi}|t}{\hbar} \ll 1$$

$$t \ll \frac{\hbar}{|V_{fi}|}$$

**Timescale:** Perturbation theory fails after time $\sim \hbar/|V_{fi}|$.

For longer times, must use exact solution (Rabi oscillations for two-level) or higher orders.

Golden rule derivation assumes $t \gg \hbar/|V_{fi}|$ but coupling to continuum—apparent contradiction resolved by irreversibility.

**Answer:** Breaks down for $t \gtrsim \hbar/|V_{fi}|$ (must use exact methods)

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
First-order amplitude involves time-integral with oscillating phase: $c_f^{(1)}(t) = -\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^t V_{fi}(t')e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}dt'$. This is essentially Fourier transform of perturbation. Resonance when perturbation frequency matches $\omega_{fi}$. For constant $V_{fi}$: gives $\sin(\omega_{fi}t/2)/\omega_{fi}$. For sinusoidal: two terms at $\omega_{fi} \pm \omega$. Linear in $V_{fi}$, not quadratic. Quadratic appears in probability $P = |c_f|^2$.

**Problem 2: C**
Fermi's golden rule derived for transitions to **continuum** of states: $\Gamma = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}|V_{fi}|^2\rho(E_f)$. Continuum essential for irreversibility—once system transitions, cannot return due to infinite number of final states with random phases. Discrete final states give Rabi oscillations (reversible). Examples: ionization, photoelectric effect, radioactive decay. All involve transition from bound state to continuum.

**Problem 3: B**
Resonance condition: perturbation frequency matches transition frequency $\omega = \omega_{fi} = (E_f - E_i)/\hbar$. This is **energy conservation** for absorption ($E_f > E_i$) or emission ($E_f < E_i$). At resonance, transition amplitude grows linearly with time (until perturbation theory breaks down). Off-resonance: oscillatory behavior, suppressed transition. Physical: photon energy equals atomic gap. Selection rules plus resonance determine transition rates.

**Problem 4: C**
Free particle in 3D: $\rho(E) = \frac{V(2m)^{3/2}}{2\pi^2\hbar^3}\sqrt{E}$ grows as $\sqrt{E}$. Comes from spherical shell in momentum space: $4\pi p^2 dp$ where $p = \sqrt{2mE}$. More phase space at higher momentum. Affects many phenomena: photoionization near threshold ($\Gamma \propto \sqrt{E}$), black body radiation, density of states in solids. 1D: constant; 2D: constant; 3D: $\sqrt{E}$.

**Problem 5: C**
Natural linewidth from energy-time uncertainty: unstable state with lifetime $\tau = 1/\Gamma$ has energy uncertainty $\Delta E \sim \hbar\Gamma$. Gives frequency width $\Delta\nu = \Gamma/(2\pi)$. Fundamental quantum limit—cannot be reduced. Additional broadening: Doppler (thermal motion), pressure/collision, power broadening (strong field), instrumental. Natural width typically smallest contribution except for very short-lived states. Example: hydrogen $2p$ state: $\tau \approx 1.6$ ns → $\Delta\nu \approx 100$ MHz.
