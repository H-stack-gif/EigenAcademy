# Quantum Mechanics 2 Topic 11.1: Feynman Path Integral Formulation

Imagine asking: "How does a quantum particle get from point A to point B?" In the Schrödinger formulation, the wavefunction evolves according to a differential equation. But Richard Feynman proposed a radically different picture: the particle simultaneously explores **every possible path** between A and B. Each path contributes an amplitude $e^{iS/\hbar}$ where $S$ is the classical action, and the quantum amplitude is the sum over all these contributions. This is the **path integral formulation**—the third great formulation of quantum mechanics alongside Schrödinger's wave mechanics and Heisenberg's matrix mechanics.

The path integral provides deep insights into the quantum-classical transition. In the classical limit ($\hbar \to 0$), rapid oscillations of $e^{iS/\hbar}$ cause destructive interference for all paths except those near the classical trajectory where the action is stationary. This recovers the principle of least action. The path integral also connects naturally to statistical mechanics through Wick rotation to imaginary time, where $e^{iS/\hbar}$ becomes $e^{-S_E/\hbar}$—the Boltzmann weight. Modern quantum field theory, quantum gravity, and string theory all rely heavily on path integral methods.

This article develops the Feynman path integral from first principles. We'll derive the propagator as a sum over paths, understand how classical mechanics emerges, solve exactly solvable cases (free particle, harmonic oscillator), explore the connection to statistical mechanics, and see why path integrals provide powerful computational and conceptual tools for quantum theory.

## The Quantum Propagator

The **propagator** (or Green's function) is the probability amplitude for a particle to travel from $(x_i, t_i)$ to $(x_f, t_f)$:

$$K(x_f, t_f; x_i, t_i) = \langle x_f | e^{-i\hat{H}(t_f - t_i)/\hbar} | x_i \rangle$$

This propagator contains complete information about quantum evolution. For time-independent Hamiltonians, we can expand in energy eigenstates.

### Example 1: Propagator in Energy Basis

Express the propagator using energy eigenstates $|n\rangle$ with energies $E_n$.

**Solution:**

Insert completeness: $\sum_n |n\rangle\langle n| = \mathbb{1}$

$$K(x_f, t_f; x_i, t_i) = \langle x_f | e^{-i\hat{H}(t_f - t_i)/\hbar} | x_i \rangle$$

$$= \sum_n \langle x_f | e^{-iE_n(t_f - t_i)/\hbar} | n \rangle \langle n | x_i \rangle$$

$$= \sum_n \psi_n(x_f) \psi_n^*(x_i) e^{-iE_n(t_f - t_i)/\hbar}$$

**Answer:** $K(x_f, t_f; x_i, t_i) = \sum_n \psi_n(x_f) \psi_n^*(x_i) e^{-iE_n(t_f - t_i)/\hbar}$ (spectral representation)

### Example 2: Free Particle Propagator

For a free particle ($V = 0$), derive the propagator directly.

**Solution:**

Free Hamiltonian: $\hat{H} = \frac{\hat{p}^2}{2m}$

Use momentum eigenstates: $|p\rangle$

$$K(x_f, t_f; x_i, t_i) = \int \frac{dp}{2\pi\hbar} \langle x_f | p \rangle \langle p | x_i \rangle e^{-ip^2(t_f - t_i)/(2m\hbar)}$$

$$= \int \frac{dp}{2\pi\hbar} e^{ip(x_f - x_i)/\hbar} e^{-ip^2(t_f - t_i)/(2m\hbar)}$$

Complete the square: $-\frac{p^2(t_f - t_i)}{2m\hbar} + \frac{ip(x_f - x_i)}{\hbar} = -\frac{(t_f - t_i)}{2m\hbar}\left[p - \frac{m(x_f - x_i)}{t_f - t_i}\right]^2 + \frac{im(x_f - x_i)^2}{2\hbar(t_f - t_i)}$

Gaussian integral: $\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-ap^2} dp = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}}$

**Answer:** $K = \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar(t_f - t_i)}} e^{im(x_f - x_i)^2/(2\hbar(t_f - t_i))}$

## Path Integral Formulation

Feynman's key insight: discretize time into $N$ intervals, insert completeness at each step, then take $N \to \infty$.

### Example 3: Two-Slit Path Integral

For a particle passing through a double slit, express the amplitude using path integrals.

**Solution:**

Paths split into two groups: those passing through slit 1 and those through slit 2.

Total amplitude: 
$$\langle x_f | x_i \rangle = \sum_{\text{paths through 1}} e^{iS_{\text{path}}/\hbar} + \sum_{\text{paths through 2}} e^{iS_{\text{path}}/\hbar}$$

This naturally gives interference:
$$|\langle x_f | x_i \rangle|^2 = |A_1|^2 + |A_2|^2 + 2\text{Re}(A_1^* A_2)$$

The cross term $2\text{Re}(A_1^* A_2)$ is the interference pattern.

**Answer:** Path integral automatically includes quantum interference from all paths

### Example 4: Discretized Path Integral

Divide time interval $T = t_f - t_i$ into $N$ steps of size $\epsilon = T/N$. Write the propagator.

**Solution:**

Insert completeness $N-1$ times:

$$K(x_f, T; x_i, 0) = \int dx_1 \cdots dx_{N-1} \prod_{j=1}^N K(x_j, j\epsilon; x_{j-1}, (j-1)\epsilon)$$

For small $\epsilon$, each short-time propagator:

$$K(x_j, \epsilon; x_{j-1}, 0) \approx \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar\epsilon}} \exp\left[\frac{i\epsilon}{\hbar}\left(\frac{m}{2}\left(\frac{x_j - x_{j-1}}{\epsilon}\right)^2 - V(x_{j-1})\right)\right]$$

This is $\exp[i\epsilon L/\hbar]$ where $L = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{x}^2 - V(x)$ is the Lagrangian!

**Answer:** $K = \lim_{N \to \infty} \int \prod_{j=1}^{N-1} dx_j \prod_{j=1}^N \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar\epsilon}} e^{i\epsilon L_j/\hbar}$

### Example 5: Continuum Limit

Taking $N \to \infty$, express the path integral in continuum notation.

**Solution:**

Define functional measure: $\mathcal{D}[x(t)] = \lim_{N \to \infty} \prod_{j=1}^{N-1} dx_j$

Action: $S[x] = \int_0^T L(x, \dot{x}, t) \, dt$

Path integral:
$$K(x_f, T; x_i, 0) = \int \mathcal{D}[x(t)] \, e^{iS[x]/\hbar}$$

where the integral is over all paths $x(t)$ satisfying $x(0) = x_i$ and $x(T) = x_f$.

**Answer:** $K = \int \mathcal{D}[x(t)] \exp\left[\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^T L \, dt\right]$ (Feynman path integral)

## Classical Limit and Stationary Phase

In the classical limit $\hbar \to 0$, the phase $S/\hbar$ oscillates rapidly. Only paths where $S$ is stationary contribute.

### Example 6: Stationary Phase Approximation

Show that in the classical limit, only the classical path contributes significantly.

**Solution:**

Consider paths $x(t) = x_{\text{cl}}(t) + \eta(t)$ where $\eta(0) = \eta(T) = 0$.

Expand action: $S[x_{\text{cl}} + \eta] = S[x_{\text{cl}}] + \frac{\delta S}{\delta x}\bigg|_{\text{cl}} \eta + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta^2 S}{\delta x^2}\eta^2 + \cdots$

For the classical path: $\frac{\delta S}{\delta x}\bigg|_{\text{cl}} = 0$ (Euler-Lagrange equation!)

For $\hbar \to 0$: Paths away from $x_{\text{cl}}$ oscillate rapidly and cancel by destructive interference.

Only the classical path survives:
$$K \approx A e^{iS_{\text{cl}}/\hbar}$$

**Answer:** Classical limit: Only action-extremizing path contributes (principle of least action)

### Example 7: Harmonic Oscillator Path Integral

For a harmonic oscillator, evaluate the path integral exactly.

**Solution:**

Lagrangian: $L = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{x}^2 - \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 x^2$

Classical action for path from $(x_i, 0)$ to $(x_f, T)$:

$$S_{\text{cl}} = \frac{m\omega}{2\sin(\omega T)}[(x_i^2 + x_f^2)\cos(\omega T) - 2x_i x_f]$$

Fluctuations around classical path give determinant factor:

$$K = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\pi i\hbar\sin(\omega T)}} \exp\left[\frac{i}{\hbar}S_{\text{cl}}\right]$$

**Answer:** Exact result matches operator method: $K = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\pi i\hbar\sin(\omega T)}} e^{iS_{\text{cl}}/\hbar}$

## Connection to Statistical Mechanics

Wick rotation $t \to -i\tau$ (imaginary time) connects quantum mechanics to statistical mechanics.

### Example 8: Euclidean Path Integral

Perform Wick rotation $t = -i\tau$. What happens to the path integral?

**Solution:**

Action becomes: $S = \int L \, dt = i \int L(-i d\tau) = -i \int (\frac{1}{2}m\dot{x}^2 - V) d\tau$

Define Euclidean action: $S_E = \int_0^\beta (\frac{1}{2}m\dot{x}^2 + V) d\tau$

Path integral: $K = \int \mathcal{D}[x] e^{iS/\hbar} \to \int \mathcal{D}[x] e^{-S_E/\hbar}$

This is exactly the partition function in statistical mechanics!

With $\beta = 1/(k_B T)$ and $\hbar = 1$:
$$Z = \text{Tr}(e^{-\beta \hat{H}}) = \int \mathcal{D}[x] e^{-S_E}$$

**Answer:** Wick rotation transforms quantum mechanics into statistical mechanics ($\beta = it/\hbar$)

### Example 9: Thermal Density Matrix

Express the thermal density matrix using Euclidean path integrals.

**Solution:**

Density matrix at temperature $T$:
$$\rho(x_f, x_i; \beta) = \langle x_f | e^{-\beta \hat{H}} | x_i \rangle$$

In Euclidean path integral:
$$\rho(x_f, x_i; \beta) = \int_{x(0)=x_i}^{x(\beta)=x_f} \mathcal{D}[x(\tau)] e^{-S_E[x]/\hbar}$$

Partition function: $Z = \text{Tr}(\rho) = \int dx \, \rho(x, x; \beta)$

This requires periodic paths: $x(0) = x(\beta)$.

**Answer:** $Z = \int_{x(0)=x(\beta)} \mathcal{D}[x] e^{-S_E/\hbar}$ (sum over closed loops)

## Applications and Generalizations

Path integrals extend to field theory, gauge theories, and quantum gravity.

### Example 10: Multiple Degrees of Freedom

For $N$ particles, how does the path integral generalize?

**Solution:**

Path integral becomes:
$$K = \int \mathcal{D}[x_1] \cdots \mathcal{D}[x_N] \exp\left[\frac{i}{\hbar}\int L(x_1, \ldots, x_N, \dot{x}_1, \ldots, \dot{x}_N) dt\right]$$

For field theory with $\phi(x, t)$:
$$Z = \int \mathcal{D}[\phi(x, t)] e^{iS[\phi]/\hbar}$$

Now integrating over all field configurations at each spacetime point.

**Answer:** Generalization to fields: Functional integral over all field configurations

### Example 11: Equivalence to Schrödinger Equation

Show that the path integral satisfies the Schrödinger equation.

**Solution:**

Consider short time evolution: $K(x_f, t + \epsilon; x_i, t)$

For small $\epsilon$:
$$K(x_f, t + \epsilon; x_i, t) = \int dx \, K(x_f, t + \epsilon; x, t) K(x, t; x_i, t)$$

$$\approx \int dx \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar\epsilon}} e^{i\epsilon L(x)/\hbar} \delta(x - x_i)$$

Expand in $\epsilon$:
$$K(x_f, t + \epsilon; x_i, t) = \delta(x_f - x_i) + \frac{\epsilon}{i\hbar}\langle x_f | \hat{H} | x_i \rangle + O(\epsilon^2)$$

This is equivalent to: $i\hbar \frac{\partial K}{\partial t} = \hat{H} K$

**Answer:** Path integral formulation is equivalent to Schrödinger equation

### Example 12: Double-Well Potential

For a double-well potential, what paths contribute at low temperature?

**Solution:**

In Euclidean time, minima at $x = \pm a$.

Low temperature ($\beta \to \infty$): System tunnels between wells.

Dominant paths: **Instantons**—classical solutions in imaginary time that interpolate between wells.

Action of instanton: $S_{\text{inst}} \sim \hbar\omega_0/\alpha$ where $\alpha$ is barrier penetration parameter.

Tunneling amplitude: $\Delta E \sim e^{-S_{\text{inst}}/\hbar}$ (exponentially small)

**Answer:** Instantons (tunneling trajectories in imaginary time) give nonperturbative effects

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
Action $S$ is real, so $e^{iS/\hbar}$ is a pure phase
$|e^{iS/\hbar}| = 1$ always (probability amplitude)
Different paths contribute different phases
Interference from phase differences
Not a probability (which must be real and positive)

**Problem 2: C**
Stationary phase approximation: $\hbar \to 0$
Phase $S/\hbar$ oscillates rapidly for $\hbar \to 0$
Destructive interference except where $\delta S = 0$
Euler-Lagrange equations: $\frac{\delta S}{\delta x} = 0$
Recovers classical principle of least action

**Problem 3: C**
Wick rotation: $t \to -i\tau$ (imaginary time)
$e^{iS/\hbar} \to e^{-S_E/\hbar}$ (Boltzmann weight)
$S_E = \int (\frac{1}{2}m\dot{x}^2 + V) d\tau$ (Euclidean action)
Connects QM to statistical mechanics at $\beta = 1/(k_B T)$
Partition function: $Z = \text{Tr}(e^{-\beta H})$

**Problem 4: B**
Free particle: $K = \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar T}} \exp[\frac{im(x_f - x_i)^2}{2\hbar T}]$
Quadratic in displacement: $(x_f - x_i)^2$
Inverse in time: $1/T$ where $T = t_f - t_i$
Gaussian form (completing the square in momentum integral)
Classical action for free particle: $S = \frac{m(x_f - x_i)^2}{2T}$

**Problem 5: B**
Path integral sums over ALL paths
Each contributes amplitude $e^{iS/\hbar}$
Interference automatic (cross terms in $|\sum e^{iS}|^2$)
Double-slit: naturally includes both paths
Spin requires additional path integral (Grassmann variables for fermions)
