# Quantum Mechanics 2 Topic 11.2: Gauge Theories and Aharonov-Bohm Effect

In classical electromagnetism, only the electric field $\mathbf{E}$ and magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ are considered physically real. The potentials $\phi$ and $\mathbf{A}$ are viewed as mathematical conveniences—you can change them by a gauge transformation without affecting the fields. But quantum mechanics tells a different story. In 1959, Yakir Aharonov and David Bohm predicted that electrons can detect the vector potential $\mathbf{A}$ even in regions where the magnetic field $\mathbf{B} = \nabla \times \mathbf{A}$ vanishes completely. This is the **Aharonov-Bohm effect**: a purely quantum, topological phenomenon demonstrating that gauge potentials are physically observable.

The Aharonov-Bohm effect revealed deep connections between quantum mechanics, gauge theory, and topology. It shows that quantum mechanics is fundamentally nonlocal—the electron's phase is affected by fields in regions it never enters. This insight became crucial for understanding gauge theories in particle physics, where forces arise from local gauge symmetries. Berry's phase, discovered in 1984, generalized the Aharonov-Bohm effect to parameter spaces, showing that geometric phases appear whenever quantum systems undergo cyclic evolution.

This article explores gauge theories in quantum mechanics. We'll understand minimal coupling, derive the Aharonov-Bohm phase shift, examine experimental verification, explore the connection to topology and fiber bundles, and see how Berry's phase extends these ideas to general parameter spaces. The Aharonov-Bohm effect exemplifies how quantum mechanics challenges classical intuitions about locality and physical reality.

## Gauge Transformations in Quantum Mechanics

In electromagnetism, potentials $\phi$ and $\mathbf{A}$ are defined by $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla\phi - \frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial t}$ and $\mathbf{B} = \nabla \times \mathbf{A}$.

### Example 1: Gauge Transformation of Potentials

Show that the gauge transformation $\mathbf{A} \to \mathbf{A} + \nabla\chi$ and $\phi \to \phi - \frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t}$ leaves $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ unchanged.

**Solution:**

New magnetic field:
$$\mathbf{B}' = \nabla \times \mathbf{A}' = \nabla \times (\mathbf{A} + \nabla\chi) = \nabla \times \mathbf{A} + \nabla \times (\nabla\chi) = \mathbf{B}$$

(since $\nabla \times (\nabla\chi) = 0$)

New electric field:
$$\mathbf{E}' = -\nabla\phi' - \frac{\partial\mathbf{A}'}{\partial t}$$

$$= -\nabla(\phi - \frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t}) - \frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\mathbf{A} + \nabla\chi)$$

$$= -\nabla\phi + \frac{\partial\nabla\chi}{\partial t} - \frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial t} - \frac{\partial\nabla\chi}{\partial t} = \mathbf{E}$$

**Answer:** Fields $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ are gauge-invariant

### Example 2: Quantum Wavefunction Transformation

Under a gauge transformation, how must the wavefunction transform to keep physics unchanged?

**Solution:**

Schrödinger equation with EM field (minimal coupling):
$$i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{2m}(\hat{\mathbf{p}} - e\mathbf{A})^2\psi + e\phi\psi$$

Under gauge transformation $\mathbf{A} \to \mathbf{A} + \nabla\chi$, $\phi \to \phi - \frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t}$:

Wavefunction must transform:
$$\psi \to \psi' = e^{ie\chi/\hbar}\psi$$

Verify: $\hat{\mathbf{p}}\psi' = -i\hbar\nabla(e^{ie\chi/\hbar}\psi) = e^{ie\chi/\hbar}(-i\hbar\nabla + e\nabla\chi)\psi$

So: $(\hat{\mathbf{p}} - e\mathbf{A}')\psi' = e^{ie\chi/\hbar}(\hat{\mathbf{p}} - e\mathbf{A})\psi$

**Answer:** Wavefunction transforms as $\psi \to e^{ie\chi/\hbar}\psi$ (gauge transformation of phase)

### Example 3: Gauge-Invariant Observable

Show that probability density $|\psi|^2$ is gauge-invariant.

**Solution:**

Under gauge transformation: $\psi \to \psi' = e^{ie\chi/\hbar}\psi$

Probability density:
$$|\psi'|^2 = |e^{ie\chi/\hbar}\psi|^2 = |e^{ie\chi/\hbar}|^2|\psi|^2 = |\psi|^2$$

(since $|e^{i\theta}| = 1$)

Similarly, current:
$$\mathbf{j} = \frac{\hbar}{2mi}(\psi^*\nabla\psi - \psi\nabla\psi^*) - \frac{e}{m}|\psi|^2\mathbf{A}$$

is gauge-invariant (verify as exercise).

**Answer:** All physical observables are gauge-invariant

## The Aharonov-Bohm Effect

Consider a solenoid producing magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ confined inside. Outside: $\mathbf{B} = 0$ but $\mathbf{A} \neq 0$.

### Example 4: Vector Potential Outside Solenoid

For an infinite solenoid along the $z$-axis with flux $\Phi_B$ through it, find $\mathbf{A}$ outside.

**Solution:**

Cylindrical symmetry: $\mathbf{A} = A_\phi(r)\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$

Inside solenoid: $\mathbf{B} = B_0\hat{\mathbf{z}}$

Outside: $\mathbf{B} = \nabla \times \mathbf{A} = 0$

But: $\oint \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \Phi_B$ (Stokes' theorem)

For circle of radius $r > R$ (solenoid radius):
$$2\pi r A_\phi = \Phi_B$$

$$A_\phi = \frac{\Phi_B}{2\pi r}$$

**Answer:** $\mathbf{A} = \frac{\Phi_B}{2\pi r}\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$ outside solenoid (field-free but $\mathbf{A} \neq 0$)

### Example 5: Phase Shift from Vector Potential

An electron travels along path $C$ in region with $\mathbf{B} = 0$ but $\mathbf{A} \neq 0$. Find the acquired phase.

**Solution:**

Schrödinger equation with $\mathbf{A}$:
$$i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{2m}(\hat{\mathbf{p}} - e\mathbf{A})^2\psi$$

For particle moving along classical path, the wavefunction acquires phase:

$$\psi(t) = \psi_0 \exp\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int (E - \frac{e\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{c}) dt\right]$$

Phase from $\mathbf{A}$:
$$\Delta\phi = -\frac{e}{\hbar}\int_C \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$$

Using Stokes' theorem:
$$\Delta\phi = -\frac{e}{\hbar}\int_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{A}) \cdot d\mathbf{S} = -\frac{e}{\hbar}\int_S \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = -\frac{e\Phi_B}{\hbar}$$

**Answer:** Phase shift $\Delta\phi = -\frac{e\Phi_B}{\hbar}$ depends only on enclosed flux, not on path details

### Example 6: Double-Slit with Solenoid

Electrons pass through a double slit with a solenoid between the slits. What happens to the interference pattern?

**Solution:**

Upper path acquires phase: $\phi_1 = -\frac{e}{\hbar}\int_{\text{upper}} \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$

Lower path acquires phase: $\phi_2 = -\frac{e}{\hbar}\int_{\text{lower}} \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$

Phase difference:
$$\Delta\phi = \phi_1 - \phi_2 = -\frac{e}{\hbar}\oint \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\frac{e\Phi_B}{\hbar}$$

Interference pattern shifts by:
$$\Delta x = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\Delta\phi = -\frac{e\Phi_B}{2\pi\hbar}\lambda = -\frac{\Phi_B}{\Phi_0}d$$

where $\Phi_0 = \frac{h}{e} = 4.14 \times 10^{-15}$ T·m$^2$ is the flux quantum.

**Answer:** Fringe shift proportional to magnetic flux (observable even though $\mathbf{B} = 0$ along paths!)

### Example 7: Flux Quantization

For flux $\Phi_B = n\Phi_0$ where $\Phi_0 = h/e$, what happens to the interference?

**Solution:**

Phase shift:
$$\Delta\phi = -\frac{e\Phi_B}{\hbar} = -\frac{e}{\hbar} \cdot n\frac{h}{e} = -2\pi n$$

For integer $n$: $\Delta\phi = 0 \pmod{2\pi}$

Interference pattern returns to original configuration!

This is why flux is quantized in superconductors: $\Phi_B = n\Phi_0$.

**Answer:** For $\Phi_B = n\Phi_0$, phase shift is multiple of $2\pi$ (no observable shift)

## Topology and Non-Simply Connected Spaces

The Aharonov-Bohm effect is fundamentally topological—it depends on the connectivity of space.

### Example 8: Simply vs. Non-Simply Connected

Why doesn't the Aharonov-Bohm effect occur in simply connected space?

**Solution:**

Simply connected: Every closed loop can be continuously shrunk to a point.

If space is simply connected:
$$\oint \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int_S \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$$

can be evaluated over a surface where $\mathbf{B} = 0$, giving zero.

With solenoid: Space becomes **non-simply connected** (punctured).

Loops encircling solenoid cannot be shrunk to zero without crossing the solenoid (where $\mathbf{B} \neq 0$).

Phase $\oint \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$ is a **topological invariant**—depends only on winding number around solenoid.

**Answer:** Topology of space (non-simply connected) allows gauge-invariant, observable phase

### Example 9: Gauge-Invariance of AB Phase

Show that the Aharonov-Bohm phase $\oint \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$ is gauge-invariant.

**Solution:**

Under gauge transformation: $\mathbf{A} \to \mathbf{A} + \nabla\chi$

Phase integral:
$$\oint (\mathbf{A} + \nabla\chi) \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \oint \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l} + \oint \nabla\chi \cdot d\mathbf{l}$$

For closed loop: $\oint \nabla\chi \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \chi(\text{end}) - \chi(\text{start}) = 0$

Therefore: Phase shift is gauge-invariant!

Key point: Even though $\mathbf{A}$ is gauge-dependent, the integral $\oint \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$ is not (for closed loops).

**Answer:** $\oint \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$ is gauge-invariant for closed paths (topological)

## Berry's Phase: Generalization to Parameter Space

Berry's phase extends Aharonov-Bohm to systems evolving in parameter space.

### Example 10: Adiabatic Evolution

A system evolves adiabatically as parameters $\mathbf{R}(t)$ change slowly. What phase does the state acquire?

**Solution:**

Instantaneous eigenstates: $\hat{H}(\mathbf{R})|n(\mathbf{R})\rangle = E_n(\mathbf{R})|n(\mathbf{R})\rangle$

Adiabatic theorem: System stays in instantaneous eigenstate $|n(\mathbf{R}(t))\rangle$.

Total phase:
$$|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{i\gamma_n(t)}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^t E_n dt}|n(\mathbf{R}(t))\rangle$$

Dynamical phase: $\phi_{\text{dyn}} = -\frac{1}{\hbar}\int E_n dt$

Geometric (Berry) phase: $\gamma_n = i\oint_C \langle n(\mathbf{R})|\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}|n(\mathbf{R})\rangle \cdot d\mathbf{R}$

**Answer:** Berry phase $\gamma_n$ is geometric, depends only on path in parameter space

### Example 11: Spin in Rotating Magnetic Field

A spin-1/2 particle in a magnetic field $\mathbf{B}(\theta, \phi)$ rotated adiabatically. Find Berry's phase.

**Solution:**

Hamiltonian: $\hat{H} = -\boldsymbol{\mu} \cdot \mathbf{B} = -\gamma B(\cos\theta\hat{\sigma}_z + \sin\theta(\cos\phi\hat{\sigma}_x + \sin\phi\hat{\sigma}_y))$

Eigenstate (spin up along $\mathbf{B}$):
$$|+\rangle = \cos(\theta/2)|↑\rangle + e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)|↓\rangle$$

Berry connection:
$$\mathbf{A}_n = i\langle+|\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}|+\rangle$$

For rotation through solid angle $\Omega$:

$$\gamma = \oint \mathbf{A}_n \cdot d\mathbf{R} = -\frac{\Omega}{2}$$

If $\mathbf{B}$ rotates $2\pi$ (full circle): $\Omega = 2\pi(1 - \cos\theta_{\text{max}})$

For complete sphere ($\theta_{\text{max}} = \pi$): $\gamma = -\pi$

**Answer:** Berry phase $= -\Omega/2$ where $\Omega$ is solid angle subtended (geometric!)

### Example 12: Berry Curvature

Define Berry curvature and relate it to Berry phase via Stokes' theorem.

**Solution:**

Berry connection: $\mathbf{A}_n(\mathbf{R}) = i\langle n(\mathbf{R})|\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}|n(\mathbf{R})\rangle$

Berry curvature: $\mathbf{B}_n(\mathbf{R}) = \nabla_{\mathbf{R}} \times \mathbf{A}_n$

Stokes' theorem:
$$\gamma_n = \oint_C \mathbf{A}_n \cdot d\mathbf{R} = \int_S \mathbf{B}_n \cdot d\mathbf{S}$$

Berry phase equals flux of Berry curvature through surface!

Complete analogy with Aharonov-Bohm: $\mathbf{A}_n \leftrightarrow \mathbf{A}$, $\mathbf{B}_n \leftrightarrow \mathbf{B}$

**Answer:** Berry phase is topological (flux of Berry curvature), gauge-invariant

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
AB effect: Observable phase shift from $\mathbf{A}$ where $\mathbf{B} = 0$
Gauge potentials are physically real in QM
Classically, only $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ matter
QM: Potentials enter Schrödinger equation fundamentally
Phase $\Delta\phi = -\frac{e}{\hbar}\oint \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$ observable

**Problem 2: C**
Phase shift: $\Delta\phi = -\frac{e\Phi_B}{\hbar}$ where $\Phi_B = \int \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$
Depends only on total enclosed flux
Independent of path details (topological)
Works even though $\mathbf{B} = 0$ along electron path
Gauge-invariant: $\oint \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \Phi_B$ (Stokes)

**Problem 3: B**
Topological: Depends on global property (winding)
Non-simply connected space (solenoid punctures space)
Phase is winding number times $2\pi e\Phi_B/h$
Cannot be continuously deformed away
Robust against local perturbations

**Problem 4: B**
Spin-1/2 in rotating field: $\gamma = -\Omega/2$
$\Omega$ is solid angle traced by $\mathbf{B}$ direction
Full rotation ($2\pi$): $\gamma = -\pi$ (sign change!)
For full sphere: $\gamma = -2\pi$ (doubly covers)
Purely geometric (independent of speed of rotation)

**Problem 5: B**
Gauge transformation changes potentials: $\mathbf{A} \to \mathbf{A} + \nabla\chi$
But all physical predictions unchanged
Wavefunction transforms: $\psi \to e^{ie\chi/\hbar}\psi$
Observables ($|\psi|^2$, currents) gauge-invariant
Choice of gauge is mathematical freedom
