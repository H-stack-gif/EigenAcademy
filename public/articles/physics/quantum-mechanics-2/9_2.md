# Quantum Mechanics Topic 9.2: Dirac Equation and Spinors

Paul Dirac's 1928 equation stands as one of the most beautiful achievements in theoretical physics. Dissatisfied with the Klein-Gordon equation's negative probabilities, Dirac sought a relativistic wave equation that was *first-order* in time—like the Schrödinger equation, ensuring positive-definite probability density. His stroke of genius was realizing this required the equation to also be first-order in spatial derivatives, which seemed impossible given that $E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4$ is fundamentally quadratic. The resolution: introduce a four-component wave function and four mysterious matrices $\gamma^\mu$ satisfying an anticommutation algebra. These gamma matrices aren't arbitrary—they're the unique solution that makes everything work.

The Dirac equation's successes were immediate and stunning. It naturally incorporates spin-1/2 (with the correct gyromagnetic ratio $g = 2$), predicts the existence of antimatter, and gives exactly the fine structure of hydrogen that appeared only as relativistic corrections in the Schrödinger theory. The four-component **spinor** $\psi$ has profound meaning: two components for spin up/down, and two more for the antiparticle. Dirac initially interpreted negative energy solutions as "holes" in an infinite sea of filled states, but the modern view treats both particles and antiparticles as excitations of a quantum field.

This article develops the Dirac equation from first principles, explores the gamma matrix algebra, constructs free-particle spinor solutions, and examines the physical content encoded in each component. We'll see how Dirac's equation unifies special relativity with quantum mechanics and how its spinor structure connects to the geometry of spacetime itself.

## Constructing the Dirac Equation

We want a relativistic equation that's first-order in both time and space derivatives:

$$i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = \hat{H}\psi$$

where $\hat{H}$ should be linear in $\mathbf{p} = -i\hbar\nabla$.

### Example 1: Requirements for the Hamiltonian

What must the Hamiltonian satisfy to be consistent with $E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4$?

**Solution:**

Try $\hat{H} = c\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{p} + \beta mc^2$ where $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3)$ and $\beta$ are matrices.

Require:
$$\hat{H}^2 = c^2p^2 + m^2c^4$$

$$(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{p})^2 + mc^2(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{p}\beta + \beta\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{p}) + m^2c^4\beta^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4$$

For this to hold for arbitrary $\mathbf{p}$:

1. $(\alpha_i)^2 = 1$ for $i = 1, 2, 3$
2. $\{\alpha_i, \alpha_j\} = 2\delta_{ij}$ (anticommute)
3. $\beta^2 = 1$
4. $\{\alpha_i, \beta\} = 0$ (anticommute)

**Answer:** Requirement: $\alpha_i, \beta$ must satisfy anticommutation relations $\{\alpha_i, \alpha_j\} = 2\delta_{ij}$, $\{\alpha_i, \beta\} = 0$, with all having eigenvalues $\pm 1$.

### Example 2: Minimum Matrix Dimension

What is the minimum dimension for matrices satisfying the Dirac algebra?

**Solution:**

We have 4 anticommuting matrices ($\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3, \beta$), each with eigenvalues $\pm 1$ (so $\text{Tr}(\alpha_i) = \text{Tr}(\beta) = 0$).

From anticommutation: $\alpha_i\beta = -\beta\alpha_i$

Taking trace: $\text{Tr}(\alpha_i\beta) = -\text{Tr}(\beta\alpha_i) = -\text{Tr}(\alpha_i\beta)$

Therefore $\text{Tr}(\alpha_i\beta) = 0$.

For anticommuting Hermitian matrices with $\text{Tr} = 0$, minimum dimension is **4** (cannot be done with 2×2 matrices—only have 3 independent traceless Pauli matrices).

**Answer:** Minimum dimension is $4 \times 4$ matrices; $\psi$ must be a four-component spinor.

### Example 3: Standard Representation (Dirac-Pauli)

Write the Dirac matrices in the standard representation.

**Solution:**

Define:
$$\alpha_i = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \sigma_i \\ \sigma_i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \beta = \begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ 0 & -I \end{pmatrix}$$

where $\sigma_i$ are Pauli matrices and $I$ is $2 \times 2$ identity.

Check anticommutation:
$$\alpha_i\alpha_j = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \sigma_i \\ \sigma_i & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & \sigma_j \\ \sigma_j & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_i\sigma_j & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma_i\sigma_j \end{pmatrix}$$

For $i \neq j$: $\sigma_i\sigma_j = -\sigma_j\sigma_i$, so $\alpha_i\alpha_j + \alpha_j\alpha_i = 0$ ✓

**Answer:** Standard representation: $\alpha_i = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \sigma_i \\ \sigma_i & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $\beta = \begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ 0 & -I \end{pmatrix}$

### Example 4: Gamma Matrix Form

Express the Dirac equation using gamma matrices $\gamma^\mu$.

**Solution:**

Define:
$$\gamma^0 = \beta, \quad \gamma^i = \beta\alpha_i$$

Then:
$$\{\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu\} = 2\eta^{\mu\nu}$$

where $\eta^{\mu\nu} = \text{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)$ is the Minkowski metric.

Dirac equation becomes:
$$(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - mc/\hbar)\psi = 0$$

or using slash notation $\not{\partial} = \gamma^\mu\partial_\mu$:
$$(i\not{\partial} - mc/\hbar)\psi = 0$$

**Answer:** Dirac equation: $(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - mc/\hbar)\psi = 0$ with $\{\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu\} = 2\eta^{\mu\nu}$.

## Free Particle Solutions

### Example 5: Positive Energy Spinors

Find the positive energy solutions for a particle at rest.

**Solution:**

For particle at rest ($\mathbf{p} = 0$), Dirac equation:
$$i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = \beta mc^2\psi$$

Try $\psi = ue^{-imc^2t/\hbar}$ where $u$ is constant spinor:
$$mc^2 u = \beta mc^2 u$$

So $\beta u = u$, meaning $u$ is eigenvector of $\beta$ with eigenvalue $+1$.

In standard representation:
$$\begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ 0 & -I \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \chi \\ \phi \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \chi \\ \phi \end{pmatrix}$$

This gives $\chi = \chi$ and $-\phi = \phi$, so $\phi = 0$.

Two independent solutions (two spin states):
$$u^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad u^{(2)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Answer:** Positive energy at rest: $\psi^{(s)} = u^{(s)}e^{-imc^2t/\hbar}$ with $u^{(1)} = (1,0,0,0)^T$, $u^{(2)} = (0,1,0,0)^T$.

### Example 6: Negative Energy Solutions

Find the negative energy solutions for a particle at rest.

**Solution:**

Try $\psi = ve^{+imc^2t/\hbar}$:
$$-mc^2 v = \beta mc^2 v$$

So $\beta v = -v$ (eigenvalue $-1$).

In standard representation, this gives $\chi = 0$:

$$v^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad v^{(2)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Answer:** Negative energy at rest: $\psi^{(s)} = v^{(s)}e^{+imc^2t/\hbar}$ with $v^{(1)} = (0,0,1,0)^T$, $v^{(2)} = (0,0,0,1)^T$.

### Example 7: General Momentum Solutions

Construct spinor solutions $u(p)$ for a particle with momentum $\mathbf{p}$.

**Solution:**

For plane wave $\psi = u(\mathbf{p})e^{i(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r} - Et)/\hbar}$:

$$(\gamma^0 E - \boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{p}c - mc^2)u = 0$$

or $({\not}{p} - m)u = 0$ where ${\not}{p} = \gamma^\mu p_\mu$.

For positive energy $E = +\sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}$:

$$u(\mathbf{p}) = \sqrt{E + mc^2}\begin{pmatrix} \chi \\ \frac{c\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{p}}{E + mc^2}\chi \end{pmatrix}$$

where $\chi$ is a two-component spinor (spin state).

For $\chi = (1, 0)^T$ (spin up) or $\chi = (0, 1)^T$ (spin down).

**Answer:** Positive energy: $u^{(s)}(\mathbf{p}) = \sqrt{E + mc^2}\begin{pmatrix} \chi^{(s)} \\ \frac{c\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{p}}{E + mc^2}\chi^{(s)} \end{pmatrix}$ where $\chi^{(1)} = (1,0)^T$, $\chi^{(2)} = (0,1)^T$.

### Example 8: Normalization and Orthogonality

Show the normalization condition for Dirac spinors.

**Solution:**

Define adjoint spinor: $\bar{u} = u^\dagger\gamma^0$

For positive energy spinors:
$$\bar{u}^{(r)}u^{(s)} = u^{(r)\dagger}\gamma^0 u^{(s)}$$

Computing:
$$u^\dagger\gamma^0 u = (\chi^\dagger, \frac{c\mathbf{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{E+mc^2}\chi^\dagger)\begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ 0 & -I \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \chi \\ \frac{c\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{p}}{E+mc^2}\chi \end{pmatrix}$$

$$= (E + mc^2)[\chi^\dagger\chi - \frac{c^2(\mathbf{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})^2}{(E+mc^2)^2}\chi^\dagger\chi]$$

Using $(\mathbf{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})^2 = p^2$:
$$= (E + mc^2)\chi^\dagger\chi - \frac{c^2p^2}{E+mc^2}\chi^\dagger\chi = \frac{(E+mc^2)^2 - c^2p^2}{E+mc^2}\chi^\dagger\chi$$

$$= \frac{E^2 + 2Emc^2 + m^2c^4 - c^2p^2}{E+mc^2}\chi^\dagger\chi = \frac{2Emc^2 + 2m^2c^4}{E+mc^2}\chi^\dagger\chi = 2mc^2\chi^\dagger\chi$$

**Answer:** Normalization: $\bar{u}^{(r)}u^{(s)} = 2mc^2\delta^{rs}$ (covariant normalization).

## Physical Interpretation

### Example 9: Probability Density and Current

Derive the probability density and current from the Dirac equation.

**Solution:**

Multiply Dirac by $\psi^\dagger$:
$$\psi^\dagger i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = \psi^\dagger(c\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{p} + \beta mc^2)\psi$$

Take Hermitian conjugate:
$$-i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi^\dagger}{\partial t}\psi = (c\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{p} + \beta mc^2)^\dagger\psi^\dagger\psi$$

Add:
$$i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\psi^\dagger\psi) = -i\hbar c\nabla\cdot(\psi^\dagger\boldsymbol{\alpha}\psi)$$

Continuity equation:
$$\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{j} = 0$$

where $\rho = \psi^\dagger\psi = |\psi|^2 \geq 0$ (positive definite!) and $\mathbf{j} = c\psi^\dagger\boldsymbol{\alpha}\psi$.

**Answer:** Probability density $\rho = \psi^\dagger\psi \geq 0$ (always positive), current $\mathbf{j} = c\psi^\dagger\boldsymbol{\alpha}\psi$.

### Example 10: Spin Operator

Show that spin emerges naturally from the Dirac equation.

**Solution:**

Define spin operator:
$$\hat{\mathbf{S}} = \frac{\hbar}{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$$

where $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{\sigma} & 0 \\ 0 & \boldsymbol{\sigma} \end{pmatrix}$.

Check commutation with Hamiltonian:
$$[\hat{H}, \hat{S}_i] = c[\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{p}, \frac{\hbar}{2}\Sigma_i]$$

This does NOT vanish (spin not conserved separately).

But orbital + spin angular momentum **is** conserved:
$$\hat{\mathbf{J}} = \hat{\mathbf{L}} + \hat{\mathbf{S}}$$

where orbital $\hat{\mathbf{L}} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}$.

For free particle, helicity $\hat{h} = \frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\cdot\mathbf{p}}{2|\mathbf{p}|}$ commutes with $\hat{H}$.

**Answer:** Spin operator $\hat{\mathbf{S}} = \frac{\hbar}{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ emerges naturally; helicity conserved for free particles.

### Example 11: Magnetic Moment

Calculate the magnetic moment of a Dirac particle.

**Solution:**

Couple to EM field via minimal coupling:
$$\mathbf{p} \to \mathbf{p} - e\mathbf{A}$$

In non-relativistic limit, expand Hamiltonian:
$$\hat{H} \approx mc^2 + \frac{(\mathbf{p} - e\mathbf{A})^2}{2m} - \frac{e\hbar}{2m}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{B} + \cdots$$

Magnetic interaction:
$$-\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{B} = -\frac{e\hbar}{2m}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{B}$$

Magnetic moment:
$$\boldsymbol{\mu} = g\frac{e}{2m}\mathbf{S}$$

where gyromagnetic ratio $g = 2$ (exactly!).

**Answer:** Dirac equation predicts $g = 2$ for magnetic moment, confirmed experimentally (QED corrections give $g \approx 2.002$).

### Example 12: Zitterbewegung

Describe the "trembling motion" predicted by the Dirac equation.

**Solution:**

Position operator in Heisenberg picture:
$$\hat{\mathbf{r}}(t) = \hat{\mathbf{r}}(0) + \mathbf{v}_0 t + \frac{c^2\boldsymbol{\alpha}}{2E}(e^{2iEt/\hbar} - 1)$$

where $\mathbf{v}_0$ is average velocity.

The oscillatory term represents rapid trembling (*Zitterbewegung*):
- Frequency: $\omega = 2mc^2/\hbar \approx 10^{21}$ Hz (for electrons)
- Amplitude: $\lambda_C = \hbar/(mc) \approx 10^{-12}$ m (Compton wavelength)

Physical interpretation: Interference between positive and negative energy components.

In QFT: Virtual pair creation/annihilation.

**Answer:** Zitterbewegung: rapid oscillation at frequency $2mc^2/\hbar$ with amplitude $\hbar/(mc)$, from positive/negative energy mixing.

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Clifford algebra: $\{\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu\} = \gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu + \gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu = 2\eta^{\mu\nu}$
ANTICOMMUTATOR (not commutator)
This uniquely defines gamma matrices (up to similarity transformation)
Minkowski metric $\eta^{\mu\nu} = \text{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$
Minimum $4 \times 4$ matrices satisfy this algebra

**Problem 2: D**
Four-component spinor (bispinor)
Two components: particle spin up/down
Two components: antiparticle spin up/down
Cannot be done with fewer components (tried 2×2, fails)
In standard rep: upper 2 = "large" components, lower 2 = "small" components

**Problem 3: B**
Probability density: $\rho = \psi^\dagger\psi \geq 0$ (ALWAYS positive!)
This is the key advantage over Klein-Gordon
Current: $\mathbf{j} = c\psi^\dagger\boldsymbol{\alpha}\psi$
Continuity: $\partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{j} = 0$
First-order in time ensures positive $\rho$

**Problem 4: B**
Dirac predicts $g = 2$ exactly (no free parameters)
Experimental: $g_e = 2.00231930436256$ (QED corrections)
Klein-Gordon gives $g = 1$ (wrong for spin-1/2)
Non-relativistic quantum: $g$ must be inserted by hand
Dirac's greatest triumph: spin emerges naturally with correct $g$

**Problem 5: D**
Modern interpretation: Negative energy solutions → antiparticles
Dirac's original: "holes" in filled negative energy sea
Both particles and antiparticles have positive energy
Time reversal: negative energy backward in time = positive energy forward
QFT: Both are field excitations
