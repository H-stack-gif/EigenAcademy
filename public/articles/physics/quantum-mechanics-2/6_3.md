# Quantum Mechanics Topic 6.3: Selection Rules

Not all quantum transitions are created equal. Some occur rapidly—hydrogen's $2p \to 1s$ has a nanosecond lifetime. Others are "forbidden"—hydrogen's $2s \to 1s$ requires emission of two photons or other mechanisms, with lifetimes of fractions of a second. **Selection rules** determine which transitions are allowed by symmetry. They arise from conservation laws (angular momentum, parity) and the properties of the interaction operator. For electric dipole radiation, the rules are simple: $\Delta l = \pm 1$, $\Delta m = 0, \pm 1$, and parity must change. Transitions violating these rules have vanishing matrix elements $\langle f|\mathbf{d}|i\rangle = 0$ and cannot occur via electric dipole radiation.

Selection rules aren't absolute—they depend on the transition mechanism. Electric dipole is strongest but has strict rules. **Magnetic dipole** transitions ($\Delta l = 0$) and **electric quadrupole** transitions ($\Delta l = 0, \pm 2$) are weaker but have different selection rules. Forbidden electric dipole transitions can proceed via these higher multipoles, albeit $10^5$–$10^{10}$ times slower. The result is **metastable states**—long-lived excited states that are crucial for lasers (population inversion requires metastable upper level) and astrophysics (forbidden lines dominate nebular spectra where densities are low).

This article develops selection rules systematically from symmetry principles. We'll derive the angular momentum and parity selection rules, understand their physical origins, explore magnetic dipole and electric quadrupole transitions, and see how forbidden lines reveal low-density astrophysical environments. Selection rules connect abstract symmetry to observable spectroscopy—they determine which spectral lines exist and their relative intensities.

## Parity Selection Rule

The parity operator $\hat{\Pi}$ reflects spatial coordinates: $\hat{\Pi}\psi(\mathbf{r}) = \psi(-\mathbf{r})$. Eigenstates have definite parity: even ($+1$) or odd ($-1$).

### Example 1: Parity of Hydrogen States

For hydrogen, parity of state $|n,l,m\rangle$ is $(-1)^l$. Show this from spherical harmonic properties.

**Solution:**

Wavefunction: $\psi_{nlm}(\mathbf{r}) = R_{nl}(r)Y_l^m(\theta,\phi)$

Under parity: $\mathbf{r} \to -\mathbf{r}$ means $r \to r$, $\theta \to \pi - \theta$, $\phi \to \phi + \pi$

Radial part: $R_{nl}(r)$ unchanged (depends only on $r$)

Spherical harmonic: $Y_l^m(\pi - \theta, \phi + \pi) = (-1)^l Y_l^m(\theta,\phi)$

Therefore: $\psi(\mathbf{r}) \to (-1)^l\psi(\mathbf{r})$

**Parity eigenvalue:** $\pi = (-1)^l$

- $s$-states ($l=0$): even parity
- $p$-states ($l=1$): odd parity  
- $d$-states ($l=2$): even parity
- etc.

**Answer:** Parity $= (-1)^l$; alternates with orbital angular momentum

### Example 2: Electric Dipole Parity Rule

The electric dipole operator $\mathbf{d} = -e\mathbf{r}$ is odd under parity: $\hat{\Pi}\mathbf{r}\hat{\Pi} = -\mathbf{r}$.

Show that $\langle f|\mathbf{d}|i\rangle = 0$ unless states have opposite parity.

**Solution:**

Let $|i\rangle$ and $|f\rangle$ have parities $\pi_i$ and $\pi_f$.

Matrix element: $\langle f|\mathbf{d}|i\rangle$

Insert parity operators: $\langle f|\hat{\Pi}^\dagger\hat{\Pi}\mathbf{d}\hat{\Pi}^\dagger\hat{\Pi}|i\rangle$

Since $\hat{\Pi}^\dagger = \hat{\Pi}$:

$$= \pi_f \pi_i \langle f|\hat{\Pi}\mathbf{d}\hat{\Pi}|i\rangle = \pi_f \pi_i \langle f|(-\mathbf{d})|i\rangle$$

$$= -\pi_f \pi_i \langle f|\mathbf{d}|i\rangle$$

For this to equal original matrix element:

$$\langle f|\mathbf{d}|i\rangle = -\pi_f \pi_i \langle f|\mathbf{d}|i\rangle$$

This requires: $\pi_f \pi_i = -1$ → opposite parity!

**Electric dipole selection rule:** $\Delta\pi = $ change parity

**Answer:** Parity must change: $\pi_f = -\pi_i$ for non-zero matrix element

### Example 3: Forbidden Transitions by Parity

Which hydrogen transitions are forbidden by parity for electric dipole radiation?

**Solution:**

Parity rule: $(-1)^{l_f} \neq (-1)^{l_i}$ → $l_f - l_i$ must be odd.

**Allowed:** $s \leftrightarrow p$, $p \leftrightarrow d$, $d \leftrightarrow f$, etc.

**Forbidden:** $s \leftrightarrow s$, $s \leftrightarrow d$, $p \leftrightarrow p$, $p \leftrightarrow f$, etc.

**Example forbidden transitions:**
- $2s \to 1s$: both $l=0$ (even parity) → forbidden
- $3d \to 1s$: both even parity → forbidden  
- $3p \to 2p$: both odd parity → forbidden

These can decay via two-photon emission or higher multipoles.

**Answer:** Transitions with $\Delta l = 0, \pm 2, \pm 4, \ldots$ forbidden by parity

## Angular Momentum Selection Rules

Photon carries angular momentum $\hbar$ (spin-1). Conservation of angular momentum constrains transitions.

### Example 4: Deriving $\Delta l = \pm 1$

For electric dipole transition, the operator $\hat{z} = r\cos\theta$ connects states. Using spherical harmonics, show $\Delta l = \pm 1$.

**Solution:**

Express $\cos\theta$ in terms of spherical harmonics:

$$\cos\theta = \sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{3}}Y_1^0(\theta,\phi)$$

Matrix element: $\langle l',m'|Y_1^0|l,m\rangle$

This is a **Clebsch-Gordan** problem: coupling $l$ and $1$ to get $l'$.

Selection rule: $|l - 1| \leq l' \leq l + 1$

Since adding $L=1$ (from $Y_1^0$): $l' = l - 1, l, l + 1$

But parity requires $l' - l$ odd → eliminates $l' = l$

**Result:** $\Delta l = l' - l = \pm 1$ only

**Physical meaning:** Photon carries $L=1$ (spin-1); orbital angular momentum changes by 1.

**Answer:** $\Delta l = \pm 1$ from angular momentum coupling and parity

### Example 5: Magnetic Quantum Number Rule

For radiation polarized along $z$: $\hat{V} \propto \hat{z}$. For circular polarization: $\hat{V} \propto \hat{x} \pm i\hat{y}$.

Derive selection rules for $m$.

**Solution:**

**Linear polarization** ($z$-direction):

Operator $\hat{z} \propto Y_1^0$ has $m=0$ component.

Adding $m=0$ to state: $\Delta m = 0$

**Circular polarization** ($x \pm iy$):

$$\hat{x} \pm i\hat{y} \propto Y_1^{\pm 1}$$

Adding $m = \pm 1$: $\Delta m = \pm 1$

**General rule:** $\Delta m = 0, \pm 1$ depending on polarization

- $\Delta m = 0$: $\pi$-polarization (linear, along field)
- $\Delta m = \pm 1$: $\sigma^{\pm}$-polarization (circular)

**Transition forbidden:** $m = 0 \to m' = 0$ if $\Delta l = 0$ (no way to conserve angular momentum)

**Answer:** $\Delta m = 0, \pm 1$; determines polarization of emitted light

### Example 6: Total Angular Momentum Selection Rules

Including spin, total angular momentum $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{L} + \mathbf{S}$. For electric dipole (acts only on orbital part), what are selection rules for $j$?

**Solution:**

Electric dipole doesn't change spin: $\Delta s = 0$

Changes orbital: $\Delta l = \pm 1$

Total angular momentum: $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{L} + \mathbf{S}$

Adding $\Delta L = 1$ to $\mathbf{J}$: triangle inequality gives

$$|j - 1| \leq j' \leq j + 1$$

**Selection rules:**
- $\Delta j = 0, \pm 1$
- But $j = 0 \not\to j' = 0$ (cannot have both zero)

**Additional for LS coupling:**
- $\Delta l = \pm 1$
- $\Delta s = 0$
- $\Delta m_j = 0, \pm 1$

**Answer:** $\Delta j = 0, \pm 1$ (but not $0 \to 0$); $\Delta s = 0$; $\Delta l = \pm 1$

## Higher Multipoles and Forbidden Transitions

When electric dipole is forbidden, higher multipoles can contribute.

### Example 7: Magnetic Dipole Transitions

Magnetic dipole operator: $\hat{\mathbf{m}} = -\mu_B(\mathbf{L} + 2\mathbf{S})$

Selection rules:
- $\Delta l = 0$
- $\Delta s = 0$ (mostly; weak spin-flip possible)
- $\Delta j = 0, \pm 1$

For hydrogen $2s \to 1s$: both $l=0$. Why is this forbidden even for magnetic dipole?

**Solution:**

Magnetic dipole: $\hat{\mathbf{m}} \propto \mathbf{L} + 2\mathbf{S}$

For $l = 0$ states: $\mathbf{L} = 0$

$$\hat{\mathbf{m}} = -2\mu_B\mathbf{S}$$

Both $2s$ and $1s$ have same $s = 1/2$, same $j = 1/2$.

But magnetic dipole operator has **even parity** (vector formed from $\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{S}$, both axial vectors).

Parity: $2s$ and $1s$ both even → no parity change → magnetic dipole also forbidden!

**Answer:** Magnetic dipole also forbidden by parity for $2s \to 1s$

### Example 8: Electric Quadrupole

Electric quadrupole operator transforms like $r^2Y_2^m$ (rank-2 tensor).

Selection rules:
- $\Delta l = 0, \pm 2$
- $\Delta m = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2$
- Even parity (no parity change)

Transition rate suppressed by factor $(ka)^2$ where $k = \omega/c$, $a$ = atomic size.

For optical transition: $(ka)^2 \sim (a_0/\lambda)^2 \sim 10^{-8}$

For $2s \to 1s$: $\Delta l = 0$, both even parity → electric quadrupole **allowed**!

Rate: $\sim 10^{-8}$ times electric dipole → lifetime $\sim 0.1$ s instead of ns

**Answer:** $\Delta l = 0, \pm 2$; no parity change; suppressed by $(ka)^2 \sim 10^{-8}$

### Example 9: Two-Photon Decay

Hydrogen $2s \to 1s$ predominantly decays via **two-photon emission** with lifetime $\tau \approx 0.12$ s.

Why two photons?

**Solution:**

Single photon electric dipole: forbidden (parity)

Single photon magnetic dipole: forbidden (parity)  

Single photon electric quadrupole: allowed but very slow ($\sim 1$ s)

**Two-photon process:** Second-order perturbation

- Virtual intermediate $2p$ state
- Two photons: each $\Delta l = \pm 1$ → total $\Delta l = 0$ ✓
- Total photon angular momentum: $0$ or $2$ → satisfies $\Delta j = 0, \pm 1$
- Parity: two odd operators → even ✓

Rate: $\Gamma \sim \alpha^5 m c^2/\hbar$ (involves fine structure constant $\alpha$ to fifth power)

Competes with one-photon quadrupole; two-photon slightly faster.

**Answer:** Two-photon decay via virtual $2p$ state; $\tau \approx 0.12$ s

## Metastable States and Astrophysics

### Example 10: Forbidden Lines in Nebulae

In low-density nebulae (density $\sim 10^6$ atoms/cm³), certain "forbidden" lines dominate the spectrum. Lines labeled [O III], [N II] indicate forbidden transitions.

Why are forbidden lines strong in nebulae but absent in laboratory?

**Solution:**

**Laboratory** (high density $\sim 10^{19}$ atoms/cm³):
- Excited atoms collide before radiating
- Collisional de-excitation rate $\Gamma_{coll} \sim 10^{10}$ s$^{-1}$
- Forbidden transition rate $\Gamma_{rad} \sim 10^2$ s$^{-1}$
- $\Gamma_{coll} \gg \Gamma_{rad}$ → atoms de-excite by collision, not radiation

**Nebulae** (low density):
- Collision rate $\Gamma_{coll} \sim 10^{-1}$ s$^{-1}$ (very rare)
- $\Gamma_{rad} \gg \Gamma_{coll}$ → atoms radiate before colliding
- Metastable states accumulate population
- Forbidden lines observable

**Physical meaning:** Forbidden lines reveal low density; density diagnostic.

**Answer:** Low density → radiative decay wins over collisions → forbidden lines visible

### Example 11: Lyman Alpha vs. Balmer Alpha

Compare:
- Lyman $\alpha$: $2p \to 1s$ (UV, $\lambda = 121$ nm)
- Balmer $\alpha$: $3p \to 2s$ (visible, $\lambda = 656$ nm)

Which has shorter lifetime?

**Solution:**

Both are **allowed** electric dipole transitions ($\Delta l = 1$, parity changes).

Spontaneous emission: $A \propto \omega^3|\mathbf{d}_{fi}|^2$

Lyman $\alpha$: Higher frequency ($\omega_{Ly} > \omega_{Bal}$) by factor $\sim 5$

$$\frac{A_{Ly}}{A_{Bal}} \sim \left(\frac{\omega_{Ly}}{\omega_{Bal}}\right)^3 \sim 5^3 \sim 125$$

(Matrix elements comparable for both)

Lyman $\alpha$ lifetime: $\tau_{Ly} \sim 1.6$ ns

Balmer $\alpha$ lifetime: $\tau_{Bal} \sim 100$ ns

**Physical meaning:** Higher frequency → more vacuum modes → faster spontaneous emission

**Answer:** Lyman $\alpha$ has $\sim 100\times$ shorter lifetime ($\omega^3$ scaling)

### Example 12: Selection Rules and Laser Design

A three-level laser uses:
- Ground state $|g\rangle$
- Pump level $|2\rangle$ (short-lived)
- Metastable $|1\rangle$ (long-lived)

Pumping: $|g\rangle \to |2\rangle$ (fast, allowed transition)

Rapid decay: $|2\rangle \to |1\rangle$ (fast, allowed)  

Laser transition: $|1\rangle \to |g\rangle$ (slow if partially forbidden)

Why is metastable state crucial for lasing?

**Solution:**

**Population inversion** required: $N_1 > N_g$

If $|1\rangle$ has short lifetime: decays quickly, can't build up population

**Metastable state:** Long lifetime allows population accumulation

- Pump continuously fills $|1\rangle$ via $|g\rangle \to |2\rangle \to |1\rangle$
- Slow decay from $|1\rangle$ → population builds up
- Inversion achieved: $N_1 > N_g$
- Stimulated emission amplifies photons

**Selection rules:** Help create metastable states (forbidden or weak transitions)

**Example:** Ruby laser uses metastable $^2E$ state (partially forbidden)

**Answer:** Metastable state essential for population inversion; selection rules create it

A) Decays instantly

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Electric dipole operator $\mathbf{d} = -e\mathbf{r}$ is **odd** under parity: $\hat{\Pi}\mathbf{r}\hat{\Pi} = -\mathbf{r}$. Matrix element $\langle f|\mathbf{r}|i\rangle$ requires opposite parity states. If both even or both odd: integral of (even function) × (odd operator) × (even function) = odd integrand → integral vanishes. Parity must change: $(-1)^{l_f} \neq (-1)^{l_i}$. Combined with angular momentum: $\Delta l = \pm 1$ (odd change). Examples: $s \leftrightarrow p$ allowed; $s \leftrightarrow s$ or $s \leftrightarrow d$ forbidden.

**Problem 2: C**
Photon carries angular momentum $L=1$ (spin-1 boson). Adding photon AM to atomic state: $|l-1| \leq l' \leq l+1$ (triangle inequality). Gives $l' = l-1, l, l+1$. But parity operator $\mathbf{r}$ is odd → parity must change → $l'-l$ must be odd. Eliminates $l'=l$. Result: $\Delta l = \pm 1$ only. Physical: combines angular momentum conservation (triangle inequality) with parity selection rule (dipole odd). Both needed—either alone gives weaker constraint.

**Problem 3: B**
Magnetic dipole: $\hat{\mathbf{m}} \propto \mathbf{L} + 2\mathbf{S}$. Acts on angular momentum directly, not position. Selection rules: $\Delta l = 0$ (orbital AM unchanged), $\Delta s = 0$ (mainly), $\Delta j = 0, \pm 1$, $\Delta m_j = 0, \pm 1$. Parity: magnetic dipole operator has **even parity** (axial vector). Transitions between same-parity states. Rate suppressed by $(v/c)^2 \sim 10^{-5}$ vs electric dipole. Example: hyperfine $21$-cm line in hydrogen ($F=1 \to 0$, magnetic dipole).

**Problem 4: B**
Forbidden lines require **low density** environment. High density (lab): collision rate $\Gamma_{coll} \gg \Gamma_{rad}$ (radiative) → metastable states collisionally de-excite before radiating. Low density (nebulae): $\Gamma_{rad} \gg \Gamma_{coll}$ → atoms radiate. Typical nebula: $n \sim 10^6$ cm$^{-3}$, $\Gamma_{coll} \sim 0.1$ s$^{-1}$; forbidden transition: $\Gamma_{rad} \sim 100$ s$^{-1}$. Population accumulates in metastable state → strong forbidden emission lines. Density diagnostic: line ratios measure density.

**Problem 5: C**
Metastable state: long-lived due to **forbidden or weak transitions** to lower states. Not truly stable (eventually decays) but much longer lifetime than typical. Examples: (1) Hydrogen $2s$: forbidden to $1s$ by parity, $\tau \sim 0.1$ s vs typical ns; (2) Oxygen [O III]: forbidden lines, $\tau \sim 10^{-4}$ to $1$ s; (3) Laser upper levels: population inversion requires metastable state to accumulate population. Not ground state (that's stable). Has allowed decay paths but they're suppressed by selection rules or higher multipole.
