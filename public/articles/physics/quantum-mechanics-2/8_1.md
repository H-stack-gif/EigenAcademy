# Quantum Mechanics Topic 8.1: Variational Principle

Most quantum systems lack exact solutions. Beyond hydrogen, harmonic oscillator, and a handful of special cases, we must approximate. Perturbation theory works when the system is "close" to a solvable one, but what if no natural unperturbed Hamiltonian exists? The **variational principle** provides a powerful alternative: it guarantees that any trial wavefunction's energy expectation value is an **upper bound** on the true ground state energy. This remarkable theorem transforms the impossibility of solving Schrödinger's equation exactly into a systematic optimization problem: choose a trial wavefunction with adjustable parameters, compute $\langle\hat{H}\rangle$, then minimize over parameters to get the best approximation.

The variational method's strength lies in its flexibility and reliability. Unlike perturbation theory, it never diverges—the worst outcome is a poor upper bound. The method scales well: simple trial functions give reasonable energies quickly, while sophisticated choices approach exact results. Variational calculations established our understanding of helium (two electrons with correlation), molecules (Born-Oppenheimer plus molecular orbitals), and atoms across the periodic table. Modern **density functional theory** (DFT), the workhorse of quantum chemistry and condensed matter physics, is fundamentally variational. The principle also enables rigorous bounds—proving that certain systems have bound states or establishing energy level orderings.

This article develops the variational principle systematically. We'll prove the fundamental theorem, explore trial wavefunction construction, understand the Rayleigh-Ritz method for finite basis sets, examine linear variational problems and secular equations, and apply the method to helium, molecules, and barrier penetration. The variational principle exemplifies how physical insight combined with mathematical structure yields practical solutions to seemingly intractable problems.

## The Variational Theorem

For any normalized trial wavefunction $|\psi_{trial}\rangle$, the energy expectation value satisfies:

$$E_0 \leq \langle\psi_{trial}|\hat{H}|\psi_{trial}\rangle \equiv E_{trial}$$

where $E_0$ is the true ground state energy. Equality holds only if $|\psi_{trial}\rangle = |\psi_0\rangle$ (exact ground state).

### Example 1: Proving the Variational Theorem

Prove that $\langle\hat{H}\rangle \geq E_0$ for any normalized state.

**Solution:**

Expand trial state in exact eigenstates: $|\psi_{trial}\rangle = \sum_n c_n|\psi_n\rangle$ where $\hat{H}|\psi_n\rangle = E_n|\psi_n\rangle$

Normalization: $\sum_n|c_n|^2 = 1$

Energy expectation:

$$\langle\hat{H}\rangle = \sum_n|c_n|^2E_n$$

Since $E_n \geq E_0$ for all $n$ (ground state lowest):

$$\langle\hat{H}\rangle = \sum_n|c_n|^2E_n \geq \sum_n|c_n|^2E_0 = E_0\sum_n|c_n|^2 = E_0$$

**Equality:** Only when all $c_n = 0$ except $c_0$ → trial state is exact ground state.

**Physical meaning:** Any deviation from true ground state increases energy—ground state minimizes $\langle\hat{H}\rangle$.

**Answer:** $\langle\hat{H}\rangle \geq E_0$ proven; equality only for exact ground state

### Example 2: Harmonic Oscillator Trial Function

For harmonic oscillator $\hat{H} = \frac{\hat{p}^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2\hat{x}^2$, use Gaussian trial function:

$$\psi_{\alpha}(x) = \left(\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}e^{-\alpha x^2/2}$$

with variational parameter $\alpha$. Find optimal $\alpha$ and compare to exact result.

**Solution:**

Compute expectation values:

$$\langle x^2\rangle = \int x^2|\psi_\alpha|^2dx = \frac{1}{2\alpha}$$

$$\langle p^2\rangle = \int\psi_\alpha^*\left(-\hbar^2\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\right)\psi_\alpha dx = \frac{\hbar^2\alpha}{2}$$

Energy:

$$E(\alpha) = \frac{\langle p^2\rangle}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2\langle x^2\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2\alpha}{4m} + \frac{m\omega^2}{4\alpha}$$

Minimize: $\frac{dE}{d\alpha} = 0$

$$\frac{\hbar^2}{4m} - \frac{m\omega^2}{4\alpha^2} = 0$$

$$\alpha^2 = \frac{m^2\omega^2}{\hbar^2} \implies \alpha = \frac{m\omega}{\hbar}$$

Minimum energy:

$$E_{min} = \frac{\hbar^2}{4m} \cdot \frac{m\omega}{\hbar} + \frac{m\omega^2\hbar}{4m\omega} = \frac{\hbar\omega}{4} + \frac{\hbar\omega}{4} = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}$$

**Exact ground state energy:** $E_0 = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}$ ✓

Trial function is **exact** ground state! (Gaussian is natural choice for harmonic oscillator)

**Answer:** Optimal $\alpha = m\omega/\hbar$; $E_{min} = \hbar\omega/2$ (exact)

The harmonic oscillator example shows that variational method can yield exact results when trial function has sufficient flexibility.

### Example 3: Hydrogen Ground State with Simple Trial

For hydrogen ground state, try $\psi(r) = Ae^{-\beta r}$ with variational parameter $\beta$.

Compute upper bound on ground state energy.

**Solution:**

Hamiltonian: $\hat{H} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 - \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}$

Normalization: $\int|\psi|^2 d^3r = 1$ gives $A = \sqrt{\beta^3/\pi}$

Kinetic energy:

$$\langle T\rangle = \int\psi^*\left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\right)\psi d^3r = \frac{\hbar^2\beta^2}{2m}$$

Potential energy:

$$\langle V\rangle = -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int\frac{|\psi|^2}{r}d^3r = -\frac{e^2\beta}{4\pi\epsilon_0}$$

Total energy:

$$E(\beta) = \frac{\hbar^2\beta^2}{2m} - \frac{e^2\beta}{4\pi\epsilon_0}$$

Minimize:

$$\frac{dE}{d\beta} = \frac{\hbar^2\beta}{m} - \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0} = 0$$

$$\beta = \frac{me^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0\hbar^2} = \frac{1}{a_0}$$ (Bohr radius)

Minimum energy:

$$E_{min} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m a_0^2} - \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 a_0} = -\frac{e^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0 a_0} = -13.6\text{ eV}$$

**Exact:** $E_0 = -13.6$ eV ✓

Again exact! Trial function $e^{-r/a_0}$ is the true $1s$ wavefunction.

**Answer:** $\beta = 1/a_0$; $E = -13.6$ eV (exact for hydrogen ground state)

## Trial Wavefunction Construction

Choosing good trial functions requires physical intuition about the system's behavior.

### Example 4: Anharmonic Oscillator

For anharmonic potential $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 + \lambda x^4$, use trial function:

$$\psi_\alpha(x) = \left(\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}e^{-\alpha x^2/2}$$

Estimate ground state energy for small $\lambda$.

**Solution:**

Harmonic part: From Example 2, optimal $\alpha_0 = \sqrt{mk}/\hbar$

With anharmonicity, need to recalculate including $\lambda x^4$ term:

$$\langle x^4\rangle = \int x^4|\psi_\alpha|^2dx = \frac{3}{4\alpha^2}$$

Energy:

$$E(\alpha) = \frac{\hbar^2\alpha}{4m} + \frac{k}{4\alpha} + \lambda\frac{3}{4\alpha^2}$$

Minimize with respect to $\alpha$ (more complex than harmonic case).

For small $\lambda$: Perturbative expansion around $\alpha_0$

$$\alpha \approx \alpha_0\left(1 - \frac{3\lambda}{2k\alpha_0^2} + \mathcal{O}(\lambda^2)\right)$$

Energy correction from anharmonicity:

$$\Delta E \approx \frac{3\lambda}{4\alpha_0^2}$$

**Physical meaning:** Quartic term increases ground state energy (makes potential steeper).

**Answer:** Variational method gives systematic energy corrections for anharmonic terms

### Example 5: Linear Trial Function for Finite Well

For finite square well $V(x) = -V_0$ for $|x| < a$, $V = 0$ otherwise, try linear trial function:

$$\psi(x) = \begin{cases} A(a - |x|) & |x| < a \\ 0 & |x| \geq a \end{cases}$$

How good is this approximation?

**Solution:**

**Normalization:** $\int_{-a}^a A^2(a-|x|)^2dx = 1$ gives $A = \sqrt{15/(4a^3)}$

**Kinetic energy:** Linear function has discontinuous derivative at $x=0$

Actually need to be careful—use symmetry and integrate by parts properly:

$$\langle T\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int|\psi'|^2dx = \frac{5\hbar^2}{4ma^2}$$

**Potential energy:** $\langle V\rangle = -V_0\int_{-a}^a|\psi|^2dx = -V_0$

Total: $E_{trial} = \frac{5\hbar^2}{4ma^2} - V_0$

**Exact ground state:** $E_0 = -V_0 + \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{8ma^2}$ (for appropriate $V_0$)

Comparing: $\frac{5}{4} \approx 1.25$ vs $\frac{\pi^2}{8} \approx 1.23$ (within 2%!)

Simple linear trial function captures essential physics.

**Answer:** Linear trial gives $E \approx \frac{5\hbar^2}{4ma^2} - V_0$; reasonable approximation

The variational principle is forgiving—even crude trial functions often give good energies because errors enter quadratically: $\Delta E \sim (\Delta\psi)^2$.

## Rayleigh-Ritz Method

For complicated systems, expand trial wavefunction in finite basis set:

$$|\psi\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^N c_i|\phi_i\rangle$$

Minimize $E(c_1, \ldots, c_N)$ subject to normalization.

### Example 6: Secular Equation Derivation

Show that minimizing $E = \frac{\langle\psi|\hat{H}|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}$ leads to the **secular equation**:

$$\det(H_{ij} - ES_{ij}) = 0$$

where $H_{ij} = \langle\phi_i|\hat{H}|\phi_j\rangle$ and $S_{ij} = \langle\phi_i|\phi_j\rangle$ (overlap matrix).

**Solution:**

Energy functional:

$$E = \frac{\sum_{ij}c_i^*c_j H_{ij}}{\sum_{ij}c_i^*c_j S_{ij}}$$

Minimize with respect to $c_k^*$:

$$\frac{\partial E}{\partial c_k^*} = 0$$

After calculation (using Lagrange multiplier for normalization):

$$\sum_j(H_{kj} - ES_{kj})c_j = 0$$ for all $k$

This is matrix eigenvalue equation: $\mathbf{H}\mathbf{c} = E\mathbf{S}\mathbf{c}$

Non-trivial solutions exist when:

$$\det(\mathbf{H} - E\mathbf{S}) = 0$$

**Physical meaning:** Eigenvalues $E_1 \leq E_2 \leq \cdots \leq E_N$ approximate lowest $N$ energy levels.

**Answer:** Variational minimization yields generalized eigenvalue problem

### Example 7: Helium Ground State (Simple Basis)

For helium (two electrons), use trial wavefunction:

$$\psi(r_1, r_2) = \phi(r_1)\phi(r_2)$$

where $\phi(r) = \left(\frac{Z_{eff}^3}{\pi a_0^3}\right)^{1/2}e^{-Z_{eff}r/a_0}$

with effective nuclear charge $Z_{eff}$ as variational parameter. Find optimal $Z_{eff}$.

**Solution:**

Hamiltonian:

$$\hat{H} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(\nabla_1^2 + \nabla_2^2) - \frac{2e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{r_1} + \frac{1}{r_2}\right) + \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_{12}}$$

**One-electron terms:** Using hydrogen-like orbitals:

$$\langle T\rangle = 2 \cdot \frac{Z_{eff}^2 \cdot 13.6\text{ eV}}{1}$$

$$\langle V_{nucleus}\rangle = -2 \cdot 2Z_{eff} \cdot 13.6\text{ eV}$$

**Electron-electron repulsion:** Average over positions:

$$\langle V_{ee}\rangle = \frac{5}{8}Z_{eff} \cdot 13.6\text{ eV}$$

Total energy:

$$E(Z_{eff}) = 13.6\text{ eV}(2Z_{eff}^2 - 4Z_{eff} + \frac{5}{8}Z_{eff})$$

$$= 13.6\text{ eV}(2Z_{eff}^2 - \frac{27}{8}Z_{eff})$$

Minimize:

$$\frac{dE}{dZ_{eff}} = 13.6(4Z_{eff} - \frac{27}{8}) = 0$$

$$Z_{eff} = \frac{27}{16} = 1.6875$$

Minimum energy:

$$E_{min} = 13.6 \times 2(1.6875)^2 - 13.6 \times \frac{27}{8}(1.6875) = -77.5\text{ eV}$$

**Experimental:** $E_0 = -79.0$ eV

**Error:** $\sim 2$% (excellent for simple trial function!)

**Physical interpretation:** $Z_{eff} < 2$ because electrons **screen** each other from nucleus.

**Answer:** Optimal $Z_{eff} = 27/16 \approx 1.69$; $E \approx -77.5$ eV (vs -79.0 eV exact)

### Example 8: Improving Helium Calculation

Include correlation by trying:

$$\psi(r_1, r_2, r_{12}) = e^{-Z_{eff}(r_1 + r_2)/a_0}(1 + cr_{12})$$

where $c$ is correlation parameter. Why does this improve accuracy?

**Solution:**

**Without correlation:** Electrons move independently (mean-field approximation).

**With $r_{12}$ dependence:** Electrons avoid each other when close (Coulomb hole).

Physical meaning of $cr_{12}$ term:
- When $r_{12}$ small (electrons close): wavefunction reduced → lower repulsion energy
- When $r_{12}$ large: wavefunction normal

Variational optimization over both $Z_{eff}$ and $c$ gives:

$$E \approx -78.8\text{ eV}$$

(Error reduced to ~0.25%)

**Lesson:** Electron correlation crucial for quantitative accuracy.

**Modern methods:** Configuration interaction, coupled cluster—systematically include correlation.

**Answer:** Correlation term reduces $\psi$ when electrons close; improves energy to -78.8 eV

## Linear Variational Problems

When trial wavefunction is linear combination of basis functions, variational problem reduces to matrix diagonalization.

### Example 9: Particle in Asymmetric Well

For potential $V(x) = \begin{cases} \infty & x < 0 \\ kx & 0 < x < L \\ \infty & x > L \end{cases}$

use two-function basis: $\phi_1 = \sqrt{2/L}\sin(\pi x/L)$, $\phi_2 = \sqrt{2/L}\sin(2\pi x/L)$

Set up secular equation.

**Solution:**

Trial: $\psi = c_1\phi_1 + c_2\phi_2$

Overlap matrix: $S_{ij} = \langle\phi_i|\phi_j\rangle = \delta_{ij}$ (orthonormal)

Hamiltonian matrix elements:

$$H_{ij} = \langle\phi_i|\hat{T} + \hat{V}|\phi_j\rangle$$

Kinetic: $\langle\phi_i|\hat{T}|\phi_i\rangle = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$

$$H_{11} = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}, \quad H_{22} = \frac{4\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$$

Potential: $\langle\phi_i|kx|\phi_j\rangle$ (requires integration)

For $i=j=1$: $H_{11} \to H_{11} + \frac{kL}{2}$

Off-diagonal: $H_{12} = H_{21} = -\frac{8kL}{3\pi^2}$

Secular equation: $\det\begin{pmatrix} H_{11} - E & H_{12} \\ H_{21} & H_{22} - E \end{pmatrix} = 0$

Solving gives two approximate energy levels.

**Answer:** Secular equation yields energies from 2×2 matrix diagonalization

### Example 10: Convergence with Basis Size

For particle in box with perturbation $V(x) = V_0\sin^2(\pi x/L)$, how does accuracy improve as basis size $N$ increases?

**Solution:**

Using $N$ basis functions $\{\phi_n = \sqrt{2/L}\sin(n\pi x/L)\}$ for $n = 1, \ldots, N$:

**$N = 1$:** Ground state energy has ~10% error

**$N = 2$:** Error drops to ~1%

**$N = 5$:** Error ~0.1%

**$N = 10$:** Error ~0.01%

**Convergence:** Errors decrease exponentially with $N$ for smooth perturbations.

**Physical reason:** Complete basis set (all sine functions) spans full Hilbert space; truncation introduces error proportional to neglected components.

**Excited states:** Need larger $N$ for accurate higher levels (must include high-$n$ basis functions).

**Answer:** Error decreases exponentially with basis size for smooth systems

The variational principle guarantees monotonic improvement: $E_N \geq E_{N+1} \geq E_\infty = E_0$ as basis grows.

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
Variational theorem: For any normalized trial state, $\langle\psi_{trial}|\hat{H}|\psi_{trial}\rangle \geq E_0$ (ground state energy). Proof: Expand in exact eigenstates: $\langle\hat{H}\rangle = \sum_n|c_n|^2E_n \geq E_0\sum_n|c_n|^2 = E_0$. Equality only if $|\psi_{trial}\rangle = |\psi_0\rangle$ (exact). **Upper bound** is key: never overestimate binding, always gives conservative estimate. Contrast perturbation (can diverge): variational always converges. Error $\sim (\Delta\psi)^2$ (quadratic)—forgiving of approximate wavefunctions.

**Problem 2: C**
Helium: two electrons, nuclear charge $Z=2$. Independent electrons see $Z_{eff} = 2$. But electron-electron repulsion **screens** nucleus. Variational calculation: $Z_{eff} = 27/16 \approx 1.69$. Physical: each electron partially shields other from nucleus. Not fully screened ($Z_{eff} = 1$, complete): electrons not at same position. Not unscreened ($Z_{eff} = 2$): significant repulsion. Gives energy $-77.5$ eV (vs -79.0 eV exact, 2% error). Simple but effective approximation.

**Problem 3: B**
Variational principle guarantees: $E_N \geq E_{N+1} \geq \cdots \geq E_\infty = E_0$. Larger basis → more flexibility → lower (better) energy bound. Converges from above to exact ground state. Cannot decrease below true $E_0$. Computational cost: $N^3$ for matrix diagonalization. Practical: balance accuracy vs cost. Excited states also improve with larger $N$. Exponential convergence for smooth problems. Complete basis recovers exact result.

**Problem 4: C**
Secular equation: $\det(H_{ij} - ES_{ij}) = 0$ where $H_{ij} = \langle\phi_i|\hat{H}|\phi_j\rangle$, $S_{ij} = \langle\phi_i|\phi_j\rangle$. Standard eigenvalue problem: determinant zero when $E$ equals eigenvalue. Solving gives $N$ approximate energy levels (for $N$-dimensional basis). Lowest eigenvalue is variational estimate of ground state. Eigenvectors give coefficients $c_i$ for optimal linear combination. Matrix formulation enables computational solution for large systems.

**Problem 5: C**
Variational method shines when: (1) No obvious perturbation expansion, (2) Complex interactions (e.g., electron correlation), (3) Need rigorous bounds, not just approximations. Examples: helium (two-body problem), molecules (many-electron), quantum dots. Complementary to perturbation: perturbation needs small parameter and known $H_0$; variational needs good trial function. Modern DFT is variational. Nuclear/atomic structure calculations. If exact solution exists, no need for variational. Perturbation applicable → often simpler than variational.
