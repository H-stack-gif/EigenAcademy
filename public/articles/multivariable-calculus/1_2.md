# Multivariable Calculus Topic 1.2: Dot Product and Cross Product

Multiplying vectors isn't as straightforward as multiplying numbers. There are two fundamentally different ways to multiply vectors, each with distinct geometric meaning and applications. The **dot product** takes two vectors and produces a **scalar**—it measures how much one vector extends in the direction of another. The **cross product** takes two vectors and produces a **new vector**—it finds a direction perpendicular to both original vectors.

These operations are indispensable in physics and engineering. The dot product computes **work** (force dotted with displacement), determines **angles** between vectors, and tests for **orthogonality**. The cross product computes **torque** (position crossed with force), finds **areas** of parallelograms, and determines **normal vectors** to surfaces. Maxwell's equations in electromagnetism are written using cross products. Moment of inertia calculations require cross products. Every time you see a right-hand rule, there's a cross product lurking.

This article develops both products systematically. We'll learn computational formulas, geometric interpretations, and essential properties. We'll master finding angles between vectors, projecting one vector onto another, computing areas and volumes, and verifying perpendicularity. The interplay between algebra (formulas) and geometry (pictures) makes these operations powerful—we can compute numerically while understanding visually. These are the building blocks for all of multivariable calculus.

## The Dot Product: Definition

**Dot product** (or **scalar product** or **inner product**):

**Algebraic definition**:
$$\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = u_1v_1 + u_2v_2 + u_3v_3$$

**Result**: A scalar (number), not a vector

**Geometric definition**:
$$\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta$$
where $\theta$ is angle between vectors.

### Example 1: Computing Dot Products

Find $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}$ where:
(a) $\mathbf{u} = \langle 2, -3, 1 \rangle$, $\mathbf{v} = \langle 4, 0, -2 \rangle$
(b) $\mathbf{u} = \langle 1, 1, 1 \rangle$, $\mathbf{v} = \langle 2, -1, -1 \rangle$

**Solution:**

**(a)** $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = (2)(4) + (-3)(0) + (1)(-2) = 8 + 0 - 2 = 6$

**(b)** $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = (1)(2) + (1)(-1) + (1)(-1) = 2 - 1 - 1 = 0$

**Answer:** (a) 6, (b) 0 (vectors are perpendicular!)

## Properties of Dot Product

1. **Commutative**: $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u}$
2. **Distributive**: $\mathbf{u} \cdot (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{w}$
3. **Scalar multiplication**: $(c\mathbf{u}) \cdot \mathbf{v} = c(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})$
4. **Self-product**: $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v} = |\mathbf{v}|^2$

### Example 2: Using Properties

If $|\mathbf{u}| = 3$, $|\mathbf{v}| = 4$, and angle between them is $60°$, find $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}$.

**Solution:**

$$\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta = (3)(4)\cos(60°) = 12 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 6$$

**Answer:** $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 6$

## Angle Between Vectors

From $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta$:

$$\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|}$$

$$\theta = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|}\right)$$

### Example 3: Finding Angle

Find the angle between $\mathbf{u} = \langle 1, 2, -1 \rangle$ and $\mathbf{v} = \langle 2, 0, 3 \rangle$.

**Solution:**

$$\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = (1)(2) + (2)(0) + (-1)(3) = 2 + 0 - 3 = -1$$

$$|\mathbf{u}| = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 1} = \sqrt{6}$$

$$|\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{4 + 0 + 9} = \sqrt{13}$$

$$\cos\theta = \frac{-1}{\sqrt{6}\sqrt{13}} = \frac{-1}{\sqrt{78}}$$

$$\theta = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{-1}{\sqrt{78}}\right) \approx 96.6°$$

**Answer:** $\theta \approx 96.6°$ (obtuse angle since dot product negative)

## Orthogonal Vectors

**Orthogonal** (perpendicular): $\mathbf{u} \perp \mathbf{v}$ if and only if $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$

### Example 4: Testing Orthogonality

Are $\mathbf{u} = \langle 2, -1, 3 \rangle$ and $\mathbf{v} = \langle 3, 6, 0 \rangle$ orthogonal?

**Solution:**

$$\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = (2)(3) + (-1)(6) + (3)(0) = 6 - 6 + 0 = 0$$

**Answer:** Yes, orthogonal since dot product is zero.

## Projections

**Scalar projection** of $\mathbf{u}$ onto $\mathbf{v}$ (component of $\mathbf{u}$ in direction of $\mathbf{v}$):
$$\text{comp}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|}$$

**Vector projection** of $\mathbf{u}$ onto $\mathbf{v}$:
$$\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|^2}\mathbf{v} = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}}\mathbf{v}$$

### Example 5: Computing Projection

Find the projection of $\mathbf{u} = \langle 3, 1, -2 \rangle$ onto $\mathbf{v} = \langle 1, 2, 2 \rangle$.

**Solution:**

$$\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 3 + 2 - 4 = 1$$

$$\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 1 + 4 + 4 = 9$$

$$\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u} = \frac{1}{9}\langle 1, 2, 2 \rangle = \left\langle \frac{1}{9}, \frac{2}{9}, \frac{2}{9} \right\rangle$$

**Answer:** $\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u} = \left\langle  \frac{1}{9}, \frac{2}{9}, \frac{2}{9} \right\rangle$

## Work as Dot Product

**Work** done by constant force $\mathbf{F}$ over displacement $\mathbf{d}$:
$$W = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d} = |\mathbf{F}||\mathbf{d}|\cos\theta$$

Only force component **parallel** to displacement does work.

### Example 6: Calculating Work

A force $\mathbf{F} = \langle 10, 5, -3 \rangle$ N moves an object from origin to $(4, 2, 1)$. Find work done.

**Solution:**

Displacement: $\mathbf{d} = \langle 4, 2, 1 \rangle$ m

$$W = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d} = (10)(4) + (5)(2) + (-3)(1) = 40 + 10 - 3 = 47 \text{ J}$$

**Answer:** $W = 47$ joules

## The Cross Product: Definition

**Cross product** (or **vector product**): $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$

**Result**: A vector (not a scalar!)

**Determinant formula**:
$$\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ u_1 & u_2 & u_3 \\ v_1 & v_2 & v_3 \end{vmatrix}$$

$$= \mathbf{i}(u_2v_3 - u_3v_2) - \mathbf{j}(u_1v_3 - u_3v_1) + \mathbf{k}(u_1v_2 - u_2v_1)$$

**Component form**:
$$\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \langle u_2v_3 - u_3v_2, u_3v_1 - u_1v_3, u_1v_2 - u_2v_1 \rangle$$

### Example 7: Computing Cross Product

Find $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$ where $\mathbf{u} = \langle 2, -1, 3 \rangle$ and $\mathbf{v} = \langle 1, 4, -2 \rangle$.

**Solution:**

$$\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ 2 & -1 & 3 \\ 1 & 4 & -2 \end{vmatrix}$$

$$= \mathbf{i}[(-1)(-2) - (3)(4)] - \mathbf{j}[(2)(-2) - (3)(1)] + \mathbf{k}[(2)(4) - (-1)(1)]$$

$$= \mathbf{i}(2 - 12) - \mathbf{j}(-4 - 3) + \mathbf{k}(8 + 1)$$

$$= -10\mathbf{i} + 7\mathbf{j} + 9\mathbf{k} = \langle -10, 7, 9 \rangle$$

**Answer:** $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \langle -10, 7, 9 \rangle$

## Properties of Cross Product

1. **Anticommutative**: $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = -(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{u})$
2. **Distributive**: $\mathbf{u} \times (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{w}$
3. **Scalar multiplication**: $(c\mathbf{u}) \times \mathbf{v} = c(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v})$
4. **Zero**: $\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$
5. **Parallel vectors**: $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ iff $\mathbf{u} \parallel \mathbf{v}$

### Example 8: Verifying Anticommutativity

Verify $\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{u} = -(\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v})$ for Example 7.

**Solution:**

$$\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{u} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ 1 & 4 & -2 \\ 2 & -1 & 3 \end{vmatrix}$$

$$= \mathbf{i}[(4)(3) - (-2)(-1)] - \mathbf{j}[(1)(3) - (-2)(2)] + \mathbf{k}[(1)(-1) - (4)(2)]$$

$$= \mathbf{i}(12 - 2) - \mathbf{j}(3 + 4) + \mathbf{k}(-1 - 8)$$

$$= 10\mathbf{i} - 7\mathbf{j} - 9\mathbf{k} = \langle 10, -7, -9 \rangle$$

From Example 7: $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \langle -10, 7, 9 \rangle$

Indeed: $\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{u} = -\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$ ✓

**Answer:** Verified.

## Geometric Interpretation of Cross Product

**Magnitude**: $|\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}| = |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\sin\theta$

**Equals area of parallelogram** spanned by $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$

**Direction**: Perpendicular to both $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ (by right-hand rule)

### Example 9: Area of Parallelogram

Find area of parallelogram with sides $\mathbf{u} = \langle 3, 0, 1 \rangle$ and $\mathbf{v} = \langle 1, 2, 0 \rangle$.

**Solution:**

$$\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ 3 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 0 \end{vmatrix}$$

$$= \mathbf{i}(0 - 2) - \mathbf{j}(0 - 1) + \mathbf{k}(6 - 0) = \langle -2, 1, 6 \rangle$$

$$|\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 36} = \sqrt{41}$$

**Answer:** Area $= \sqrt{41}$ square units

### Example 10: Verifying Perpendicularity

Show that $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$ is perpendicular to both $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ for Example 9.

**Solution:**

$\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \langle -2, 1, 6 \rangle$

**Check** $\mathbf{u} \cdot (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v})$:
$$\langle 3, 0, 1 \rangle \cdot \langle -2, 1, 6 \rangle = -6 + 0 + 6 = 0$$ ✓

**Check** $\mathbf{v} \cdot (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v})$:
$$\langle 1, 2, 0 \rangle \cdot \langle -2, 1, 6 \rangle = -2 + 2 + 0 = 0$$ ✓

**Answer:** Perpendicular to both (as expected).

## Triple Scalar Product

**Triple scalar product**: $\mathbf{u} \cdot (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w})$

**Geometric meaning**: Volume of parallelepiped with edges $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}$

**Determinant form**:
$$\mathbf{u} \cdot (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w}) = \begin{vmatrix} u_1 & u_2 & u_3 \\ v_1 & v_2 & v_3 \\ w_1 & w_2 & w_3 \end{vmatrix}$$

### Example 11: Computing Volume

Find volume of parallelepiped with edges:
$\mathbf{u} = \langle 2, 1, 0 \rangle$, $\mathbf{v} = \langle 1, 0, 1 \rangle$, $\mathbf{w} = \langle 0, 2, 1 \rangle$

**Solution:**

$$V = |\mathbf{u} \cdot (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w})| = \left|\begin{vmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \end{vmatrix}\right|$$

Expand along first row:
$$= |2(0-2) - 1(1-0) + 0| = |-4 - 1| = |-5| = 5$$

**Answer:** Volume = 5 cubic units

## Standard Basis Cross Products

$$\mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{j} = \mathbf{k}$$
$$\mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{k} = \mathbf{i}$$
$$\mathbf{k} \times \mathbf{i} = \mathbf{j}$$

(Cyclic pattern following right-hand rule)

$$\mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{i} = -\mathbf{k}$$
$$\mathbf{k} \times \mathbf{j} = -\mathbf{i}$$
$$\mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{k} = -\mathbf{j}$$

### Example 12: Using Basis Vectors

Compute $(\mathbf{i} + 2\mathbf{j}) \times (3\mathbf{i} - \mathbf{k})$ directly.

**Solution:**

$$= (1\mathbf{i} + 2\mathbf{j} + 0\mathbf{k}) \times (3\mathbf{i} + 0\mathbf{j} - 1\mathbf{k})$$

$$= \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 3 & 0 & -1 \end{vmatrix}$$

$$= \mathbf{i}(-2 - 0) - \mathbf{j}(-1 - 0) + \mathbf{k}(0 - 6)$$

$$= -2\mathbf{i} + \mathbf{j} - 6\mathbf{k}$$

**Answer:** $-2\mathbf{i} + \mathbf{j} - 6\mathbf{k}$ or $\langle -2, 1, -6 \rangle$

### Example 13: Area of Triangle

Find area of triangle with vertices $A(1, 0, 0)$, $B(0, 2, 0)$, $C(0, 0, 3)$.

**Solution:**

Form vectors from $A$:
$$\overrightarrow{AB} = \langle -1, 2, 0 \rangle$$
$$\overrightarrow{AC} = \langle -1, 0, 3 \rangle$$

$$\overrightarrow{AB} \times \overrightarrow{AC} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ -1 & 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 3 \end{vmatrix}$$

$$= \mathbf{i}(6-0) - \mathbf{j}(-3-0) + \mathbf{k}(0+2) = 6\mathbf{i} + 3\mathbf{j} + 2\mathbf{k}$$

$$|\overrightarrow{AB} \times \overrightarrow{AC}| = \sqrt{36 + 9 + 4} = \sqrt{49} = 7$$

**Triangle area** = $ \frac{1}{2}$(parallelogram area) $= \frac{7}{2}$

**Answer:** Area $=  \frac{7}{2}$ square units

## Practice Problems

**Problem 1:** If $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$ and neither vector is zero, then:

A) The vectors are parallel
B) The vectors are orthogonal
C) The angle between them is $45°$
D) One vector is twice the other
E) The cross product is zero

**Problem 2:** The magnitude $|\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}|$ equals:

A) The dot product $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}$
B) The area of triangle with sides $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$
C) The area of parallelogram with sides $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$
D) The volume of parallelepiped
E) $|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|$

**Problem 3:** If $\mathbf{u} = \langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle$ and $\mathbf{v} = \langle 2, 4, 6 \rangle$, then $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$ equals:

A) $\langle 0, 0, 0 \rangle$
B) $\langle 2, 8, 18 \rangle$
C) $\langle 12, 0, 0 \rangle$
D) $\langle -6, 0, 0 \rangle$
E) $\langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle$

**Problem 4:** The angle between $\mathbf{u} = \langle 1, 0, 0 \rangle$ and $\mathbf{v} = \langle 1, 1, 0 \rangle$ is:

A) $30°$
B) $45°$
C) $60°$
D) $90°$
E) $0°$

**Problem 5:** For any vector $\mathbf{v}$, which is always true?

A) $\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{v} = |\mathbf{v}|^2$
B) $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v} = |\mathbf{v}|^2$
C) $\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}$
D) $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$
E) $|\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{v}| = 1$

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
$\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta = 0$
Since neither vector is zero: $\cos\theta = 0$
Therefore $\theta = 90°$ (orthogonal/perpendicular)
Parallel vectors would have $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$, not dot product

**Problem 2: C**
$|\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}| = |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\sin\theta$
This equals area of parallelogram with sides $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$
Triangle area would be half this value
Not to be confused with dot product (which is scalar, not magnitude)

**Problem 3: A**
Notice $\mathbf{v} = 2\mathbf{u}$ (vectors are parallel)
Cross product of parallel vectors is zero vector
Can verify: all components of determinant will be zero
Parallel iff $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$

**Problem 4: B**
$\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = (1)(1) + (0)(1) + (0)(0) = 1$
$|\mathbf{u}| = 1$, $|\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{2}$
$\cos\theta = \frac{1}{1 \cdot \sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$
$\theta = 45°$

**Problem 5: B**
$\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v} = v_1^2 + v_2^2 + v_3^2 = |\mathbf{v}|^2$ always
$\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ always (parallel to itself)
This is property 4 of dot product
Not to be confused with magnitude formula
