# Multivariable Calculus Topic 2.1: Curves and Parametrizations

In single-variable calculus, we studied functions $y = f(x)$—graphs sitting in the plane. The independent variable $x$ controlled the dependent variable $y$. But many curves can't be expressed this way. Consider a circle: solving $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ for $y$ gives $y = \pm\sqrt{1-x^2}$, splitting the circle into two pieces. Or imagine the path of a baseball through the air—both $x$ and $y$ coordinates change over time. We need both as functions of a third variable: **time**.

**Vector-valued functions** solve this problem elegantly. Instead of $y = f(x)$, we write $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t), z(t) \rangle$. As the parameter $t$ varies (often representing time), the tip of the vector $\mathbf{r}(t)$ traces out a curve in space. This parametric representation is incredibly flexible—it handles circles, helices, knots, and any curve you can imagine. Different parametrizations can describe the same geometric curve, like different speedometers recording the same journey.

This article develops the theory of parametric curves systematically. We'll learn to write parametrizations for common curves, understand the role of the parameter, visualize curves in three dimensions, and work with limits and continuity. These skills are foundational—derivatives, arc length, and curvature all build on parametric representations. Every motion in physics, every path in space, every trajectory in engineering is described by a vector-valued function. This is where motion becomes mathematics.

## Vector-Valued Functions: Definition

**Vector-valued function**: $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle f(t), g(t), h(t) \rangle = f(t)\mathbf{i} + g(t)\mathbf{j} + h(t)\mathbf{k}$

**Component functions**: $f(t)$, $g(t)$, $h(t)$ are scalar functions

**Domain**: All $t$ where all component functions are defined

**Graph**: Set of all points $(f(t), g(t), h(t))$ as $t$ varies—traces out a **curve** in space

### Example 1: Evaluating Vector Functions

Let $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t^2, 2t-1, \sqrt{t} \rangle$. Find:
(a) $\mathbf{r}(4)$
(b) The domain of $\mathbf{r}(t)$

**Solution:**

**(a)** $\mathbf{r}(4) = \langle 16, 7, 2 \rangle$

**(b)** $f(t) = t^2$ defined for all $t$
$g(t) = 2t-1$ defined for all $t$
$h(t) = \sqrt{t}$ requires $t \geq 0$

**Domain**: $[0, \infty)$

**Answer:** (a) $\langle 16, 7, 2 \rangle$, (b) $[0, \infty)$

## Parametric Curves in 2D

**Plane curve**: $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t) \rangle$ or equivalently:
$$x = f(t), \quad y = g(t)$$

### Example 2: Circle Parametrization

Parametrize the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 4$.

**Solution:**

Standard parametrization:
$$x = 2\cos t, \quad y = 2\sin t, \quad t \in [0, 2\pi]$$

**Verify**: $x^2 + y^2 = 4\cos^2 t + 4\sin^2 t = 4(\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t) = 4$ ✓

**Vector form**: $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 2\cos t, 2\sin t \rangle$

**Answer:** $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 2\cos t, 2\sin t \rangle$, $t \in [0, 2\pi]$

### Example 3: Ellipse Parametrization

Parametrize the ellipse $ \frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{4} = 1$.

**Solution:**

$$x = 3\cos t, \quad y = 2\sin t, \quad t \in [0, 2\pi]$$

**Verify**: $ \frac{9\cos^2 t}{9} + \frac{4\sin^2 t}{4} = \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$ ✓

**Answer:** $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 3\cos t, 2\sin t \rangle$

### Example 4: Line Segment

Parametrize the line segment from $A(1, 2)$ to $B(4, -1)$.

**Solution:**

**Formula**: $\mathbf{r}(t) = (1-t)\mathbf{r}_A + t\mathbf{r}_B$ for $t \in [0, 1]$

$$\mathbf{r}(t) = (1-t)\langle 1, 2 \rangle + t\langle 4, -1 \rangle$$

$$= \langle 1-t+4t, 2-2t-t \rangle = \langle 1+3t, 2-3t \rangle$$

**Check**: At $t=0$: $\langle 1, 2 \rangle = A$ ✓
At $t=1$: $\langle 4, -1 \rangle = B$ ✓

**Answer:** $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 1+3t, 2-3t \rangle$, $t \in [0, 1]$

## Space Curves in 3D

### Example 5: Helix

Describe the curve $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t, t \rangle$.

**Solution:**

**Projection onto $xy$-plane**: $x = \cos t$, $y = \sin t$ gives circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$

**$z$-component**: Increases linearly with $t$

**Result**: Curve spirals upward around $z$-axis

**Answer:** Circular helix with radius 1, pitch $2\pi$ (rises $2\pi$ per revolution)

### Example 6: Twisted Cubic

Sketch the curve $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t, t^2, t^3 \rangle$ for $t \in [-2, 2]$.

**Solution:**

**Projections**:
- $xy$-plane ($z=0$): parabola $y = x^2$
- $xz$-plane ($y=0$): cubic $z = x^3$
- $yz$-plane ($x=0$): $z = y^{3/2}$ (for $y \geq 0$)

Curve passes through origin at $t=0$, extends in both directions.

**Answer:** Three-dimensional curve called the twisted cubic.

### Example 7: Intersection of Surfaces

Find a parametrization for the curve of intersection of cylinder $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ and plane $z = y$.

**Solution:**

On cylinder: $x = \cos t$, $y = \sin t$

From plane: $z = y = \sin t$

**Parametrization**: $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t, \sin t \rangle$, $t \in [0, 2\pi]$

**Answer:** $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t, \sin t \rangle$

## Multiple Parametrizations

**Key fact**: Same curve can have infinitely many parametrizations.

Different parametrizations represent different "speeds" along the same path.

### Example 8: Reparametrization

The circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ can be parametrized as:
(a) $\mathbf{r}_1(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t \rangle$, $t \in [0, 2\pi]$
(b) $\mathbf{r}_2(t) = \langle \cos 2t, \sin 2t \rangle$, $t \in [0, \pi]$
(c) $\mathbf{r}_3(t) = \langle \cos t, -\sin t \rangle$, $t \in [0, 2\pi]$

Describe the differences.

**Solution:**

**(a)** Traverses circle once counterclockwise, speed $|\mathbf{r}_1'(t)| = 1$

**(b)** Traverses circle once counterclockwise in half the time, speed $|\mathbf{r}_2'(t)| = 2$

**(c)** Traverses circle once **clockwise**, speed $|\mathbf{r}_3'(t)| = 1$

**Answer:** Same geometric path, different speeds and orientations.

### Example 9: Line Parametrization

Parametrize the line through $(1, 2, 3)$ in direction $\langle 2, -1, 4 \rangle$.

**Solution:**

**Standard form**: $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{r}_0 + t\mathbf{v}$

$$\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle + t\langle 2, -1, 4 \rangle = \langle 1+2t, 2-t, 3+4t \rangle$$

**Answer:** $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 1+2t, 2-t, 3+4t \rangle$, $t \in (-\infty, \infty)$

## Limits and Continuity

**Limit**: $ \lim_{t \to a} \mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle \lim_{t \to a} f(t), \lim_{t \to a} g(t), \lim_{t \to a} h(t) \right\rangle$

(computed component-wise)

**Continuity**: $\mathbf{r}(t)$ is continuous at $t = a$ if $ \lim_{t \to a} \mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{r}(a)$

### Example 10: Computing Limits

Find $ \lim_{t \to 0} \mathbf{r}(t)$ where $\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle \frac{\sin t}{t}, e^t, t^2 + 1 \right\rangle$.

**Solution:**

$$\lim_{t \to 0} \frac{\sin t}{t} = 1$$

$$\lim_{t \to 0} e^t = 1$$

$$\lim_{t \to 0} (t^2 + 1) = 1$$

**Answer:** $ \lim_{t \to 0} \mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 1, 1, 1 \rangle$

### Example 11: Continuity

Is $\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle t^2,  \frac{1}{t}, \sqrt{t} \right\rangle$ continuous at $t = 1$?

**Solution:**

Domain: $t > 0$ (from $1/t$ and $\sqrt{t}$)

At $t = 1$: all components continuous
- $f(1) = 1$ and $\lim_{t \to 1} t^2 = 1$ ✓
- $g(1) = 1$ and $\lim_{t \to 1} 1/t = 1$ ✓
- $h(1) = 1$ and $\lim_{t \to 1} \sqrt{t} = 1$ ✓

**Answer:** Yes, continuous at $t = 1$.

## Eliminating the Parameter

Sometimes we can eliminate $t$ to find Cartesian equation.

### Example 12: Parabola

Find Cartesian equation for $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t, t^2 \rangle$.

**Solution:**

$x = t$, $y = t^2$

From first equation: $t = x$

Substitute into second: $y = x^2$

**Answer:** $y = x^2$ (parabola)

### Example 13: When Elimination Fails

Can we eliminate the parameter from $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t, t \rangle$?

**Solution:**

From first two: $x^2 + y^2 = \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$ (cylinder)

Third component: $z = t$

Cannot eliminate $t$ completely—need both $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ AND $z = t$ to describe curve.

**Answer:** No single Cartesian equation; curve is intersection of cylinder $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ with "surface" where $z$ varies freely.

## Practice Problems

**Problem 1:** The parametrization $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 3\cos t, 3\sin t \rangle$ for $t \in [0, 2\pi]$ represents:

A) A line segment
B) A circle of radius 3
C) An ellipse
D) A parabola
E) A spiral

**Problem 2:** The domain of $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \ln t, \sqrt{4-t}, t^2 \rangle$ is:

A) $(0, 4]$
B) $[0, 4]$
C) $(0, \infty)$
D) $(-\infty, 4]$
E) $(0, 4)$

**Problem 3:** The curve $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t, 2t \rangle$ is:

A) A circle
B) An ellipse
C) A helix
D) A line
E) A parabola

**Problem 4:** To parametrize the line segment from $(0, 1)$ to $(3, 4)$, which works?

A) $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t, 1+t \rangle$, $t \in [0, 3]$
B) $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 3t, 1+3t \rangle$, $t \in [0, 1]$
C) $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t, t \rangle$, $t \in [0, 3]$
D) $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 3-t, 4-t \rangle$, $t \in [0, 1]$
E) $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 2t, 3t \rangle$, $t \in [0, 1]$

**Problem 5:** The limit $ \lim_{t \to 1} \langle t^2, 2t, e^t \rangle$ equals:

A) $\langle 1, 2, e \rangle$
B) $\langle 1, 1, 1 \rangle$
C) $\langle 2, 2, e \rangle$
D) $\langle 0, 2, e \rangle$
E) Does not exist

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
$x = 3\cos t$, $y = 3\sin t$
Check: $x^2 + y^2 = 9\cos^2 t + 9\sin^2 t = 9$
Circle of radius 3 centered at origin
Traversed once counterclockwise as $t$ goes from 0 to $2\pi$
Not ellipse (would need different coefficients)

**Problem 2: A**
$f(t) = \ln t$ requires $t > 0$
$g(t) = \sqrt{4-t}$ requires $4-t \geq 0$, so $t \leq 4$
$h(t) = t^2$ defined for all $t$
Intersection: $0 < t \leq 4$, written $(0, 4]$
Note: $(0, 4)$ would exclude $t=4$, but $t=4$ is valid

**Problem 3: C**
$x = \cos t$, $y = \sin t$ gives circle in $xy$-plane
$z = 2t$ increases linearly
Curve spirals upward around $z$-axis
Helix with radius 1, vertical spacing $4\pi$ per revolution
Not a circle (that would require constant $z$)

**Problem 4: B**
Need $\mathbf{r}(0) = \langle 0, 1 \rangle$ and $\mathbf{r}(1) = \langle 3, 4 \rangle$
Check B: $\mathbf{r}(0) = \langle 0, 1 \rangle$ ✓, $\mathbf{r}(1) = \langle 3, 4 \rangle$ ✓
Check A: $\mathbf{r}(3) = \langle 3, 4 \rangle$ but domain is wrong
General formula: $\mathbf{r}(t) = (1-t)\langle 0, 1 \rangle + t\langle 3, 4 \rangle = \langle 3t, 1+3t \rangle$

**Problem 5: A**
Limits computed component-wise:
$\lim_{t \to 1} t^2 = 1$
$\lim_{t \to 1} 2t = 2$
$\lim_{t \to 1} e^t = e$
Result: $\langle 1, 2, e \rangle$
