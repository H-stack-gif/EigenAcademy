# Multivariable Calculus Topic 4.2: Double Integrals in Polar Coordinates

Many regions have natural circular or radial symmetry—circles, disks, sectors, spirals. In rectangular coordinates, these regions have complicated boundaries like $y = \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}$. But in **polar coordinates** $(r, \theta)$, a circle becomes simply $r = R$, and integration becomes much easier. The price we pay is a more complex area element: $dA = r \, dr \, d\theta$ instead of $dx \, dy$. That extra factor of $r$ is the **Jacobian**, compensating for how polar coordinates stretch space.

Converting double integrals to polar coordinates is an art. The key is recognizing when a region or integrand has circular symmetry. Circles, annuli (rings), and sectors integrate beautifully in polar form. Functions like $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$ simplify to $f(r, \theta) = r^2$, and integrals that seemed impossible become routine. Even without perfect symmetry, polar coordinates can dramatically simplify computation.

This article develops double integrals in polar coordinates. We'll derive the area element $r \, dr \, d\theta$, convert rectangular integrals to polar form, integrate over circles and sectors, and apply these techniques to find areas, volumes, and compute famous integrals (like the Gaussian integral). Polar coordinates reveal hidden structure in problems, transforming complexity into simplicity through the right change of perspective.

## Polar Coordinates Review

**Conversion formulas**:
$$x = r\cos\theta, \quad y = r\sin\theta$$
$$r^2 = x^2 + y^2, \quad \theta = \tan^{-1}(y/x)$$

**Range**: $r \geq 0$, $0 \leq \theta < 2\pi$ (or $-\pi < \theta \leq \pi$)

### Example 1: Converting Points

Convert $(x, y) = (1, \sqrt{3})$ to polar coordinates.

**Solution:**

$$r = \sqrt{1^2 + (\sqrt{3})^2} = \sqrt{1 + 3} = 2$$

$$\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{1}\right) = \tan^{-1}(\sqrt{3}) = \frac{\pi}{3}$$

**Answer:** $(r, \theta) = \left(2, \displaystyle \frac{\pi}{3}\right)$

### Example 2: Converting Equations

Convert circle $x^2 + y^2 = 9$ to polar form.

**Solution:**

Since $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$:

$$r^2 = 9$$
$$r = 3$$ (taking positive root)

**Answer:** $r = 3$ (circle of radius 3)

## Area Element in Polar Coordinates

**Key formula**: $dA = r \, dr \, d\theta$

**Derivation**: Small "rectangle" in polar coordinates has:
- Radial width: $dr$
- Arc length: $r \, d\theta$
- Area: $dA \approx (dr)(r \, d\theta) = r \, dr \, d\theta$

### Example 3: Why the Factor of $r$?

Explain geometrically why $dA = r \, dr \, d\theta$ instead of just $dr \, d\theta$.

**Solution:**

At distance $r$ from origin, arc subtending angle $d\theta$ has length $r \, d\theta$ (not just $d\theta$).

**Small sector**:
- Inner radius: $r$
- Outer radius: $r + dr$
- Angle: $d\theta$

Arc length at radius $r$: $r \, d\theta$

**Area**: (radial width) × (arc length) $= dr \cdot r \, d\theta = r \, dr \, d\theta$

**Answer:** The $r$ factor accounts for arc length increasing with distance from origin.

## Double Integrals in Polar Coordinates

**Formula**:
$$\iint_R f(x, y) \, dA = \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} \int_{r_1(\theta)}^{r_2(\theta)} f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta) \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta$$

**Steps**:
1. Convert function: $f(x, y) \to f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$
2. Determine limits: $\alpha \leq \theta \leq \beta$, $r_1(\theta) \leq r \leq r_2(\theta)$
3. Include factor $r$: integrate $f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta) \cdot r$

### Example 4: Disk

Evaluate $\displaystyle \iint_R (x^2 + y^2) \, dA$ where $R$ is disk $x^2 + y^2 \leq 4$.

**Solution:**

**In polar**: $R$ is $0 \leq r \leq 2$, $0 \leq \theta \leq 2\pi$

$x^2 + y^2 = r^2$

$$\iint_R r^2 \, dA = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^2 r^2 \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^2 r^3 \, dr \, d\theta$$

**Inner**:
$$\int_0^2 r^3 \, dr = \left[\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^2 = \frac{16}{4} = 4$$

**Outer**:
$$\int_0^{2\pi} 4 \, d\theta = 4[\theta]_0^{2\pi} = 8\pi$$

**Answer:** $8\pi$

### Example 5: Quarter Disk

Find the volume under $z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ over the quarter disk in first quadrant with radius 3.

**Solution:**

**Region**: $0 \leq r \leq 3$, $0 \leq \theta \leq \pi/2$

$z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = r$

$$V = \int_0^{\pi/2} \int_0^3 r \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta = \int_0^{\pi/2} \int_0^3 r^2 \, dr \, d\theta$$

$$= \int_0^{\pi/2} \left[\frac{r^3}{3}\right]_0^3 d\theta = \int_0^{\pi/2} 9 \, d\theta = 9 \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{9\pi}{2}$$

**Answer:** Volume $= \displaystyle \frac{9\pi}{2}$

## Annular Regions

**Annulus** (ring): Region between two circles

### Example 6: Annulus

Evaluate $\displaystyle \iint_R xy \, dA$ where $R$ is annulus $1 \leq x^2 + y^2 \leq 4$.

**Solution:**

**In polar**: $1 \leq r \leq 2$, $0 \leq \theta \leq 2\pi$

$xy = (r\cos\theta)(r\sin\theta) = r^2\cos\theta\sin\theta$

$$\iint_R r^2\cos\theta\sin\theta \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_1^2 r^3\cos\theta\sin\theta \, dr \, d\theta$$

**Inner**:
$$\int_1^2 r^3 \, dr = \left[\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_1^2 = \frac{16}{4} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{15}{4}$$

**Outer**:
$$\int_0^{2\pi} \frac{15}{4}\cos\theta\sin\theta \, d\theta = \frac{15}{4} \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{\sin 2\theta}{2} \, d\theta$$

$$= \frac{15}{8}\left[-\frac{\cos 2\theta}{2}\right]_0^{2\pi} = \frac{15}{8} \cdot 0 = 0$$

**Answer:** $0$ (by symmetry: integrating over full circle, positive and negative parts cancel)

### Example 7: Polar Integration Bounds

Set up $\displaystyle \iint_R f(x, y) \, dA$ in polar coordinates where $R$ is inside $r = 2\cos\theta$.

**Solution:**

Curve $r = 2\cos\theta$ is a circle.

**Convert**: $r = 2\cos\theta$
$$r^2 = 2r\cos\theta$$
$$x^2 + y^2 = 2x$$
$$(x-1)^2 + y^2 = 1$$

Circle of radius 1 centered at $(1, 0)$.

**Range**: $-\pi/2 \leq \theta \leq \pi/2$, $0 \leq r \leq 2\cos\theta$

$$\iint_R f(x, y) \, dA = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \int_0^{2\cos\theta} f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta) \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta$$

**Answer:** $\displaystyle \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \int_0^{2\cos\theta} f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta) \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta$

## Converting from Rectangular to Polar

### Example 8: Simplifying with Polar

Evaluate $\displaystyle \int_0^2 \int_0^{\sqrt{4-x^2}} (x^2 + y^2)^{3/2} \, dy \, dx$ by converting to polar.

**Solution:**

**Region**: $0 \leq x \leq 2$, $0 \leq y \leq \sqrt{4 - x^2}$

Upper boundary: $y = \sqrt{4 - x^2}$ gives $y^2 = 4 - x^2$, so $x^2 + y^2 = 4$

**Region**: Quarter disk of radius 2 (first quadrant)

**In polar**: $0 \leq r \leq 2$, $0 \leq \theta \leq \pi/2$

$(x^2 + y^2)^{3/2} = (r^2)^{3/2} = r^3$

$$\int_0^{\pi/2} \int_0^2 r^3 \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta = \int_0^{\pi/2} \int_0^2 r^4 \, dr \, d\theta$$

$$= \int_0^{\pi/2} \left[\frac{r^5}{5}\right]_0^2 d\theta = \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{32}{5} \, d\theta = \frac{32}{5} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{16\pi}{5}$$

**Answer:** $\displaystyle \frac{16\pi}{5}$ (much simpler than rectangular!)

### Example 9: Region Between Circles

Find area of region inside $r = 2$ and outside $r = 1$.

**Solution:**

**Region**: $1 \leq r \leq 2$, $0 \leq \theta \leq 2\pi$

$$A = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_1^2 r \, dr \, d\theta$$

$$= \int_0^{2\pi} \left[\frac{r^2}{2}\right]_1^2 d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} \left(\frac{4}{2} - \frac{1}{2}\right) d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{3}{2} \, d\theta$$

$$= \frac{3}{2}[\theta]_0^{2\pi} = 3\pi$$

**Answer:** Area $= 3\pi$ (confirms $\pi(2^2) - \pi(1^2) = 3\pi$)

## Applications

### Example 10: Volume of Paraboloid

Find volume inside paraboloid $z = x^2 + y^2$ and below plane $z = 4$.

**Solution:**

**Region in $xy$-plane**: Where $x^2 + y^2 \leq 4$ (disk of radius 2)

**Volume**:
$$V = \iint_R (4 - (x^2 + y^2)) \, dA$$

**In polar**:
$$V = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^2 (4 - r^2) \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^2 (4r - r^3) \, dr \, d\theta$$

$$= \int_0^{2\pi} \left[2r^2 - \frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^2 d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} (8 - 4) \, d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} 4 \, d\theta = 8\pi$$

**Answer:** Volume $= 8\pi$

### Example 11: Mass with Density

A disk $x^2 + y^2 \leq 1$ has density $\rho(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$. Find total mass.

**Solution:**

$$m = \iint_R \rho(x, y) \, dA = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^1 r^2 \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta$$

$$= \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^1 r^3 \, dr \, d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} \left[\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^1 d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{1}{4} \, d\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$$

**Answer:** Mass $= \displaystyle \frac{\pi}{2}$

### Example 12: Gaussian Integral

Show that $\displaystyle I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2} \, dx = \sqrt{\pi}$ using polar coordinates.

**Solution:**

$$I^2 = \left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2} \, dx\right)\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-y^2} \, dy\right) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-(x^2+y^2)} \, dy \, dx$$

**In polar**: $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$, region is entire plane

$$I^2 = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\infty} e^{-r^2} \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta$$

**Inner** (substitute $u = r^2$, $du = 2r \, dr$):
$$\int_0^{\infty} re^{-r^2} \, dr = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{\infty} e^{-u} \, du = \frac{1}{2}[-e^{-u}]_0^{\infty} = \frac{1}{2}$$

**Outer**:
$$\int_0^{2\pi} \frac{1}{2} \, d\theta = \frac{1}{2}(2\pi) = \pi$$

Therefore $I^2 = \pi$, so $I = \sqrt{\pi}$ ✓

**Answer:** $\displaystyle \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2} \, dx = \sqrt{\pi}$

### Example 13: Area of Cardioid

Find area enclosed by cardioid $r = 1 + \cos\theta$.

**Solution:**

$$A = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{1+\cos\theta} r \, dr \, d\theta$$

$$= \int_0^{2\pi} \left[\frac{r^2}{2}\right]_0^{1+\cos\theta} d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{(1+\cos\theta)^2}{2} \, d\theta$$

$$= \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{2\pi} (1 + 2\cos\theta + \cos^2\theta) \, d\theta$$

Use $\cos^2\theta = \frac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}$:

$$= \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{2\pi} \left(1 + 2\cos\theta + \frac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}\right) d\theta$$

$$= \frac{1}{2}\left[\theta + 2\sin\theta + \frac{\theta}{2} + \frac{\sin 2\theta}{4}\right]_0^{2\pi} = \frac{1}{2}\left(2\pi + 0 + \pi + 0\right) = \frac{3\pi}{2}$$

**Answer:** Area $= \displaystyle \frac{3\pi}{2}$

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Area element in polar: $dA = r \, dr \, d\theta$
The $r$ factor is crucial (Jacobian)
Accounts for arc length $= r \, d\theta$ at distance $r$
Not $dr \, d\theta$ (that would underestimate area far from origin)

**Problem 2: C**
Circle $x^2 + y^2 = 9$ becomes $r^2 = 9$, so $r = 3$
Disk: $0 \leq r \leq 3$ (all points within circle)
Full disk: $0 \leq \theta \leq 2\pi$ (all angles)
Not $0 \leq \theta \leq \pi$ (that's only half the disk)

**Problem 3: B**
$x^2 + y^2 = (r\cos\theta)^2 + (r\sin\theta)^2$
$= r^2\cos^2\theta + r^2\sin^2\theta = r^2(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = r^2$
This simplification makes polar coordinates powerful
Not $r$ (that would be $\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$)

**Problem 4: A**
$\displaystyle \int_0^1 r \, dr = \left[\frac{r^2}{2}\right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{2}$
$\displaystyle \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{1}{2} \, d\theta = \frac{1}{2}(2\pi) = \pi$
This is area of unit disk: $\pi r^2 = \pi(1)^2 = \pi$ ✓

**Problem 5: B**
Polar coordinates natural for circles, disks, sectors, spirals
Boundaries like $r = $ constant are simple
Functions with $x^2 + y^2$ simplify to $r^2$
Rectangular regions better in Cartesian coordinates
