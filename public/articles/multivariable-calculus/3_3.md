# Multivariable Calculus Topic 3.3: Chain Rule and Directional Derivatives

The ordinary derivative measures how a function changes. For multivariable functions, change is more nuanced—you can move in infinitely many directions. The **directional derivative** $D_{\mathbf{u}}f$ measures the rate of change of $f$ in a specific direction $\mathbf{u}$. Walk northeast across hilly terrain, and the elevation changes differently than if you walk due north. Each direction has its own rate of change.

The **gradient vector** $\nabla f$ ties everything together. It points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its magnitude gives the maximum rate of increase. The directional derivative in any direction is simply the dot product $\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}$. This elegant formula connects vector algebra to multivariable calculus. The gradient also has a crucial geometric property: it's **perpendicular to level curves** and level surfaces.

This article develops the chain rule for multivariable functions and explores directional derivatives and gradients. We'll learn to differentiate composite functions using tree diagrams, compute rates of change in arbitrary directions, find directions of maximum increase/decrease, and understand the geometric meaning of the gradient. These tools are essential for optimization, physics (force is negative gradient of potential), and machine learning (gradient descent algorithms minimize cost functions).

## Chain Rule: Single Parameter

If $z = f(x, y)$ and $x = x(t)$, $y = y(t)$, then:

$$\frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}$$

**Interpretation**: Total rate of change of $z$ with respect to $t$

### Example 1: Chain Rule with Parametric Path

Let $z = x^2 + y^2$ where $x = \cos t$, $y = \sin t$. Find $ \frac{dz}{dt}$.

**Solution:**

**Method 1: Chain rule**
$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = 2x, \quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = 2y$$

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = -\sin t, \quad \frac{dy}{dt} = \cos t$$

$$\frac{dz}{dt} = 2x(-\sin t) + 2y(\cos t) = 2\cos t(-\sin t) + 2\sin t(\cos t) = 0$$

**Method 2: Direct substitution**
$$z = \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$$
$$\frac{dz}{dt} = 0$$ ✓

**Answer:** $ \frac{dz}{dt} = 0$ (moving on circle of constant radius, $z = x^2 + y^2$ doesn't change)

### Example 2: Exponential Function

Let $w = e^{x+2y}$ where $x = t^2$, $y = t$. Find $ \frac{dw}{dt}$.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{\partial w}{\partial x} = e^{x+2y}, \quad \frac{\partial w}{\partial y} = 2e^{x+2y}$$

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = 2t, \quad \frac{dy}{dt} = 1$$

$$\frac{dw}{dt} = e^{x+2y}(2t) + 2e^{x+2y}(1) = e^{x+2y}(2t + 2)$$

Substitute $x = t^2$, $y = t$:
$$\frac{dw}{dt} = e^{t^2+2t}(2t + 2) = 2(t+1)e^{t^2+2t}$$

**Answer:** $ \frac{dw}{dt} = 2(t+1)e^{t^2+2t}$

## Chain Rule: Multiple Parameters

If $z = f(x, y)$ and $x = x(s, t)$, $y = y(s, t)$, then:

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial s} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial s} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial s}$$

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}$$

**Tree diagram** helps track dependencies.

### Example 3: Two Parameters

Let $z = x^2y$ where $x = s + t$, $y = s - t$. Find $ \frac{\partial z}{\partial s}$ and $\frac{\partial z}{\partial t}$.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = 2xy, \quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = x^2$$

$$\frac{\partial x}{\partial s} = 1, \quad \frac{\partial y}{\partial s} = 1$$

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial s} = 2xy(1) + x^2(1) = 2xy + x^2$$

$$\frac{\partial x}{\partial t} = 1, \quad \frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = -1$$

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial t} = 2xy(1) + x^2(-1) = 2xy - x^2$$

**Answer:** $ \frac{\partial z}{\partial s} = 2xy + x^2$, $\frac{\partial z}{\partial t} = 2xy - x^2$

### Example 4: Polar Coordinates

Express $ \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta}$ in terms of $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ where $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$.

**Solution:**

$$\frac{\partial x}{\partial r} = \cos\theta, \quad \frac{\partial y}{\partial r} = \sin\theta$$

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial r} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\cos\theta + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\sin\theta$$

$$\frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta} = -r\sin\theta, \quad \frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta} = r\cos\theta$$

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(-r\sin\theta) + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(r\cos\theta)$$

**Answer:** $ \frac{\partial f}{\partial r} = f_x\cos\theta + f_y\sin\theta$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta} = -rf_x\sin\theta + rf_y\cos\theta$

## Implicit Differentiation

If $F(x, y) = 0$ defines $y$ implicitly as function of $x$:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{F_x}{F_y}$$ (provided $F_y \neq 0$)

### Example 5: Implicit Curve

Find $ \frac{dy}{dx}$ for $x^3 + y^3 = 6xy$.

**Solution:**

Let $F(x, y) = x^3 + y^3 - 6xy$

$$F_x = 3x^2 - 6y$$
$$F_y = 3y^2 - 6x$$

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{3x^2 - 6y}{3y^2 - 6x} = -\frac{x^2 - 2y}{y^2 - 2x} = \frac{2y - x^2}{y^2 - 2x}$$

**Answer:** $ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2y - x^2}{y^2 - 2x}$

### Example 6: Implicit Surface

For $x^2 + 2y^2 + 3z^2 = 6$, find $ \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}$.

**Solution:**

Let $F(x, y, z) = x^2 + 2y^2 + 3z^2 - 6$

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = -\frac{F_x}{F_z} = -\frac{2x}{6z} = -\frac{x}{3z}$$

**Answer:** $ \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = -\frac{x}{3z}$

## Directional Derivatives

**Directional derivative** of $f$ at $(x_0, y_0)$ in direction of unit vector $\mathbf{u} = \langle a, b \rangle$:

$$D_{\mathbf{u}}f(x_0, y_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + ha, y_0 + hb) - f(x_0, y_0)}{h}$$

**Formula**: $D_{\mathbf{u}}f = f_x a + f_y b = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}$

### Example 7: Computing Directional Derivative

Find the directional derivative of $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$ at $(1, 2)$ in direction of $\mathbf{v} = \langle 3, 4 \rangle$.

**Solution:**

First, normalize $\mathbf{v}$:
$$\mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|} = \frac{\langle 3, 4 \rangle}{5} = \left\langle \frac{3}{5}, \frac{4}{5} \right\rangle$$

$$f_x = 2x, \quad f_x(1, 2) = 2$$
$$f_y = 2y, \quad f_y(1, 2) = 4$$

$$D_{\mathbf{u}}f(1, 2) = 2 \cdot \frac{3}{5} + 4 \cdot \frac{4}{5} = \frac{6 + 16}{5} = \frac{22}{5}$$

**Answer:** $D_{\mathbf{u}}f(1, 2) =  \frac{22}{5} = 4.4$

## The Gradient Vector

**Gradient** of $f(x, y)$:
$$\nabla f = \left\langle \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \right\rangle = f_x\mathbf{i} + f_y\mathbf{j}$$

For three variables:
$$\nabla f = \langle f_x, f_y, f_z \rangle$$

**Properties**:
1. $D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}$
2. $\nabla f$ points in direction of maximum increase
3. $|\nabla f|$ = maximum rate of increase
4. $\nabla f \perp$ level curves/surfaces

### Example 8: Finding Gradient

Find $\nabla f$ for $f(x, y) = x^2y + e^y$.

**Solution:**

$$f_x = 2xy$$
$$f_y = x^2 + e^y$$

$$\nabla f = \langle 2xy, x^2 + e^y \rangle$$

**Answer:** $\nabla f = \langle 2xy, x^2 + e^y \rangle$

### Example 9: Maximum Rate of Increase

For $f(x, y) = xe^y$ at point $(2, 0)$, find:
(a) Direction of maximum increase
(b) Maximum rate of increase

**Solution:**

$$\nabla f = \langle e^y, xe^y \rangle$$
$$\nabla f(2, 0) = \langle 1, 2 \rangle$$

**(a)** Direction of maximum increase: $\nabla f(2, 0) = \langle 1, 2 \rangle$ (or normalized: $\left\langle  \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}, \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}} \right\rangle$)

**(b)** Maximum rate: $|\nabla f(2, 0)| = \sqrt{1 + 4} = \sqrt{5}$

**Answer:** (a) Direction $\langle 1, 2 \rangle$, (b) Rate $= \sqrt{5}$

### Example 10: Gradient Perpendicular to Level Curves

Show that $\nabla f$ is perpendicular to level curve $f(x, y) = c$.

**Solution:**

Level curve can be parametrized by $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t) \rangle$ where $f(x(t), y(t)) = c$.

Differentiate both sides:
$$\frac{d}{dt}[f(x(t), y(t))] = 0$$

By chain rule:
$$f_x\frac{dx}{dt} + f_y\frac{dy}{dt} = 0$$

$$\langle f_x, f_y \rangle \cdot \left\langle \frac{dx}{dt}, \frac{dy}{dt} \right\rangle = 0$$

$$\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t) = 0$$

Since $\mathbf{r}'(t)$ is tangent to level curve, $\nabla f \perp$ level curve ✓

**Answer:** Gradient perpendicular to tangent of level curve

## Applications of Gradient

### Example 11: Temperature Distribution

Temperature in a room is $T(x, y) = 60 - 2x^2 - y^2$ (degrees). You're at $(1, 2)$. In which direction should you move to warm up fastest?

**Solution:**

$$\nabla T = \langle -4x, -2y \rangle$$
$$\nabla T(1, 2) = \langle -4, -4 \rangle$$

To **warm up** (increase $T$), move in direction of $\nabla T$: $\langle -4, -4 \rangle$ or $\langle -1, -1 \rangle$ (southwest)

To cool down, move opposite: $\langle 1, 1 \rangle$ (northeast)

**Answer:** Move southwest (direction $\langle -1, -1 \rangle$)

### Example 12: Tangent Line to Level Curve

Find equation of tangent line to level curve $x^2 + 2y^2 = 6$ at point $(2, 1)$.

**Solution:**

Let $f(x, y) = x^2 + 2y^2$

$$\nabla f = \langle 2x, 4y \rangle$$
$$\nabla f(2, 1) = \langle 4, 4 \rangle$$

Gradient is **normal** to level curve, so tangent direction is perpendicular: $\langle -4, 4 \rangle$ or $\langle -1, 1 \rangle$

**Tangent line** through $(2, 1)$ with direction $\langle -1, 1 \rangle$:

Point-slope: $ \frac{y - 1}{x - 2} = \frac{1}{-1} = -1$

$$y - 1 = -(x - 2)$$
$$y = -x + 3$$

Or using normal: $4(x - 2) + 4(y - 1) = 0$, which gives $x + y = 3$ ✓

**Answer:** $x + y = 3$

### Example 13: Gradient in Three Variables

For $f(x, y, z) = xyz$, find $\nabla f$ and evaluate at $(1, 2, 3)$.

**Solution:**

$$\nabla f = \langle yz, xz, xy \rangle$$

$$\nabla f(1, 2, 3) = \langle 6, 3, 2 \rangle$$

**Answer:** $\nabla f(1, 2, 3) = \langle 6, 3, 2 \rangle$

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Chain rule: $ \frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}$
$ \frac{dx}{dt} = 2t$, $\frac{dy}{dt} = 1$
Result: $f_x(2t) + f_y(1) = 2tf_x + f_y$

**Problem 2: C**
By definition: $D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}$
$\mathbf{u}$ must be unit vector
Dot product gives component of gradient in direction $\mathbf{u}$
$|\nabla f|$ is maximum directional derivative (when $\mathbf{u} = \nabla f/|\nabla f|$)

**Problem 3: C**
Fundamental property of gradient
$\nabla f$ points "uphill" (steepest ascent)
$-\nabla f$ points downhill (steepest descent)
Magnitude $|\nabla f|$ = rate of maximum increase

**Problem 4: A**
From implicit differentiation formula
$F_x + F_y  \frac{dy}{dx} = 0$
Solve: $ \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{F_x}{F_y}$
Provided $F_y \neq 0$

**Problem 5: B**
Gradient perpendicular to level curves/surfaces
Proven using chain rule: $\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t) = 0$
$\mathbf{r}'(t)$ tangent to level curve
Therefore $\nabla f$ is normal to level curve
