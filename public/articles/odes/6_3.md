# ODE Topic 6.3: Mathematical Modeling

Differential equations don't exist in a vacuum—they model reality. Newton's second law $F = ma$ becomes $my'' = F(t, y, y')$. Population growth follows $ \frac{dP}{dt} = rP$. Chemical reactions obey rate laws $ \frac{dc}{dt} = -kc^n$. Every differential equation in this course originated from observing the natural world and translating phenomena into mathematical language.

**Mathematical modeling** is the art and science of this translation. Given a real-world problem, we make assumptions, identify variables, formulate equations, solve them (analytically or numerically), and interpret results. The model must balance **realism** (capturing essential features) with **tractability** (solvable with available tools). A model of Earth's orbit that includes every grain of space dust is useless if it can't be computed.

This article explores the modeling process systematically. We'll learn to identify rates of change, build differential equations from physical laws, validate models against data, and refine when predictions fail. We'll see models from diverse fields: epidemiology (disease spread), ecology (predator-prey), physics (projectile motion with drag), economics (supply-demand dynamics). The goal isn't memorizing models—it's understanding how to **create** them, the true power of differential equations.

## The Modeling Process

**Steps in mathematical modeling**:

1. **Identify the question**: What do we want to predict or understand?
2. **Make assumptions**: Simplify reality (identify what matters)
3. **Define variables**: Quantities that change (dependent) and control parameters (independent)
4. **Formulate equations**: Translate assumptions into mathematics
5. **Solve**: Analytical or numerical methods
6. **Interpret**: What do solutions mean physically?
7. **Validate**: Compare predictions to data
8. **Refine**: If validation fails, revise assumptions and repeat

### Example 1: Falling Object

Model a ball dropped from height $h_0$.

**Solution:**

**Question**: When does it hit the ground?

**Assumptions**: 
- Ignore air resistance
- Constant gravity $g$
- Point mass

**Variables**: $h(t)$ = height at time $t$

**Equation**: $ \frac{d^2h}{dt^2} = -g$ (Newton's law)

**Initial conditions**: $h(0) = h_0$, $h'(0) = 0$ (dropped, not thrown)

**Solve**: Integrate twice
$$h'(t) = -gt + C_1$$
$$h(0) = 0 \implies C_1 = 0$$

$$h(t) = -\frac{1}{2}gt^2 + C_2$$
$$h(0) = h_0 \implies C_2 = h_0$$

$$h(t) = h_0 - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$$

**Impact time**: $h(t) = 0$ gives $t = \sqrt{2h_0/g}$

**Answer:** $t = \sqrt{2h_0/g}$ (classical free-fall formula)

### Example 2: Adding Air Resistance

Refine Example 1 to include drag proportional to velocity.

**Solution:**

**Assumption**: Drag force $F_d = -bv$ where $b > 0$

**Equation**: $ m\frac{dv}{dt} = -mg - bv$

or: $ \frac{dv}{dt} = -g - \frac{b}{m}v$

Let $k = b/m$:
$$\frac{dv}{dt} + kv = -g$$

**Linear first-order**! Integrating factor $e^{kt}$:

$$v(t) = -\frac{g}{k} + \left(v_0 + \frac{g}{k}\right)e^{-kt}$$

For dropped object ($v_0 = 0$):
$$v(t) = -\frac{g}{k}(1 - e^{-kt})$$

**Terminal velocity**: $v_{\infty} = -g/k$

**Answer:** Velocity approaches terminal speed, doesn't accelerate forever.

## Population Models

### Example 3: Exponential Growth

Bacteria population doubles every hour. Model $P(t)$.

**Solution:**

**Assumption**: Growth rate proportional to population

$$\frac{dP}{dt} = rP$$

**Solution**: $P = P_0e^{rt}$

**Determine $r$**: At $t = 1$ hr, $P(1) = 2P_0$

$$2P_0 = P_0e^r$$
$$e^r = 2$$
$$r = \ln 2 \approx 0.693 \text{ hr}^{-1}$$

**Answer:** $P(t) = P_0 e^{(\ln 2)t} = P_0 \cdot 2^t$

### Example 4: Logistic Growth with Data

Population data: $P(0) = 100$, $P(1) = 180$, growth slows as $P$ increases.

Assume carrying capacity $M = 1000$.

**Solution:**

**Logistic model**:
$$\frac{dP}{dt} = rP\left(1 - \frac{P}{M}\right)$$

**Solution**: $ P(t) = \frac{M}{1 + Ae^{-rt}}$ where $A = (M - P_0)/P_0$

Here: $A = (1000 - 100)/100 = 9$

$$P(t) = \frac{1000}{1 + 9e^{-rt}}$$

Use $P(1) = 180$:
$$180 = \frac{1000}{1 + 9e^{-r}}$$

$$1 + 9e^{-r} = \frac{1000}{180} = 5.556$$

$$9e^{-r} = 4.556$$

$$e^{-r} = 0.506$$

$$r = -\ln(0.506) \approx 0.68$$

**Answer:** $ P(t) = \frac{1000}{1 + 9e^{-0.68t}}$

### Example 5: Harvesting Model

Logistic population with constant harvesting rate $H$.

**Solution:**

**Equation**:
$$\frac{dP}{dt} = rP\left(1 - \frac{P}{M}\right) - H$$

**Equilibria**: Set $dP/dt = 0$
$$rP\left(1 - \frac{P}{M}\right) = H$$

Quadratic in $P$:
$$-\frac{r}{M}P^2 + rP - H = 0$$

**Critical harvesting**: Maximum sustainable $H$ when discriminant $= 0$

$$r^2 - 4\frac{r}{M}H = 0$$
$$H_{\text{max}} = \frac{rM}{4}$$

**Answer:** Harvesting above $rM/4$ drives population to extinction.

## Mixing Problems

### Example 6: Salt Tank with Variable Volume

Tank initially has 100 L pure water. Brine (2 g/L salt) flows in at 5 L/min. Well-mixed solution flows out at 3 L/min.

Find salt amount $A(t)$.

**Solution:**

**Volume**: $V(t) = 100 + (5-3)t = 100 + 2t$ L

**Rate in**: $(2 \text{ g/L})(5 \text{ L/min}) = 10$ g/min

**Rate out**: $ \left(\frac{A}{100+2t} \text{ g/L}\right)(3 \text{ L/min}) = \frac{3A}{100+2t}$ g/min

**Equation**:
$$\frac{dA}{dt} = 10 - \frac{3A}{100+2t}$$

**Linear first-order**:
$$\frac{dA}{dt} + \frac{3}{100+2t}A = 10$$

Integrating factor: $\mu = (100+2t)^{3/2}$

(Since $ \int \frac{3}{100+2t}dt = \frac{3}{2}\ln(100+2t)$, $\mu = e^{(3/2)\ln(100+2t)} = (100+2t)^{3/2}$)

Solution involves integration...

**Answer:** Concentration approaches 2 g/L (input concentration) as $t \to \infty$.

## Epidemiological Models

### Example 7: SIR Model

**Compartments**: Susceptible ($S$), Infected ($I$), Recovered ($R$)

**Assumptions**:
- Total population $N = S + I + R$ constant
- Infection rate proportional to $SI$ (contacts)
- Recovery rate proportional to $I$

**Equations**:
$$\frac{dS}{dt} = -\beta SI$$
$$\frac{dI}{dt} = \beta SI - \gamma I$$
$$\frac{dR}{dt} = \gamma I$$

where $\beta$ = transmission rate, $\gamma$ = recovery rate.

**Basic reproduction number**: $R_0 =  \frac{\beta S_0}{\gamma}$

If $R_0 > 1$: epidemic occurs

If $R_0 < 1$: disease dies out

**Answer:** System of three coupled ODEs (solve numerically).

### Example 8: Threshold Condition

For SIR model, when does infection peak?

**Solution:**

Peak when $dI/dt = 0$:
$$\beta SI - \gamma I = 0$$
$$I(\beta S - \gamma) = 0$$

Either $I = 0$ (no disease) or $S = \gamma/\beta$

**Threshold**: Infection increases when $S > \gamma/\beta$, decreases when $S < \gamma/\beta$

Peak occurs when susceptible population drops to $\gamma/\beta$.

**Answer:** Peak infection when $S = \gamma/\beta = S_0/R_0$.

## Mechanical Systems

### Example 9: Projectile with Quadratic Drag

Object thrown upward with quadratic air resistance.

**Solution:**

**Equations** (vertical motion):
$$m\frac{dv}{dt} = -mg - kv^2 \text{ sign}(v)$$

where sign$(v)$ ensures drag opposes motion.

**Upward** ($v > 0$): $ \frac{dv}{dt} = -g - \frac{k}{m}v^2$

**Downward** ($v < 0$): $ \frac{dv}{dt} = -g + \frac{k}{m}v^2$

**Separable** but different equations for up/down!

**Answer:** Solve separately for ascent and descent phases.

### Example 10: Pendulum

For small angles $\theta$, pendulum equation is:
$$\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} + \frac{g}{L}\theta = 0$$

For large angles (exact):
$$\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} + \frac{g}{L}\sin\theta = 0$$

**Solution:**

**Small angle**: Linear ODE, solution $\theta = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ where $\omega = \sqrt{g/L}$

**Large angle**: Nonlinear, solve numerically. Period increases with amplitude.

**Answer:** Small-angle approximation valid for $\theta < 10°$ approximately.

## Chemical Kinetics

### Example 11: First-Order Reaction

Chemical decomposes: $A \to$ products

Rate proportional to $[A]$:
$$\frac{d[A]}{dt} = -k[A]$$

**Solution**: $[A] = [A]_0 e^{-kt}$

**Half-life**: $t_{1/2} = \ln 2/k$ (independent of initial concentration!)

**Answer:** Exponential decay with constant half-life.

### Example 12: Second-Order Reaction

Two chemicals react: $A + B \to$ products

If $[A]_0 = [B]_0 = a$:
$$\frac{d[A]}{dt} = -k[A]^2$$

**Separable**:
$$\frac{d[A]}{[A]^2} = -k \, dt$$

$$-\frac{1}{[A]} = -kt + C$$

$$\frac{1}{[A]} = kt + \frac{1}{a}$$

$$[A] = \frac{a}{1 + akt}$$

**Half-life**: $t_{1/2} = 1/(ka)$ (depends on initial concentration!)

**Answer:** Hyperbolic decay, not exponential.

## Economics and Finance

### Example 13: Supply-Demand Dynamics

Price $P(t)$ adjusts based on supply-demand gap.

**Model**:
$$\frac{dP}{dt} = k(D(P) - S(P))$$

where $D(P)$ = demand at price $P$, $S(P)$ = supply at price $P$

**Linear case**: $D = a - bP$, $S = c + dP$

$$\frac{dP}{dt} = k[(a-bP) - (c+dP)] = k[a - c - (b+d)P]$$

**Equilibrium**: $P^* = (a-c)/(b+d)$

**Solution**: $P(t) = P^* + (P_0 - P^*)e^{-k(b+d)t}$

Price exponentially approaches equilibrium.

**Answer:** Stable equilibrium if $b, d > 0$ (demand decreases, supply increases with price).

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Exponential model: $dP/dt = rP$
Growth rate ($dP/dt$) proportional to population ($P$)
Constant $r$ is proportionality constant (per capita growth rate)
Different from constant growth rate: $dP/dt = r$ (linear growth)

**Problem 2: C**
Logistic: $ \frac{dP}{dt} = rP\left(1 - \frac{P}{M}\right)$
Factor $(1 - P/M)$ includes carrying capacity $M$
Growth slows as $P \to M$
Reduces to exponential when $P << M$
Models resource limitation

**Problem 3: C**
Concentration = amount per unit volume
Units: (grams)/(liters) or similar
Amount $A(t)$ changes with in/out flows
Volume $V(t)$ may change if flows unequal
Concentration: $c(t) = A(t)/V(t)$

**Problem 4: C**
$S + I + R = N$ (constant)
No births, deaths, or migration
Only transfers between compartments
Simplifying assumption for short time scales
More complex models allow varying $N$

**Problem 5: E**
Most realistic models are nonlinear
Analytical solutions rare
Linearization gives local behavior near equilibria
Numerical methods (Euler, RK4) handle any ODE
Essential for practical modeling
