# ODE Topic 1.1: Introduction and Direction Fields

Imagine you're watching a cup of hot coffee cool down. The rate of cooling depends on the temperature difference between the coffee and the room. If we call the temperature $T$ and time $t$, we can write: "the rate of change of temperature equals some constant times the temperature difference." In symbols: $ \frac{dT}{dt} = -k(T - T_{\text{room}})$. This is a **differential equation**—an equation involving a function and its derivatives.

Differential equations are the language of change. Population growth, radioactive decay, planetary motion, chemical reactions, electric circuits, fluid flow—all described by differential equations. Newton's second law $F = ma$ is really $ F = m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}$, a differential equation. Rather than knowing the position directly, we know how position changes, and we must work backwards to find the position function itself.

This article introduces the fundamental concepts: what differential equations are, what it means to solve them, and how we verify solutions. We'll focus on **first-order** equations (involving only first derivatives) and learn to analyze them geometrically using **direction fields**. Even when we can't solve an equation analytically, direction fields reveal the qualitative behavior of solutions—whether they increase or decrease, where they stabilize, and how they depend on initial conditions.

## What is a Differential Equation?

A **differential equation** relates a function to its derivatives. An **ordinary differential equation (ODE)** involves a function of one variable.

**First-order ODE**: $ \frac{dy}{dx} = f(x, y)$ or equivalently $y' = f(x, y)$

The **order** is the highest derivative that appears. First-order means we see $y'$ but not $y''$ or higher derivatives.

### Example 1: Identifying Differential Equations

Which are first-order ODEs?

(a) $ \frac{dy}{dx} = x^2 + y$

(b) $ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + 3\frac{dy}{dx} - y = 0$

(c) $y = x^2 + 3x - 1$

(d) $ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y}{x}$

**Solution:**

(a) First-order ODE ✓ (involves $y'$ and no higher derivatives)

(b) Second-order ODE (involves $y''$)

(c) Not a differential equation (no derivatives)

(d) First-order ODE ✓

**Answer:** (a) and (d) are first-order ODEs.

### Example 2: Writing Differential Equations

Express "the rate of change of population is proportional to the current population" as a differential equation.

**Solution:**

Let $P(t)$ = population at time $t$.

"Rate of change" = $ \frac{dP}{dt}$

"Proportional to current population" = $kP$ for some constant $k$

**Answer:** $ \frac{dP}{dt} = kP$

## Solutions and Initial Value Problems

A **solution** to $y' = f(x, y)$ is a function $y(x)$ that satisfies the equation on some interval.

**General solution**: Contains arbitrary constant(s), represents all solutions.

**Particular solution**: Specific function obtained by choosing constant value(s).

An **initial value problem (IVP)** specifies $y(x_0) = y_0$ and seeks the particular solution satisfying this condition.

### Example 3: Verifying Solutions

Verify that $y = Ce^{2x}$ solves $y' = 2y$.

**Solution:**

Given $y = Ce^{2x}$, find $y'$:

$$ y' = C \cdot 2e^{2x} = 2(Ce^{2x}) = 2y$$ ✓

**Answer:** Yes, $y = Ce^{2x}$ satisfies $y' = 2y$ for any constant $C$.

### Example 4: Finding Particular Solutions

Given that $y = Ce^{2x}$ is the general solution to $y' = 2y$, find the particular solution with $y(0) = 3$.

**Solution:**

Use initial condition: $y(0) = 3$

$$ Ce^{2(0)} = Ce^0 = C = 3$$

**Answer:** $y = 3e^{2x}$

### Example 5: Existence and Uniqueness

For IVP $y' = 2y$, $y(0) = 3$, how many solutions exist?

**Solution:**

The **Existence and Uniqueness Theorem** states: If $f(x, y)$ and $ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ are continuous near $(x_0, y_0)$, then the IVP $y' = f(x, y)$, $y(x_0) = y_0$ has exactly one solution in some interval around $x_0$.

Here $f(x, y) = 2y$ is continuous everywhere, as is $ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 2$.

**Answer:** Exactly one solution exists (which we found: $y = 3e^{2x}$).

## Direction Fields

A **direction field** (or **slope field**) is a visual representation of a differential equation $y' = f(x, y)$.

At each point $(x, y)$, we draw a small line segment with slope $f(x, y)$. This shows the "direction" solutions move through that point.

**Key insight**: Solution curves are tangent to these line segments at every point.

### Example 6: Computing Slopes for Direction Field

For $y' = x + y$, find slopes at points $(0, 0)$, $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$, $(1, 1)$.

**Solution:**

At $(0, 0)$: slope $= 0 + 0 = 0$ (horizontal segment)

At $(1, 0)$: slope $= 1 + 0 = 1$ (diagonal, rising)

At $(0, 1)$: slope $= 0 + 1 = 1$ (diagonal, rising)

At $(1, 1)$: slope $= 1 + 1 = 2$ (steep, rising)

**Answer:** Slopes are 0, 1, 1, 2 respectively.

### Example 7: Isoclines

For $y' = x + y$, where is the slope equal to 1?

**Solution:**

Set $f(x, y) = 1$:

$$ x + y = 1$$

This is the line $y = 1 - x$.

Along this **isocline** (curve of constant slope), all direction field segments have slope 1.

**Answer:** Along line $y = 1 - x$, all segments have slope 1.

### Example 8: Equilibrium Solutions

For $y' = y(1 - y)$, where are the equilibrium solutions?

**Solution:**

**Equilibrium solutions** are constant functions where $y' = 0$.

Set $y(1 - y) = 0$:

$$ y = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad y = 1$$

These are horizontal lines where direction field segments are horizontal.

Verify: If $y = 0$, then $y' = 0(1-0) = 0$ ✓

If $y = 1$, then $y' = 1(1-1) = 0$ ✓

**Answer:** Equilibrium solutions are $y = 0$ and $y = 1$.

## Analyzing Direction Fields

Direction fields reveal:
- **Increasing/decreasing regions**: Where $f(x, y) > 0$ (slopes positive) or $f(x, y) < 0$ (slopes negative)
- **Equilibrium solutions**: Horizontal lines where $f(x, y) = 0$
- **Stability**: Whether solutions approach or move away from equilibria
- **Long-term behavior**: What happens as $x \to \infty$

### Example 9: Stability Analysis

For $y' = y(1 - y)$, analyze stability of equilibria $y = 0$ and $y = 1$.

**Solution:**

Test points between and outside equilibria:

If $y = 0.5$ (between equilibria): $y' = 0.5(1 - 0.5) = 0.25 > 0$ → solution increases

If $y = 1.5$ (above $y = 1$): $y' = 1.5(1 - 1.5) = -0.75 < 0$ → solution decreases

If $y = -0.5$ (below $y = 0$): $y' = -0.5(1 - (-0.5)) = -0.75 < 0$ → solution decreases

**Behavior**:
- Solutions between 0 and 1 move toward $y = 1$
- Solutions above 1 move down toward $y = 1$
- Solutions below 0 move down away from $y = 0$

**Answer:** $y = 1$ is **stable** (solutions approach it). $y = 0$ is **unstable** (solutions move away).

### Example 10: Interpreting Solution Curves

Given direction field for $y' = -y$, if $y(0) = 2$, what is $ \lim_{x \to \infty} y(x)$?

**Solution:**

For $y' = -y$:
- When $y > 0$: $y' < 0$ (decreasing)
- When $y < 0$: $y' > 0$ (increasing)
- When $y = 0$: $y' = 0$ (equilibrium)

Starting at $y(0) = 2 > 0$, solution decreases toward equilibrium $y = 0$.

**Answer:** $ \lim_{x \to \infty} y(x) = 0$ (approaches stable equilibrium).

## Autonomous Equations

An **autonomous** equation has form $y' = f(y)$ (right side doesn't depend explicitly on $x$).

**Properties**:
- Direction field is same along any horizontal line
- Vertical shifts of solutions are also solutions
- Equilibria found by solving $f(y) = 0$

### Example 11: Autonomous vs Non-Autonomous

Which are autonomous?

(a) $y' = y^2 - 4$

(b) $y' = xy$

(c) $y' = \sin y$

**Solution:**

(a) Autonomous ✓ (right side is $f(y) = y^2 - 4$, no $x$)

(b) Not autonomous (right side is $xy$, depends on $x$)

(c) Autonomous ✓ (right side is $f(y) = \sin y$, no $x$)

**Answer:** (a) and (c) are autonomous.

### Example 12: Phase Line Analysis

For $y' = y^2 - 4$, create phase line diagram.

**Solution:**


- $y > 2$ (say $y = 3$): $y' = 9 - 4 = 5 > 0$ → increasing away from 2 ↑
- $-2 < y < 2$ (say $y = 0$): $y' = 0 - 4 = -4 < 0$ → decreasing ↓
- $y < -2$ (say $y = -3$): $y' = 9 - 4 = 5 > 0$ → increasing toward -2 ↑

**Answer:** $y = 2$ unstable (solutions move away). $y = -2$ is semi-stable (solutions from below approach, from above move away).

### Example 13: Drawing Solution Curves

For $y' = 1 - y$, sketch several solution curves using direction field reasoning.

**Solution:**

Equilibrium: $1 - y = 0$ gives $y = 1$

Behavior:
- If $y > 1$: $y' = 1 - y < 0$ → decreasing toward 1
- If $y < 1$: $y' = 1 - y > 0$ → increasing toward 1

All solutions approach $y = 1$ as $x \to \infty$.

Direction field has:
- Positive slopes below $y = 1$
- Negative slopes above $y = 1$
- Horizontal slopes at $y = 1$

**Answer:** All solution curves are increasing if they start below $y = 1$, decreasing if above, and all approach the horizontal line $y = 1$ asymptotically.

## Practice Problems

**Problem 1:** Which is a first-order ODE?

A) $y = 3x^2 + 1$
B) $ \frac{dy}{dx} = 3x^2 + 1$
C) $ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = 3x^2 + 1$
D) $ \int y \, dx = 3x^2 + 1$
E) $y' + y'' = x$

**Problem 2:** For direction field of $y' = -x/y$, the slope at point $(2, 1)$ is:

A) -2
B) -1/2
C) 0
D) 1/2
E) 2

**Problem 3:** Equilibrium solutions of $y' = y(4 - y)$ are:

A) $y = 0$ only
B) $y = 4$ only
C) $y = 0$ and $y = 4$
D) $y = 2$
E) No equilibrium solutions

**Problem 4:** An autonomous differential equation:

A) Has constant coefficients
B) Has right side independent of $x$
C) Has linear right side
D) Always has closed-form solution
E) Has no equilibrium points

**Problem 5:** For $y' = 2 - y$, the equilibrium $y = 2$ is:

A) Unstable
B) Stable
C) Semi-stable
D) Not an equilibrium
E) Cannot determine

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
$ \frac{dy}{dx} = 3x^2 + 1$ involves first derivative (and no higher derivatives).
(A) is not a differential equation—no derivatives.
(C) is second-order (has $y''$).
(D) is integral equation, not ODE.
(E) has both $y'$ and $y''$, making it second-order.

**Problem 2: A**
$ y' = -\frac{x}{y}$
At $(x, y) = (2, 1)$: slope $ = -\frac{2}{1} = -2$
Direction field segment at this point slopes downward steeply.

**Problem 3: C**
Set $y(4 - y) = 0$:
$y = 0$ or $4 - y = 0$
$y = 0$ or $y = 4$
Both are equilibrium solutions (horizontal lines where $y' = 0$).

**Problem 4: B**
Autonomous equation: $y' = f(y)$ where right side depends only on $y$, not on independent variable $x$.
Example: $y' = y^2 - 1$ is autonomous.
Non-example: $y' = xy$ is not autonomous (has $x$).

**Problem 5: B**
For $y' = 2 - y$:
If $y < 2$: $y' = 2 - y > 0$ → increasing toward 2
If $y > 2$: $y' = 2 - y < 0$ → decreasing toward 2
Solutions from both sides approach $y = 2$.
This is stable equilibrium (attracting).
