# ODE Topic 1.3: Linear First-Order Equations

Not all first-order equations are separable. Consider $ \frac{dy}{dx} + xy = x$. We can't separate variables here—the $y$ and $x$ are mixed together in the left side. But this equation has special structure: it's **linear in $y$**. The terms involving $y$ and $y'$ appear to the first power, and there's no product $y \cdot y'$ or $y^2$. This linearity opens the door to a powerful technique: the **integrating factor method**.

The idea is brilliant: multiply the entire equation by a carefully chosen function $\mu(x)$ that makes the left side become an exact derivative. Then we can integrate both sides and solve for $y$. The integrating factor always exists for linear equations and is constructed systematically from the coefficient of $y$.

Linear first-order equations arise throughout science and engineering: RC circuits (voltage across capacitor), mixing problems with varying volumes, population models with immigration, economics (capital accumulation with investment), and more. The integrating factor method is a workhorse technique that every student of differential equations must master.

## Standard Form

A **linear first-order ODE** has form:
$$ \frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)y = Q(x)$$

This is **standard form**. The equation is linear in $y$ and $y'$ (no $y^2$, $y'y$, $\sin y$, etc.).

**Note**: Coefficient of $y'$ must be 1. If not, divide through.

### Example 1: Identifying Linear Equations

Which are linear first-order?

(a) $y' + 3y = x^2$

(b) $yy' + x = 0$

(c) $2y' - xy = \cos x$

(d) $y' = y^2 + x$

**Solution:**

(a) Linear ✓ (form $y' + P(x)y = Q(x)$ with $P = 3$, $Q = x^2$)

(b) Not linear (has $yy'$, product of $y$ and $y'$)

(c) Linear ✓ (divide by 2: $y' - \frac{x}{2}y = \frac{\cos x}{2}$)

(d) Not linear (has $y^2$, nonlinear in $y$)

**Answer:** (a) and (c) are linear.

### Example 2: Converting to Standard Form

Write $3xy' + 2y = x^2$ in standard form.

**Solution:**

Divide by $3x$ (coefficient of $y'$):
$$ y' + \frac{2}{3x}y = \frac{x}{3}$$

**Answer:** $ y' + \frac{2}{3x}y = \frac{x}{3}$ with $P(x) = \frac{2}{3x}$, $Q(x) = \frac{x}{3}$

## Integrating Factor Method

For $y' + P(x)y = Q(x)$:

**Step 1**: Compute integrating factor $ \mu(x) = e^{\int P(x) \, dx}$

**Step 2**: Multiply equation by $\mu(x)$:
$$ \mu(x)y' + \mu(x)P(x)y = \mu(x)Q(x)$$

**Step 3**: Recognize left side as derivative:
$$ \frac{d}{dx}[\mu(x)y] = \mu(x)Q(x)$$

**Step 4**: Integrate both sides:
$$ \mu(x)y = \int \mu(x)Q(x) \, dx$$

**Step 5**: Solve for $y$:
$$ y = \frac{1}{\mu(x)} \int \mu(x)Q(x) \, dx$$

### Example 3: Basic Integrating Factor

Solve $y' + 2y = 4$.

**Solution:**

Standard form: $y' + 2y = 4$ with $P(x) = 2$, $Q(x) = 4$

**Integrating factor**: $ \mu = e^{\int 2 \, dx} = e^{2x}$

**Multiply**: $e^{2x}y' + 2e^{2x}y = 4e^{2x}$

**Recognize**: $ \frac{d}{dx}[e^{2x}y] = 4e^{2x}$

**Integrate**: $ e^{2x}y = \int 4e^{2x} \, dx = 2e^{2x} + C$

**Solve for $y$**: $ y = \frac{2e^{2x} + C}{e^{2x}} = 2 + Ce^{-2x}$

**Answer:** $y = 2 + Ce^{-2x}$

### Example 4: With Initial Condition

Solve $y' + y = e^x$, $y(0) = 1$.

**Solution:**

$P(x) = 1$, $Q(x) = e^x$

$\mu = e^{\int 1 \, dx} = e^x$

Multiply: $e^xy' + e^xy = e^{2x}$

$ \frac{d}{dx}[e^xy] = e^{2x}$

Integrate: $ e^xy = \int e^{2x} \, dx = \frac{1}{2}e^{2x} + C$

$ y = \frac{e^{2x}/2 + C}{e^x} = \frac{1}{2}e^x + Ce^{-x}$

Use $y(0) = 1$:
$$ 1 = \frac{1}{2}e^0 + Ce^0 = \frac{1}{2} + C$$

$$ C = \frac{1}{2}$$

**Answer:** $ y = \frac{1}{2}e^x + \frac{1}{2}e^{-x} = \frac{1}{2}(e^x + e^{-x})$

### Example 5: Variable Coefficient

Solve $ y' + \frac{2}{x}y = x^2$, $x > 0$.

**Solution:**

$ P(x) = \frac{2}{x}$, $Q(x) = x^2$

$ \mu = e^{\int (2/x) \, dx} = e^{2\ln x} = e^{\ln x^2} = x^2$

Multiply by $x^2$: $x^2y' + 2xy = x^4$

Left side: $ \frac{d}{dx}[x^2y] = x^4$

Integrate: $ x^2y = \int x^4 \, dx = \frac{x^5}{5} + C$

$ y = \frac{x^5/5 + C}{x^2} = \frac{x^3}{5} + \frac{C}{x^2}$

**Answer:** $ y = \frac{x^3}{5} + \frac{C}{x^2}$

## Why Integrating Factor Works

The key insight: if $ \mu = e^{\int P \, dx}$, then $\mu' = \mu P$ (by chain rule).

So: $\mu y' + \mu Py = \mu y' + \mu' y =  \frac{d}{dx}[\mu y]$ (product rule!)

This is why multiplying by $\mu$ creates an exact derivative.

### Example 6: Verifying the Formula

For $y' + 3y = 0$, verify that $\mu = e^{3x}$ makes left side an exact derivative.

**Solution:**

Multiply by $\mu = e^{3x}$:
$$ e^{3x}y' + 3e^{3x}y$$

Check if this equals $ \frac{d}{dx}[e^{3x}y]$:

By product rule: $ \frac{d}{dx}[e^{3x}y] = e^{3x}y' + 3e^{3x}y$ ✓

**Answer:** Yes, $\mu = e^{3x}$ works because $\mu' = 3e^{3x} = 3\mu = \mu P$.

### Example 7: Exponential Integrating Factor

Solve $y' - 3y = 6$.

**Solution:**

$P(x) = -3$, $Q(x) = 6$

$\mu = e^{\int (-3) \, dx} = e^{-3x}$

Multiply: $e^{-3x}y' - 3e^{-3x}y = 6e^{-3x}$

$ \frac{d}{dx}[e^{-3x}y] = 6e^{-3x}$

Integrate: $ e^{-3x}y = \int 6e^{-3x} \, dx = -2e^{-3x} + C$

$y = -2 + Ce^{3x}$

**Answer:** $y = -2 + Ce^{3x}$

## Applications

### Example 8: RC Circuit

An RC circuit with resistance $R$, capacitance $C$, and voltage source $E(t)$ satisfies:
$$ R\frac{dq}{dt} + \frac{q}{C} = E(t)$$

where $q$ is charge. If $R = 100\Omega$, $C = 0.01$F, $E = 10$V (constant), and $q(0) = 0$, find $q(t)$.

**Solution:**

$ 100\frac{dq}{dt} + \frac{q}{0.01} = 10$

$ \frac{dq}{dt} + q = 0.1$

$P = 1$, $Q = 0.1$

$\mu = e^t$

$e^t  \frac{dq}{dt} + e^tq = 0.1e^t$

$ \frac{d}{dx}[e^tq] = 0.1e^t$

$e^tq = 0.1e^t + C$

$q = 0.1 + Ce^{-t}$

Use $q(0) = 0$: $0 = 0.1 + C$, so $C = -0.1$

**Answer:** $q(t) = 0.1(1 - e^{-t})$ coulombs. As $t \to \infty$, $q \to 0.1$C (steady state).

### Example 9: Mixing with Variable Volume

Tank starts with 100L pure water. Brine (0.5 kg/L salt) flows in at 4 L/min; mixture flows out at 2 L/min. Find salt amount $A(t)$.

**Solution:**

Volume: $V(t) = 100 + (4-2)t = 100 + 2t$

Rate in: $0.5 \times 4 = 2$ kg/min

Rate out: $ \frac{A}{100+2t} \times 2 = \frac{2A}{100+2t}$ kg/min

$$ \frac{dA}{dt} = 2 - \frac{2A}{100+2t}$$

$$ \frac{dA}{dt} + \frac{2A}{100+2t} = 2$$

Simplify: $ \frac{dA}{dt} + \frac{A}{50+t} = 2$

$P =  \frac{1}{50+t}$, $Q = 2$

$\mu = e^{\int (1/(50+t)) \, dt} = e^{\ln(50+t)} = 50+t$

Multiply: $(50+t) \frac{dA}{dt} + A = 2(50+t)$

$ \frac{d}{dt}[(50+t)A] = 2(50+t)$

Integrate: $(50+t)A = (50+t)^2 + C$

$A = 50+t +  \frac{C}{50+t}$

Use $A(0) = 0$: $0 = 50 +  \frac{C}{50}$, so $C = -2500$

**Answer:** $ A(t) = 50+t - \frac{2500}{50+t}$ kg

### Example 10: Population with Immigration

Population grows at rate $0.02P$ and receives 100 immigrants per year. Find $P(t)$ if $P(0) = 1000$.

**Solution:**

$$ \frac{dP}{dt} = 0.02P + 100$$

Rearrange: $ \frac{dP}{dt} - 0.02P = 100$

$P = -0.02$, $Q = 100$

$\mu = e^{-0.02t}$

Multiply: $e^{-0.02t} \frac{dP}{dt} - 0.02e^{-0.02t}P = 100e^{-0.02t}$

$ \frac{d}{dt}[e^{-0.02t}P] = 100e^{-0.02t}$

$e^{-0.02t}P =  \frac{100e^{-0.02t}}{-0.02} + C = -5000e^{-0.02t} + C$

$P = -5000 + Ce^{0.02t}$

Use $P(0) = 1000$: $1000 = -5000 + C$, so $C = 6000$

**Answer:** $P(t) = -5000 + 6000e^{0.02t}$

The model suggests unbounded growth with constant immigration.

## Comparison with Separation

Some equations can be solved by either method. Integrating factor often cleaner for linear equations.

### Example 11: Two Methods

Solve $y' + y = 1$ by (a) separation, (b) integrating factor.

**Solution:**

**(a) Separation:**

$y' = 1 - y$

$ \frac{dy}{1-y} = dx$

$-\ln|1-y| = x + C$

$\ln|1-y| = -x + C$

$1 - y = Ae^{-x}$

$y = 1 - Ae^{-x}$

**(b) Integrating factor:**

$P = 1$, $Q = 1$

$\mu = e^x$

$e^xy' + e^xy = e^x$

$ \frac{d}{dx}[e^xy] = e^x$

$e^xy = e^x + C$

$y = 1 + Ce^{-x}$

**Answer:** Both give $y = 1 + Ce^{-x}$ (where $C = -A$ relates the constants).

### Example 12: When Separation Fails

Why can't we separate $y' + xy = x$?

**Solution:**

$-\ln|1-y| =  \frac{x^2}{2} + C$

$1 - y = Ae^{-x^2/2}$

$y = 1 - Ae^{-x^2/2}$

**Answer:** This equation is separable. However, the integrating factor method is often more systematic for linear equations.

### Example 13: Bernoulli Equation Preview

Equation $y' + P(x)y = Q(x)y^n$ (where $n \neq 0, 1$) is **Bernoulli**. It's not linear, but substitution $v = y^{1-n}$ converts it to linear.

For $y' + y = y^2$, what substitution linearizes it?

**Solution:**

This is Bernoulli with $n = 2$.

Substitution: $v = y^{1-2} = y^{-1} =  \frac{1}{y}$

Then $ \frac{dv}{dx} = -\frac{1}{y^2}\frac{dy}{dx}$

Multiply original equation by $-y^{-2}$:
$$-y^{-2}y' - y^{-1} = -1$$

$$ \frac{dv}{dx} - v = -1$$

This is linear in $v$!

**Answer:** Substitution $v = 1/y$ linearizes the equation (covered fully in Topic 1.4).

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Divide equation by 2 (coefficient of $y'$):
$ y' + 3y = \frac{x}{2}$
Standard form: $y' + P(x)y = Q(x)$ with $P = 3$, $Q = x/2$.

**Problem 2: A**
Integrating factor: $\mu = e^{\int P \, dx}$ where $P = 4$
$\mu = e^{\int 4 \, dx} = e^{4x}$
This makes the left side equal to $ \frac{d}{dx}[e^{4x}y]$.

**Problem 3: B**
$y' - 2y = 0$ has $P = -2$, $Q = 0$
$\mu = e^{\int (-2) \, dx} = e^{-2x}$
$e^{-2x}y' - 2e^{-2x}y = 0$
$ \frac{d}{dx}[e^{-2x}y] = 0$
$e^{-2x}y = C$
$y = Ce^{2x}$

**Problem 4: C**
The whole point of the integrating factor!
$\mu y' + \mu Py =  \frac{d}{dx}[\mu y]$ by product rule.
This is why we can integrate both sides.

**Problem 5: C**
$y' + y = 0$ is separable or use integrating factor:
$\mu = e^x$
$e^xy' + e^xy = 0$
$ \frac{d}{dx}[e^xy] = 0$
$e^xy = C$
$y = Ce^{-x}$
Use $y(0) = 5$: $C = 5$
So $y = 5e^{-x}$.
