# ODE Topic 3.1: Systems and Matrix Form

A predator-prey ecosystem. Two interacting chemical reactions. A double pendulum. Coupled electrical circuits. These systems can't be described by a single differential equation—they need **systems of differential equations**. The fox population depends on rabbit population, which depends on fox population. The concentrations of two chemicals influence each other's reaction rates. Motion of one pendulum bob affects the other.

Systems of differential equations model interactions. Rather than one unknown function $y(t)$, we have multiple unknown functions $x_1(t), x_2(t), \ldots, x_n(t)$ that evolve together according to coupled equations. The power of linear algebra transforms these systems into elegant matrix form: $ \frac{d\vec{x}}{dt} = A\vec{x}$. Suddenly, eigenvalues and eigenvectors from linear algebra become the key to understanding dynamic systems.

This article develops the theory of **linear systems with constant coefficients**. We'll learn to write systems in matrix form, convert higher-order equations to first-order systems, and solve homogeneous systems using eigenvalues. The characteristic equation from second-order ODEs generalizes to the **characteristic polynomial** of a matrix. The three cases (distinct real eigenvalues, repeated eigenvalues, complex eigenvalues) parallel our second-order classification, but now in higher dimensions.

## What is a System of Differential Equations?

A **system** consists of multiple equations involving multiple unknown functions.

**Example**: Two-species interaction
$$ \begin{cases} \frac{dx}{dt} = 2x - y \\ \frac{dy}{dt} = x + 3y \end{cases}$$

Here $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ are unknown functions coupled together.

**First-order system**: All equations involve only first derivatives.

### Example 1: Identifying Systems

Which are first-order systems?

(a) $ \begin{cases} x' = 2x + y \\ y' = -x + y \end{cases}$

(b) $ \begin{cases} x'' + y = 0 \\ y' - x = 0 \end{cases}$

(c) $ \begin{cases} x' = xy \\ y' = x^2 - y \end{cases}$

**Solution:**

(a) First-order system ✓ (only $x'$, $y'$ appear)

(b) Not first-order (has $x''$)

(c) First-order system ✓ (though nonlinear in variables)

**Answer:** (a) and (c) are first-order systems.

## Matrix Form

System $ \begin{cases} x' = ax + by \\ y' = cx + dy \end{cases}$ becomes:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}$$

**Compact notation**: $\vec{x}' = A\vec{x}$

where $ \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}$ and $ A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$.

### Example 2: Writing in Matrix Form

Write in matrix form: $ \begin{cases} x' = 3x - 2y \\ y' = 4x + y \end{cases}$

**Solution:**

Identify coefficients:
- $x'$: coefficient of $x$ is 3, coefficient of $y$ is $-2$
- $y'$: coefficient of $x$ is 4, coefficient of $y$ is 1

$$ \begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ \vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x}$

### Example 3: Converting from Matrix Form

Given $ \vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x}$, write as system.

**Solution:**

Multiply out:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2x + 5y \\ -x + 3y \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ \begin{cases} x' = 2x + 5y \\ y' = -x + 3y \end{cases}$

## Converting Higher-Order to Systems

Any $n$-th order equation becomes a first-order system of $n$ equations.

For $y'' = f(t, y, y')$, let:
- $x_1 = y$
- $x_2 = y'$

Then:
- $x_1' = y' = x_2$
- $x_2' = y'' = f(t, x_1, x_2)$

### Example 4: Second-Order to System

Convert $y'' + 3y' - 4y = 0$ to first-order system.

**Solution:**

Let $x_1 = y$, $x_2 = y'$

Then $x_1' = y' = x_2$

And $x_2' = y'' = -3y' + 4y = -3x_2 + 4x_1$

System:
$$ \begin{cases} x_1' = x_2 \\ x_2' = 4x_1 - 3x_2 \end{cases}$$

**Matrix form**:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} x_1' \\ x_2' \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 4 & -3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ \vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 4 & -3 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x}$

### Example 5: Third-Order Equation

Convert $y''' + 2y'' - y' + 3y = 0$ to system.

**Solution:**

Let $x_1 = y$, $x_2 = y'$, $x_3 = y''$

Then:
- $x_1' = y' = x_2$
- $x_2' = y'' = x_3$
- $x_3' = y''' = -2y'' + y' - 3y = -2x_3 + x_2 - 3x_1$

**Matrix form**:
$$ \vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ -3 & 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x}$$

**Answer:** $3 \times 3$ system with matrix as shown.

## Solving Systems: Eigenvalue Method

For $\vec{x}' = A\vec{x}$, try solution $\vec{x} = \vec{v}e^{\lambda t}$ where $\vec{v}$ is constant vector.

Then $\vec{x}' = \lambda\vec{v}e^{\lambda t}$.

Substituting: $\lambda\vec{v}e^{\lambda t} = A\vec{v}e^{\lambda t}$

Divide by $e^{\lambda t}$: $\lambda\vec{v} = A\vec{v}$

This is the **eigenvalue problem**: $A\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v}$

**Method**:
1. Find eigenvalues $\lambda$ of $A$ (solve $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$)
2. Find eigenvector $\vec{v}$ for each $\lambda$ (solve $(A - \lambda I)\vec{v} = \vec{0}$)
3. Write solution $\vec{x} = \vec{v}e^{\lambda t}$

### Example 6: Solving $2 \times 2$ System

Solve $ \vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x}$.

**Solution:**

**Step 1**: Eigenvalues

$$\det(A - \lambda I) = \det\begin{bmatrix} 1-\lambda & 2 \\ 2 & 1-\lambda \end{bmatrix} = (1-\lambda)^2 - 4 = \lambda^2 - 2\lambda - 3$$

$$= (\lambda - 3)(\lambda + 1) = 0$$

Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 3$, $\lambda_2 = -1$

**Step 2**: Eigenvectors

For $\lambda_1 = 3$:
$$(A - 3I)\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 2 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \vec{0}$$

From row 1: $-2v_1 + 2v_2 = 0$, so $v_2 = v_1$

Choose $v_1 = 1$: $ \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

For $\lambda_2 = -1$:
$$(A + I)\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \vec{0}$$

From row 1: $2v_1 + 2v_2 = 0$, so $v_2 = -v_1$

Choose $v_1 = 1$: $ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$

**Step 3**: General solution

$$\vec{x} = c_1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}e^{3t} + c_2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}e^{-t}$$

**Answer:** $ \vec{x} = c_1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}e^{3t} + c_2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}e^{-t}$

### Example 7: Initial Conditions

For Example 6, find solution with $ \vec{x}(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

From Example 6: $ \vec{x} = c_1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}e^{3t} + c_2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}e^{-t}$

At $t = 0$:
$$\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = c_1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + c_2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} c_1 + c_2 \\ c_1 - c_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

System: $c_1 + c_2 = 2$, $c_1 - c_2 = 0$

From second: $c_1 = c_2$

Substitute: $2c_1 = 2$, so $c_1 = c_2 = 1$

**Answer:** $ \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}e^{3t} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}e^{-t}$

## Case 1: Distinct Real Eigenvalues

If $A$ has $n$ distinct real eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n$ with eigenvectors $\vec{v}_1, \ldots, \vec{v}_n$:

**General solution**: $ \vec{x} = c_1\vec{v}_1e^{\lambda_1 t} + \cdots + c_n\vec{v}_ne^{\lambda_n t}$

Each term $\vec{v}_ie^{\lambda_i t}$ is a fundamental solution.

### Example 8: Three Equations

Solve $ \vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x}$.

**Solution:**

Matrix is diagonal! Eigenvalues are diagonal entries: $\lambda_1 = 2$, $\lambda_2 = 3$, $\lambda_3 = -1$

Eigenvectors are standard basis vectors:
$$\vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \vec{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**General solution**:
$$\vec{x} = c_1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}e^{2t} + c_2\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}e^{3t} + c_3\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}e^{-t}$$

Or: $x_1 = c_1e^{2t}$, $x_2 = c_2e^{3t}$, $x_3 = c_3e^{-t}$ (decoupled!)

**Answer:** System decouples into independent equations when matrix is diagonal.

## Case 2: Repeated Eigenvalues

If eigenvalue $\lambda$ has **algebraic multiplicity** $m > 1$:

- If there are $m$ linearly independent eigenvectors: use all of them
- If fewer eigenvectors: need **generalized eigenvectors**

For $2 \times 2$ with repeated eigenvalue $\lambda$ and only one eigenvector $\vec{v}$:

Second solution: $\vec{x}_2 = (\vec{w}t + \vec{v})e^{\lambda t}$

where $\vec{w}$ satisfies $(A - \lambda I)\vec{w} = \vec{v}$ (generalized eigenvector).

### Example 9: Repeated Eigenvalue

Solve $ \vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x}$.

**Solution:**

Eigenvalues: $\det(A - \lambda I) = (2-\lambda)^2 = 0$

$\lambda = 2$ (repeated)

Eigenvector:
$$(A - 2I)\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \vec{0}$$

From row 1: $v_2 = 0$

Choose $v_1 = 1$: $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$

Only one eigenvector! Need generalized eigenvector $\vec{w}$ with $(A - 2I)\vec{w} = \vec{v}$:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} w_1 \\ w_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

From row 1: $w_2 = 1$

Choose $w_1 = 0$: $ \vec{w} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

**Solutions**:
$$\vec{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}e^{2t}$$

$$\vec{x}_2 = \left(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}t + \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right)e^{2t} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ t \end{bmatrix}e^{2t}$$

**Answer:** $ \vec{x} = c_1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}e^{2t} + c_2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ t \end{bmatrix}e^{2t}$

## Case 3: Complex Eigenvalues

If $\lambda = \alpha \pm i\beta$ with eigenvector $\vec{v} = \vec{a} + i\vec{b}$:

**Real solutions**:
$$\vec{x}_1 = e^{\alpha t}(\vec{a}\cos\beta t - \vec{b}\sin\beta t)$$
$$\vec{x}_2 = e^{\alpha t}(\vec{a}\sin\beta t + \vec{b}\cos\beta t)$$

(Separate real and imaginary parts of complex solution)

### Example 10: Complex Eigenvalues

Solve $ \vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x}$.

**Solution:**

Eigenvalues:
$$\det(A - \lambda I) = \det\begin{bmatrix} -\lambda & -1 \\ 1 & -\lambda \end{bmatrix} = \lambda^2 + 1 = 0$$

$\lambda = \pm i$ (so $\alpha = 0$, $\beta = 1$)

For $\lambda = i$:
$$(A - iI)\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -i & -1 \\ 1 & -i \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \vec{0}$$

From row 1: $-iv_1 - v_2 = 0$, so $v_2 = -iv_1$

Choose $v_1 = 1$: $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + i\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$

So $ \vec{a} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $ \vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$

**Real solutions**:
$$\vec{x}_1 = e^{0 \cdot t}\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\cos t - \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\sin t\right) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos t \\ \sin t \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\vec{x}_2 = e^{0 \cdot t}\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\sin t + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\cos t\right) = \begin{bmatrix} \sin t \\ -\cos t \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ \vec{x} = c_1\begin{bmatrix} \cos t \\ \sin t \end{bmatrix} + c_2\begin{bmatrix} \sin t \\ -\cos t \end{bmatrix}$

This represents rotation! The matrix $A$ is a 90° rotation matrix.

### Example 11: Complex with Damping

Solve $ \vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & -2 \\ 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x}$.

**Solution:**

Eigenvalues:
$$\det(A - \lambda I) = (-1-\lambda)^2 + 4 = \lambda^2 + 2\lambda + 5 = 0$$

$$\lambda = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4-20}}{2} = \frac{-2 \pm 4i}{2} = -1 \pm 2i$$

$\alpha = -1$, $\beta = 2$

For $\lambda = -1 + 2i$:
$$(A - (-1+2i)I)\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -2i & -2 \\ 2 & -2i \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \vec{0}$$

From row 1: $-2iv_1 - 2v_2 = 0$, so $v_2 = -iv_1$

Choose $v_1 = 1$: $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{bmatrix}$

$ \vec{a} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $ \vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$

**Real solutions**:
$$\vec{x}_1 = e^{-t}\begin{bmatrix} \cos 2t \\ \sin 2t \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\vec{x}_2 = e^{-t}\begin{bmatrix} \sin 2t \\ -\cos 2t \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** Spiral inward (oscillations with exponential decay).

## Fundamental Matrix

The **fundamental matrix** $\Phi(t)$ has columns that are fundamental solutions:
$$\Phi(t) = \begin{bmatrix} \vec{x}_1(t) & \vec{x}_2(t) & \cdots \end{bmatrix}$$

General solution: $\vec{x}(t) = \Phi(t)\vec{c}$ where $\vec{c}$ is constant vector.

### Example 12: Constructing Fundamental Matrix

For Example 6, write fundamental matrix.

**Solution:**

Fundamental solutions:
$$\vec{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}e^{3t}, \quad \vec{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}e^{-t}$$

$$\Phi(t) = \begin{bmatrix} e^{3t} & e^{-t} \\ e^{3t} & -e^{-t} \end{bmatrix}$$

General solution:
$$\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} e^{3t} & e^{-t} \\ e^{3t} & -e^{-t} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ \Phi(t) = \begin{bmatrix} e^{3t} & e^{-t} \\ e^{3t} & -e^{-t} \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 13: Application - Coupled Masses

Two masses connected by springs satisfy:
$$\begin{cases} m_1 x_1'' = -k_1 x_1 + k_2(x_2 - x_1) \\ m_2 x_2'' = -k_2(x_2 - x_1) - k_3 x_2 \end{cases}$$

How would you solve this system?

**Solution:**

This is a second-order system. Convert to first-order by letting:
$$y_1 = x_1, \quad y_2 = x_1', \quad y_3 = x_2, \quad y_4 = x_2'$$

Then get $4 \times 4$ first-order system $\vec{y}' = A\vec{y}$.

Solve using eigenvalues of $A$.

**Answer:** Convert to first-order $4 \times 4$ system, then use eigenvalue method.

The eigenvalues give the natural frequencies of oscillation (normal modes).

## Practice Problems

**Problem 1:** System $\begin{cases} x' = 2x + y \\ y' = 3x + 4y \end{cases}$ in matrix form is:

A) $ \vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x}$

B) $ \vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x}$

C) $ \vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x}$

D) $ \vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$

E) Cannot be written in matrix form

**Problem 2:** To solve $\vec{x}' = A\vec{x}$, we need:

A) Determinant of $A$
B) Eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $A$
C) Inverse of $A$
D) Transpose of $A$
E) Trace of $A$ only

**Problem 3:** If $A$ has eigenvalue $\lambda = 3$ with eigenvector $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$, one solution is:

A) $ \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}e^t$

B) $ \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}e^{3t}$

C) $ \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}e^t$

D) $ \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} e^t \\ 2e^t \end{bmatrix}$

E) $ \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} t \\ 2t \end{bmatrix}e^{3t}$

**Problem 4:** For repeated eigenvalue with only one eigenvector, the second solution involves:

A) Another eigenvector
B) Generalized eigenvector and factor $t$
C) Complex numbers
D) Integration
E) Higher derivatives

**Problem 5:** Complex eigenvalues $\lambda = \alpha \pm i\beta$ produce solutions that:

A) Grow exponentially
B) Decay exponentially
C) Oscillate
D) Are constant
E) Behavior depends on sign of $\alpha$

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Matrix form: rows correspond to equations.
Row 1 from $x' = 2x + y$: coefficients [2, 1]
Row 2 from $y' = 3x + 4y$: coefficients [3, 4]
$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Problem 2: B**
Eigenvalue method: try $\vec{x} = \vec{v}e^{\lambda t}$
Leads to $A\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v}$ (eigenvalue problem)
Need both $\lambda$ (determines exponential) and $\vec{v}$ (determines direction)

**Problem 3: B**
Solution form: $\vec{x} = \vec{v}e^{\lambda t}$
With $\lambda = 3$ and $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$:
$$\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}e^{3t}$$
Exponential rate determined by eigenvalue.

**Problem 4: B**
For repeated $\lambda$ with deficient eigenvectors:
Second solution: $\vec{x}_2 = (\vec{w}t + \vec{v})e^{\lambda t}$
where $\vec{w}$ is generalized eigenvector satisfying $(A - \lambda I)\vec{w} = \vec{v}$
Similar to $y = (c_1 + c_2x)e^{rx}$ for repeated roots in second-order ODEs.

**Problem 5: E**
Solutions: $e^{\alpha t}(\text{trig functions})$
If $\alpha < 0$: decay × oscillation → damped spiral
If $\alpha = 0$: pure oscillation (center)
If $\alpha > 0$: growth × oscillation → growing spiral
Sign of real part $\alpha$ determines growth/decay.
