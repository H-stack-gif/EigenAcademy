# Linear Algebra Topic 8.4: Vector Calculus Connections

Calculus extends to functions of multiple variables: $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ or $\vec{f}: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$. The derivative becomes a **matrix**—the **Jacobian**—containing all partial derivatives. For scalar functions, the **gradient** $\nabla f$ points in the direction of steepest ascent. For vector functions, the **Hessian** matrix of second derivatives determines the curvature.

These linear algebra tools transform multivariable calculus from computational drudgery into geometric insight. The gradient is orthogonal to level curves. The Hessian's eigenvalues determine whether critical points are minima, maxima, or saddle points. **Directional derivatives** become dot products. **Chain rule** becomes matrix multiplication.

Understanding these connections reveals deep structure. Optimization becomes eigenvalue analysis. Machine learning's gradient descent follows $-\nabla f$ (steepest descent direction). Physics uses gradients (force = $-\nabla U$), divergence, and curl. This article shows how linear algebra provides the natural language for multivariable calculus and optimization.

## Gradient Vector

For scalar function $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, the **gradient** is:
$$ \nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} \\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2} \\ \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix}$$

**Properties**:
- $\nabla f$ points in direction of **steepest ascent**
- $\|\nabla f\|$ = rate of increase in that direction
- $\nabla f \perp$ level curves/surfaces

### Example 1: Computing Gradient

Find $\nabla f$ for $f(x, y) = x^2 + 3xy + y^2$.

**Solution:**

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x + 3y$$

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 3x + 2y$$

$$ \nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} 2x + 3y \\ 3x + 2y \end{bmatrix}$$

At point $(1, 1)$: $ \nabla f(1, 1) = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$

**Answer:** $ \nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} 2x + 3y \\ 3x + 2y \end{bmatrix}$; at $(1, 1)$, gradient is $\begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$ (pointing northeast, 45°).

### Example 2: Gradient Perpendicular to Level Curves

Show that $\nabla f \perp$ level curve $f(x, y) = c$.

**Solution:**

Level curve $f(x, y) = c$ is implicitly defined.

Parametric curve $\vec{r}(t) = (x(t), y(t))$ on level curve satisfies $f(x(t), y(t)) = c$.

Differentiate with respect to $t$:
$$ \frac{d}{dt}f(x(t), y(t)) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt} = 0$$

$$ \nabla f \cdot \vec{r}'(t) = 0$$

**Answer:** Gradient $\nabla f$ is orthogonal to tangent vector $\vec{r}'(t)$ of level curve, hence perpendicular to level curve.

### Example 3: Direction of Steepest Ascent

For $f(x, y) = x^2 - y^2$, find direction of steepest ascent at $(1, 1)$.

**Solution:**

$$ \nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} 2x \\ -2y \end{bmatrix}$$

At $(1, 1)$: $ \nabla f(1, 1) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}$

Normalize: $ \vec{u} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$

**Answer:** Steepest ascent direction is $ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$ (southeast, 45° below horizontal).

## Directional Derivative

The **directional derivative** of $f$ at $\vec{a}$ in direction $\vec{u}$ (unit vector) is:
$$ D_{\vec{u}}f(\vec{a}) = \nabla f(\vec{a}) \cdot \vec{u}$$

**Interpretation**: Rate of change of $f$ when moving from $\vec{a}$ in direction $\vec{u}$.

**Maximum** directional derivative: $\|\nabla f\|$ (in direction $\vec{u} = \nabla f/\|\nabla f\|$)

**Minimum** directional derivative: $-\|\nabla f\|$ (in direction $\vec{u} = -\nabla f/\|\nabla f\|$)

### Example 4: Directional Derivative Calculation

For $f(x, y) = xy$, find directional derivative at $(2, 1)$ in direction $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

$$ \nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} y \\ x \end{bmatrix}, \quad \nabla f(2, 1) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Normalize direction: $ \vec{u} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$

$$ D_{\vec{u}}f(2, 1) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{5}(3 + 8) = \frac{11}{5}$$

**Answer:** $D_{\vec{u}}f = 2.2$ (function increasing at rate 2.2 in given direction).

### Example 5: Maximizing Directional Derivative

What direction maximizes $D_{\vec{u}}f$ for $f(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$ at $(1, 1, 1)$?

**Solution:**

$$ \nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} 2x \\ 2y \\ 2z \end{bmatrix}, \quad \nabla f(1, 1, 1) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Maximum directional derivative occurs in direction of gradient:
$$ \vec{u} = \frac{\nabla f}{\|\nabla f\|} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Maximum rate: $ \|\nabla f\| = 2\sqrt{3}$

**Answer:** Direction $ \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ with rate $2\sqrt{3} \approx 3.46$.

## Jacobian Matrix

For vector function $\vec{f}: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$, the **Jacobian matrix** is the $m \times n$ matrix of partial derivatives:
$$ J = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n} \\ \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix}$$

**Row $i$**: $\nabla f_i$ (gradient of $i$-th component function)

**Special case** ($m = 1$): Jacobian is gradient (row vector)

### Example 6: Computing Jacobian

Find Jacobian of $ \vec{f}(x, y) = \begin{bmatrix} x^2 + y \\ xy \\ x - y^2 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

$$ J = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2 + y) & \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^2 + y) \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(xy) & \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(xy) \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x - y^2) & \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x - y^2) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2x & 1 \\ y & x \\ 1 & -2y \end{bmatrix}$$

At $(1, 2)$: $ J(1, 2) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -4 \end{bmatrix}$

**Answer:** $ J = \begin{bmatrix} 2x & 1 \\ y & x \\ 1 & -2y \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 7: Linear Approximation

Use Jacobian to approximate $\vec{f}(\vec{a} + \Delta\vec{x})$.

**Solution:**

**Linear approximation**:
$$ \vec{f}(\vec{a} + \Delta\vec{x}) \approx \vec{f}(\vec{a}) + J(\vec{a})\Delta\vec{x}$$

This is multivariable version of $f(a + \Delta x) \approx f(a) + f'(a)\Delta x$.

For Example 6 at $(1, 2)$ with $ \Delta\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 \\ -0.1 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$ \vec{f}(1, 2) = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \vec{f}(1.1, 1.9) \approx \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0.1 \\ -0.1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 \\ 0.1 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3.1 \\ 2.1 \\ -2.5 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** Linear approximation $ \approx \begin{bmatrix} 3.1 \\ 2.1 \\ -2.5 \end{bmatrix}$.

## Chain Rule

For composite function $\vec{h} = \vec{g} \circ \vec{f}$ (i.e., $\vec{h}(\vec{x}) = \vec{g}(\vec{f}(\vec{x}))$):
$$ J_{\vec{h}} = J_{\vec{g}} \cdot J_{\vec{f}}$$

**Matrix multiplication** of Jacobians!

### Example 8: Chain Rule Application

Let $\vec{f}(x, y) = \begin{bmatrix} x + y \\ x - y \end{bmatrix}$ and $\vec{g}(u, v) = \begin{bmatrix} u^2 + v^2 \end{bmatrix}$.

Find Jacobian of $h = \vec{g} \circ \vec{f}$.

**Solution:**

$$ J_{\vec{f}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ J_{\vec{g}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2u & 2v \end{bmatrix}$$

At $(u, v) = (x+y, x-y)$:
$$ J_{\vec{g}}(\vec{f}(x, y)) = \begin{bmatrix} 2(x+y) & 2(x-y) \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ J_h = J_{\vec{g}} \cdot J_{\vec{f}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2(x+y) & 2(x-y) \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = \begin{bmatrix} 2(x+y) + 2(x-y) & 2(x+y) - 2(x-y) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4x & 4y \end{bmatrix}$$

Verify directly: $h(x, y) = (x+y)^2 + (x-y)^2 = 2x^2 + 2y^2$, so $\nabla h = \begin{bmatrix} 4x & 4y \end{bmatrix}$ ✓

**Answer:** $J_h = \begin{bmatrix} 4x & 4y \end{bmatrix}$

## Hessian Matrix

For scalar $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, the **Hessian matrix** is the $n \times n$ matrix of second partial derivatives:
$$ H = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1^2} & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1 \partial x_2} & \cdots \\ \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_2 \partial x_1} & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_2^2} & \cdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{bmatrix}$$

**Key property**: If $f$ has continuous second partials, then $H$ is **symmetric** (Schwarz's theorem: mixed partials commute).

### Example 9: Computing Hessian

Find Hessian of $f(x, y) = x^3 + xy^2 + y^3$.

**Solution:**

First partials:
$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 3x^2 + y^2, \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 2xy + 3y^2$$

Second partials:
$$ \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} = 6x$$

$$ \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2} = 2x + 6y$$

$$ \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \partial y} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y \partial x} = 2y$$

$$ H = \begin{bmatrix} 6x & 2y \\ 2y & 2x + 6y \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ H = \begin{bmatrix} 6x & 2y \\ 2y & 2x + 6y \end{bmatrix}$ (symmetric as expected).

### Example 10: Second Derivative Test

Use Hessian to classify critical point of $f(x, y) = x^2 - 2xy + 2y^2$.

**Solution:**

$$ \nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} 2x - 2y \\ -2x + 4y \end{bmatrix} = \vec{0}$$

Gives $x = y$ and $-2x + 4x = 0$, so $x = y = 0$.

Hessian:
$$ H = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ -2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

Eigenvalues: $\det(H - \lambda I) = (2-\lambda)(4-\lambda) - 4 = \lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 4 = 0$

$$ \lambda = \frac{6 \pm \sqrt{36-16}}{2} = \frac{6 \pm \sqrt{20}}{2} = 3 \pm \sqrt{5}$$

Both eigenvalues positive ($3 - \sqrt{5} \approx 0.76 > 0$, $3 + \sqrt{5} \approx 5.24 > 0$).

**Answer:** Local minimum at $(0, 0)$ (Hessian positive definite).

## Optimization Applications

**Gradient descent**: Iteratively update $\vec{x}_{k+1} = \vec{x}_k - \alpha \nabla f(\vec{x}_k)$ to minimize $f$.

**Newton's method**: $\vec{x}_{k+1} = \vec{x}_k - H^{-1}\nabla f(\vec{x}_k)$ (uses second derivatives for faster convergence).

**Lagrange multipliers**: To optimize $f(\vec{x})$ subject to $g(\vec{x}) = 0$, solve:
$$ \nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$$

### Example 11: Gradient Descent Step

For $f(x, y) = (x-1)^2 + (y-2)^2$, perform one gradient descent step from $(0, 0)$ with $\alpha = 0.1$.

**Solution:**

$$ \nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} 2(x-1) \\ 2(y-2) \end{bmatrix}, \quad \nabla f(0, 0) = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -4 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \vec{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} - 0.1\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.2 \\ 0.4 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** New point $(0.2, 0.4)$ (moving toward minimum at $(1, 2)$).

### Example 12: Lagrange Multipliers

Maximize $f(x, y) = xy$ subject to $x + y = 10$.

**Solution:**

Constraint: $g(x, y) = x + y - 10 = 0$

$$ \nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} y \\ x \end{bmatrix}, \quad \nabla g = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Condition: $ \begin{bmatrix} y \\ x \end{bmatrix} = \lambda \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

Gives $y = \lambda$ and $x = \lambda$, so $x = y$.

From constraint: $x + x = 10$, so $x = 5, y = 5$.

**Answer:** Maximum at $(5, 5)$ with $f(5, 5) = 25$.

### Example 13: Physics Application

**Potential energy** $U(\vec{x})$ generates **force** $\vec{F} = -\nabla U$.

For $U(x, y) = \frac{1}{2}k(x^2 + y^2)$ (spring potential):

$$ \vec{F} = -\nabla U = -\begin{bmatrix} kx \\ ky \end{bmatrix} = -k\vec{r}$$

**Answer:** Restoring force proportional to displacement (Hooke's law), pointing toward origin.

## Practice Problems

**Problem 2:** For Jacobian matrix, row $i$ contains:

A) Partial derivatives of all functions with respect to $x_i$
B) Gradient of $i$-th component function
C) $i$-th column of Hessian
D) Eigenvalues
E) Second derivatives

**Problem 3:** The Hessian matrix is always:

A) Diagonal
B) Symmetric (for smooth functions)
C) Positive definite
D) Invertible
E) Upper triangular

**Problem 4:** At a local minimum, the Hessian is:

A) Negative definite
B) Positive definite
C) Indefinite
D) Zero
E) Singular

**Problem 5:** Directional derivative $D_{\vec{u}}f$ equals:

A) $\|\nabla f\|$
B) $\nabla f \times \vec{u}$
C) $\nabla f \cdot \vec{u}$
D) $f'(\vec{u})$
E) $\vec{u}^T H \vec{u}$

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Gradient points in direction of steepest ascent.
$-\nabla f$ points in direction of steepest descent.
$\nabla f$ is perpendicular to level curves.

**Problem 2: B**
Jacobian row $i$ = $\nabla f_i$ (gradient of $i$-th component).
Each row shows how one output varies with all inputs.
Columns show how all outputs vary with one input.

**Problem 3: B**
Hessian is symmetric if mixed partials commute (Schwarz's theorem).
Holds for $C^2$ functions (continuous second derivatives).
Not always positive definite (depends on function).

**Problem 4: B**
At local minimum, Hessian is positive definite.
All eigenvalues $> 0$.
Positive definite → bowl-shaped locally.
Negative definite → local maximum.

**Problem 5: C**
$D_{\vec{u}}f = \nabla f \cdot \vec{u}$ (dot product).
Projects gradient onto direction $\vec{u}$.
Maximum when $\vec{u}$ aligned with $\nabla f$.
