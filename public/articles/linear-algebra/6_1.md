# Linear Algebra Topic 6.1: Inner Products and Orthogonality

The dot product isn't just a computational tool—it measures **geometric relationships**. When two vectors have dot product zero, they're perpendicular, or **orthogonal**. This simple idea has profound consequences. Orthogonal vectors are independent in the strongest possible sense: they point in completely different directions. Orthogonal bases make computations trivial—coordinates are just projections.

In calculus, we integrate to find areas. In linear algebra, we use inner products to measure "overlap" between vectors or functions. This abstraction lets us apply geometric intuition to spaces of polynomials, continuous functions, or even solutions to differential equations. The Fourier series, which decomposes signals into pure frequencies, relies entirely on orthogonality of trigonometric functions.

Understanding orthogonality transforms linear algebra from computational machinery into geometric insight. We'll develop the language of inner products, explore orthogonal and orthonormal sets, and see why orthogonal bases are optimal for computation. These concepts underpin computer graphics, signal processing, quantum mechanics, and numerical analysis.

## The Dot Product (Standard Inner Product)

For vectors $\vec{u}, \vec{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the **dot product** (or **inner product**) is:
$$ \vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = u_1v_1 + u_2v_2 + \cdots + u_nv_n = \sum_{i=1}^n u_iv_i$$

**Properties**:
1. $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = \vec{v} \cdot \vec{u}$ (commutative)
2. $(\vec{u} + \vec{v}) \cdot \vec{w} = \vec{u} \cdot \vec{w} + \vec{v} \cdot \vec{w}$ (distributive)
3. $(c\vec{u}) \cdot \vec{v} = c(\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v})$ (scalar multiplication)
4. $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{u} \geq 0$, and $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{u} = 0$ iff $\vec{u} = \vec{0}$ (positive definite)

**Matrix notation**: $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = \vec{u}^T\vec{v}$

### Example 1: Computing Dot Products

Find $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}$ for $ \vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 4 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

$$ \vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = (2)(1) + (-1)(4) + (3)(-2) = 2 - 4 - 6 = -8$$

**Answer:** $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = -8$

### Example 2: Dot Product and Angle

The angle $\theta$ between vectors satisfies:
$$ \vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = \|\vec{u}\| \|\vec{v}\| \cos\theta$$

Find the angle between $ \vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

$$ \vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = (1)(1) + (0)(1) = 1$$

$$ \|\vec{u}\| = \sqrt{1^2 + 0^2} = 1$$

$$ \|\vec{v}\| = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}$$

$$ \cos\theta = \frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{\|\vec{u}\| \|\vec{v}\|} = \frac{1}{1 \cdot \sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$$

$$ \theta = \arccos\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = 45°$$

**Answer:** The angle is 45°.

## Orthogonality

Vectors $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ are **orthogonal** if:
$$ \vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 0$$

Notation: $\vec{u} \perp \vec{v}$

**Geometric meaning**: Orthogonal vectors are perpendicular (meet at 90°).

**Convention**: The zero vector is orthogonal to every vector.

### Example 3: Checking Orthogonality

Determine if $ \vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ -4 \end{bmatrix}$ are orthogonal.

**Solution:**

$$ \vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = (2)(1) + (3)(-2) + (-1)(-4) = 2 - 6 + 4 = 0$$

Since the dot product is zero, the vectors are orthogonal.

**Answer:** Yes, orthogonal.

### Example 4: Finding Orthogonal Vector

Find a vector orthogonal to $ \vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**Solution:**

Need $\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix}$ with $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 0$:

$$ 3v_1 + 2v_2 = 0$$

Choose $v_2 = 3$: then $3v_1 + 6 = 0$, giving $v_1 = -2$

**Answer:** $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$ (or any scalar multiple, like $ \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}$)

**General pattern in $\mathbb{R}^2$**: If $\vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix}$, then $\begin{bmatrix} -b \\ a \end{bmatrix}$ is orthogonal.

## Length and Normalization

The **length** (or **norm**) of vector $\vec{v}$ is:
$$ \|\vec{v}\| = \sqrt{\vec{v} \cdot \vec{v}} = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \cdots + v_n^2}$$

A **unit vector** has length 1.

To **normalize** $\vec{v}$ (make it unit length):
$$ \vec{u} = \frac{1}{\|\vec{v}\|}\vec{v}$$

### Example 5: Normalizing a Vector

Normalize $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

$$ \|\vec{v}\| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$$

$$ \vec{u} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3/5 \\ 4/5 \end{bmatrix}$$

Verify: $ \|\vec{u}\| = \sqrt{(3/5)^2 + (4/5)^2} = \sqrt{\frac{9+16}{25}} = \sqrt{\frac{25}{25}} = 1$ ✓

**Answer:** $ \vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 \\ 0.8 \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 6: Distance Between Vectors

The distance between $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ is $\|\vec{u} - \vec{v}\|$.

Find distance between $ \vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

$$ \vec{u} - \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \|\vec{u} - \vec{v}\| = \sqrt{(-3)^2 + 2^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{9 + 4 + 4} = \sqrt{17}$$

**Answer:** Distance = $\sqrt{17} \approx 4.12$

## Orthogonal Sets

A set $\{\vec{u}_1, \vec{u}_2, \ldots, \vec{u}_p\}$ is an **orthogonal set** if:
$$ \vec{u}_i \cdot \vec{u}_j = 0 \text{ for all } i \neq j$$

**Key theorem**: If $\{\vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_p\}$ is an orthogonal set of nonzero vectors, then the set is **linearly independent**.

**Proof idea**: If $c_1\vec{u}_1 + \cdots + c_p\vec{u}_p = \vec{0}$, dot both sides with $\vec{u}_i$ to get $c_i\|\vec{u}_i\|^2 = 0$, so $c_i = 0$.

### Example 7: Verifying Orthogonal Set

Verify that $ \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$ is an orthogonal set.

**Solution:**

Check all pairs:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = 1 - 1 + 0 = 0$$ ✓

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0$$ ✓

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0$$ ✓

**Answer:** Yes, orthogonal set. Since we have 3 orthogonal vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$, they form a basis.

### Example 8: Showing Linear Independence

Show that the orthogonal set in Example 7 is linearly independent.

**Solution:**

Suppose $ c_1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + c_2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + c_3\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$

Dot both sides with $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$ c_1 \cdot 2 + c_2 \cdot 0 + c_3 \cdot 0 = 0 \implies c_1 = 0$$

Similarly, dotting with the other vectors gives $c_2 = 0$ and $c_3 = 0$.

**Answer:** Linearly independent (as guaranteed by the theorem for orthogonal sets).

## Orthonormal Sets

A set is **orthonormal** if:
1. It's orthogonal (all pairs perpendicular)
2. Each vector has length 1 (unit vectors)

**Standard basis** $\{\vec{e}_1, \vec{e}_2, \ldots, \vec{e}_n\}$ is orthonormal.

### Example 9: Creating Orthonormal Set

Convert the orthogonal set from Example 7 to an orthonormal set.

**Solution:**

Normalize each vector:

$$ \vec{u}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \vec{u}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} \\ -1/\sqrt{2} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \vec{u}_3 = \frac{1}{1}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** Orthonormal set: $ \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} \\ -1/\sqrt{2} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$

## Orthogonal Bases

An **orthogonal basis** for subspace $W$ is a basis for $W$ consisting of orthogonal vectors.

An **orthonormal basis** is an orthogonal basis of unit vectors.

**Why orthonormal bases are great**: Coordinates are easy to compute!

If $\{\vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_p\}$ is orthonormal basis for $W$, then for any $\vec{y} \in W$:
$$ \vec{y} = (\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_1)\vec{u}_1 + (\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_2)\vec{u}_2 + \cdots + (\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_p)\vec{u}_p$$

The coefficients are just dot products—no solving systems!

### Example 10: Coordinates in Orthonormal Basis

Express $ \vec{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ as a linear combination of the orthonormal basis from Example 9.

**Solution:**

$$ c_1 = \vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_1 = 3 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} + 1 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} + 0 \cdot 0 = \frac{4}{\sqrt{2}} = 2\sqrt{2}$$

$$ c_2 = \vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_2 = 3 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} + 1 \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) + 0 \cdot 0 = \frac{2}{\sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{2}$$

$$ c_3 = \vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_3 = 3 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 = 0$$

**Answer:** $ \vec{y} = 2\sqrt{2}\,\vec{u}_1 + \sqrt{2}\,\vec{u}_2 + 0\,\vec{u}_3$

Verify: $ 2\sqrt{2} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \sqrt{2} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ ✓

## Orthogonal Complements

The **orthogonal complement** of subspace $W$ is:
$$ W^{\perp} = \{\vec{v} : \vec{v} \cdot \vec{w} = 0 \text{ for all } \vec{w} \in W\}$$

**Key facts**:
- $W^{\perp}$ is a subspace
- $(W^{\perp})^{\perp} = W$
- $W \cap W^{\perp} = \{\vec{0}\}$
- $\dim(W) + \dim(W^{\perp}) = n$ (for subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^n$)

### Example 11: Finding Orthogonal Complement

Find $W^{\perp}$ where $ W = \text{Span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

**Solution:**

$\vec{v} \in W^{\perp}$ iff $\vec{v} \perp \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$ v_1 + 2v_2 + 3v_3 = 0$$

This defines a plane through the origin. Free variables: $v_2 = s$, $v_3 = t$

Then $v_1 = -2s - 3t$

$$ W^{\perp} = \text{Span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$$

**Answer:** $W^{\perp}$ is the plane perpendicular to $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

Verify: $\dim(W) = 1$, $\dim(W^{\perp}) = 2$, and $1 + 2 = 3$ ✓

### Example 12: Row Space and Null Space

Show that $(\text{Row}(A))^{\perp} = \text{Nul}(A)$.

**Solution:**

$\vec{x} \in \text{Nul}(A)$ iff $A\vec{x} = \vec{0}$

This means each row of $A$ dotted with $\vec{x}$ equals zero.

So $\vec{x}$ is orthogonal to every row of $A$.

Therefore $\vec{x} \in (\text{Row}(A))^{\perp}$.

**Answer:** $(\text{Row}(A))^{\perp} = \text{Nul}(A)$ and $(\text{Col}(A))^{\perp} = \text{Nul}(A^T)$

### Example 13: Orthogonal Decomposition

Every $\vec{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ can be uniquely written as:
$$ \vec{y} = \hat{\vec{y}} + \vec{z}$$

where $\hat{\vec{y}} \in W$ and $\vec{z} \in W^{\perp}$.

For $W = \text{Span}\{\vec{u}\}$ and unit vector $\vec{u}$:
$$ \hat{\vec{y}} = (\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u})\vec{u}$$

This is the **orthogonal projection** of $\vec{y}$ onto $W$.

Find the projection of $ \vec{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ onto $ W = \text{Span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$.

**Solution:**

First normalize: $ \vec{u} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3/5 \\ 4/5 \end{bmatrix}$

$$ \vec{y} \cdot \vec{u} = 5 \cdot \frac{3}{5} + 1 \cdot \frac{4}{5} = \frac{15 + 4}{5} = \frac{19}{5}$$

$$ \hat{\vec{y}} = \frac{19}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 3/5 \\ 4/5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 57/25 \\ 76/25 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** Projection $=  \begin{bmatrix} 2.28 \\ 3.04 \end{bmatrix}$
