# Linear Algebra Topic 7.3: Applications of Spectral Decomposition

The spectral theorem isn't just beautiful theory—it's a computational and conceptual powerhouse. From Google's PageRank algorithm to analyzing vibrating mechanical systems, from data compression to quantum mechanics, spectral decomposition reveals the fundamental "modes" or "components" underlying complex systems. When we write $A = \sum \lambda_i\vec{u}_i\vec{u}_i^T$, we're saying "this transformation is just weighted stretching along perpendicular directions."

**Principal Component Analysis (PCA)** uses spectral decomposition to find the most important patterns in high-dimensional data. Have 10,000 gene expression measurements? PCA finds the 3 principal directions capturing most variation. **Image compression** keeps the largest eigenvalues and discards small ones. **Stability analysis** of differential equations checks if eigenvalues are negative (stable) or positive (unstable).

This article explores how spectral decomposition transforms abstract eigenvalue problems into concrete applications. We'll see how eigenvectors reveal natural vibration modes, how eigenvalues measure data variance, and how symmetric matrices provide geometric insight into optimization landscapes. Understanding these applications means seeing linear algebra in action across science, engineering, and data science.

## Spectral Decomposition Review

For symmetric $n \times n$ matrix $A$ with orthonormal eigenvectors $\vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_n$ and eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n$:

$$ A = PDP^T = \lambda_1\vec{u}_1\vec{u}_1^T + \lambda_2\vec{u}_2\vec{u}_2^T + \cdots + \lambda_n\vec{u}_n\vec{u}_n^T$$

Each term $P_i = \vec{u}_i\vec{u}_i^T$ is an **orthogonal projection** onto span$\{\vec{u}_i\}$.

**Interpretation**: $A$ is a weighted sum of projections onto eigenspaces.

### Example 1: Rank-1 Projections

For unit vector $ \vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 3/5 \\ 4/5 \end{bmatrix}$, compute $P = \vec{u}\vec{u}^T$.

**Solution:**

$$ P = \begin{bmatrix} 3/5 \\ 4/5 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3/5 & 4/5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 9/25 & 12/25 \\ 12/25 & 16/25 \end{bmatrix}$$

Verify it's a projection: $P^2 = P$:

$$ P^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 9/25 & 12/25 \\ 12/25 & 16/25 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 9/25 & 12/25 \\ 12/25 & 16/25 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = \begin{bmatrix} 81/625 + 144/625 & 108/625 + 192/625 \\ 108/625 + 192/625 & 144/625 + 256/625 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 9/25 & 12/25 \\ 12/25 & 16/25 \end{bmatrix} = P$$ ✓

**Answer:** $P$ projects onto the line through $\vec{u}$.

### Example 2: Spectral Decomposition Construction

For $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 6$, $\lambda_2 = 1$ and normalized eigenvectors $ \vec{u}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $ \vec{u}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$, write the spectral decomposition.

**Solution:**

$$ A = 6\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) + 1\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\right)$$

$$ = \frac{6}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ -2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = \begin{bmatrix} 24/5 & 12/5 \\ 12/5 & 6/5 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1/5 & -2/5 \\ -2/5 & 4/5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$ ✓

**Answer:** $ A = \frac{6}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ -2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$

## Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

**Setup**: Data matrix $X$ with $m$ observations (rows) and $n$ features (columns).

**Goal**: Find directions of maximum variance in the data.

**Method**:
1. Center data: subtract mean from each column
2. Compute covariance matrix: $ C = \frac{1}{m}X^TX$ (symmetric!)
3. Find eigenvectors of $C$ (principal components)
4. Project data onto top eigenvectors

**Eigenvalues** = variance along principal component directions.

**Eigenvectors** = principal component directions (orthogonal).

### Example 3: Two-Dimensional PCA

Data: $ X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \\ 3 & 3 \\ 4 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ (4 points along $y = x$).

Find principal components.

**Solution:**

Mean: $ \vec{\mu} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.5 \\ 2.5 \end{bmatrix}$

Centered data:
$$ X_c = \begin{bmatrix} -1.5 & -1.5 \\ -0.5 & -0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 1.5 & 1.5 \end{bmatrix}$$

Covariance:
$$ C = \frac{1}{4}\begin{bmatrix} -1.5 & -0.5 & 0.5 & 1.5 \\ -1.5 & -0.5 & 0.5 & 1.5 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} -1.5 & -1.5 \\ -0.5 & -0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 1.5 & 1.5 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = \frac{1}{4}\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 5 \\ 5 & 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.25 & 1.25 \\ 1.25 & 1.25 \end{bmatrix}$$

Eigenvalues: $\det(C - \lambda I) = (1.25 - \lambda)^2 - 1.25^2 = -\lambda(2.5 - \lambda) = 0$

$\lambda_1 = 2.5$ (all variance), $\lambda_2 = 0$ (no variance perpendicular to line)

Eigenvector for $\lambda_1 = 2.5$: $ \vec{u}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ (direction of line)

**Answer:** First PC is along $y = x$ with variance 2.5; second PC has zero variance (data is 1-dimensional).

### Example 4: Dimensionality Reduction

If eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 100$, $\lambda_2 = 50$, $\lambda_3 = 1$, $\lambda_4 = 0.5$, how many PCs capture most variance?

**Solution:**

Total variance: $100 + 50 + 1 + 0.5 = 151.5$

First two PCs: $100 + 50 = 150$ (99% of variance)

**Answer:** Keep first 2 PCs, reduce from 4D to 2D with minimal information loss.

This is **dimensionality reduction**: represent data in fewer dimensions.

### Example 5: Image Compression

A $100 \times 100$ grayscale image is a matrix $A$. SVD (similar to spectral decomposition) gives $A = \sum \sigma_i \vec{u}_i\vec{v}_i^T$.

If first 10 singular values are large and rest are small, approximate:
$$ A \approx \sigma_1\vec{u}_1\vec{v}_1^T + \cdots + \sigma_{10}\vec{u}_{10}\vec{v}_{10}^T$$

**Storage**: Original: $100 \times 100 = 10,000$ values. Compressed: $10 \times (100 + 100 + 1) = 2,010$ values.

**Answer:** 80% compression with minimal quality loss (keep dominant components).

## Optimization and Quadratic Forms

For quadratic function $f(\vec{x}) = \frac{1}{2}\vec{x}^TA\vec{x} - \vec{b}^T\vec{x}$:

**Gradient**: $\nabla f = A\vec{x} - \vec{b}$

**Critical point**: $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$

**Hessian**: $H = A$ (constant!)

**Nature of critical point** determined by eigenvalues of $A$:
- All $\lambda_i > 0$ → minimum
- All $\lambda_i < 0$ → maximum
- Mixed signs → saddle

### Example 6: Minimization Problem

Minimize $f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + 4x_2^2 - 2x_1 - 8x_2$.

**Solution:**

Write as quadratic form: $f = \frac{1}{2}\vec{x}^TA\vec{x} - \vec{b}^T\vec{x}$ where
$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 8 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix}$$

Critical point: $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$
$$ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 8 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Eigenvalues of $A$: $\lambda_1 = 2 > 0$, $\lambda_2 = 8 > 0$ (both positive).

**Answer:** Minimum at $(1, 1)$ with value $f(1, 1) = 1 + 4 - 2 - 8 = -5$.

### Example 7: Constrained Optimization

Maximize $\vec{x}^TA\vec{x}$ subject to $\|\vec{x}\| = 1$.

**Solution (Rayleigh Quotient)**:

At maximum, $\vec{x}$ is the eigenvector corresponding to the **largest eigenvalue** $\lambda_{\max}$, and the maximum value is $\lambda_{\max}$.

**Proof idea**: In eigenbasis, $\vec{x}^TA\vec{x} = \sum \lambda_i y_i^2$ where $\sum y_i^2 = 1$. Maximized when all weight on largest $\lambda_i$.

**Answer:** Maximum value = largest eigenvalue; achieved at corresponding eigenvector.

## Vibration and Differential Equations

System of ODEs: $ \frac{d\vec{x}}{dt} = A\vec{x}$

**Solution**: $\vec{x}(t) = \sum c_i e^{\lambda_i t}\vec{v}_i$ where $\lambda_i$ are eigenvalues, $\vec{v}_i$ are eigenvectors.

For **symmetric** $A$ in mechanical systems (like springs), eigenvalues are real:
- $\lambda_i < 0$ → exponential decay (stable)
- $\lambda_i > 0$ → exponential growth (unstable)
- $\lambda_i = 0$ → constant (neutrally stable)

### Example 8: Two-Mass Spring System

Two masses connected by springs:
$$ \frac{d^2\vec{x}}{dt^2} = -A\vec{x}$$

where $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ (symmetric!).

Find natural frequencies.

**Solution:**

Eigenvalues of $A$:
$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = (2-\lambda)^2 - 1 = \lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = (\lambda - 3)(\lambda - 1) = 0$$

$\lambda_1 = 3$, $\lambda_2 = 1$

Natural frequencies: $\omega_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$

$\omega_1 = \sqrt{3}$, $\omega_2 = 1$

**Answer:** System vibrates at frequencies $\sqrt{3}$ and $1$. Eigenvectors give vibration modes (patterns of oscillation).

### Example 9: Normal Modes

For Example 8, find vibration modes.

**Solution:**

**For $\lambda_1 = 3$**:
$$ (A - 3I)\vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\vec{v}_1 = \vec{0}$$

Eigenvector: $ \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$ (masses move opposite directions)

**For $\lambda_2 = 1$**:
$$ (A - I)\vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\vec{v}_2 = \vec{0}$$

Eigenvector: $ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ (masses move together)

**Answer:** Mode 1 (high frequency): opposite motion. Mode 2 (low frequency): in-phase motion.

## Graph Theory Applications

For undirected graph, **adjacency matrix** $A$ is symmetric ($a_{ij} = 1$ if edge between $i$ and $j$).

**Degree matrix** $D$: diagonal with $d_{ii} = $ degree of vertex $i$.

**Laplacian matrix**: $L = D - A$ (symmetric!).

Eigenvalues of $L$ reveal graph structure:
- Number of zero eigenvalues = number of connected components
- Second-smallest eigenvalue (algebraic connectivity) measures how well-connected the graph is

### Example 10: Simple Graph Laplacian

For triangle graph (vertices 1, 2, 3 all connected):

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad D = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ L = D - A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ -1 & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Find eigenvalues.

**Solution:**

By symmetry, expect $ \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ as eigenvector:

$$ L\vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2-1-1 \\ -1+2-1 \\ -1-1+2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

So $\lambda_1 = 0$ (always for connected graph).

Other eigenvalues (computation omitted): $\lambda_2 = \lambda_3 = 3$.

**Answer:** One zero eigenvalue confirms graph is connected.

## PageRank Algorithm

Google's PageRank models web as graph where pages are vertices and links are edges.

**PageRank vector** $\vec{p}$ satisfies:
$$ A\vec{p} = \lambda\vec{p}$$

where $A$ is a modified adjacency matrix (stochastic, accounts for damping).

The dominant eigenvector (for $\lambda = 1$) gives page rankings!

### Example 11: Mini PageRank

Three pages: 1 links to 2 and 3; 2 links to 3; 3 links to 1.

Transition matrix: $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1/2 & 0 & 0 \\ 1/2 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$

Find steady-state distribution.

**Solution:**

Need eigenvector for $\lambda = 1$:
$$ (A - I)\vec{p} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1/2 & -1 & 0 \\ 1/2 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\vec{p} = \vec{0}$$

From row 1: $p_3 = p_1$
From row 2: $p_1 = 2p_2$
From row 3: $p_1 + 2p_2 = 2p_3 = 2p_1$, so $p_2 = p_1/2$

With normalization $p_1 + p_2 + p_3 = 1$: $p_1 + p_1/2 + p_1 = 1$, giving $p_1 = 2/5$

**Answer:** $ \vec{p} = \begin{bmatrix} 2/5 \\ 1/5 \\ 2/5 \end{bmatrix}$ (pages 1 and 3 have equal importance, page 2 is half as important).

### Example 12: Matrix Approximation

Best rank-$k$ approximation to symmetric $A$:
$$ A_k = \lambda_1\vec{u}_1\vec{u}_1^T + \cdots + \lambda_k\vec{u}_k\vec{u}_k^T$$

(sum of $k$ largest eigenvalue terms).

This minimizes $\|A - A_k\|_F$ (Frobenius norm).

For $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ with eigenvalues 6, 1, find rank-1 approximation.

**Solution:**

Keep only largest eigenvalue term:
$$ A_1 = \frac{6}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4.8 & 2.4 \\ 2.4 & 1.2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Error: $\|A - A_1\|_F = \sqrt{\lambda_2^2} = 1$ (equals discarded eigenvalue).

**Answer:** Best rank-1 approximation has error equal to second eigenvalue.

## Positive Definite Matrices in Statistics

Covariance matrix $\Sigma$ is always **positive semidefinite**:
$$ \vec{x}^T\Sigma\vec{x} = \text{Var}(\vec{x}^T\vec{X}) \geq 0$$

If variables are linearly independent, $\Sigma$ is **positive definite** (all eigenvalues $> 0$).

**Mahalanobis distance**: $ d(\vec{x}, \vec{\mu}) = \sqrt{(\vec{x} - \vec{\mu})^T\Sigma^{-1}(\vec{x} - \vec{\mu})}$

Measures distance accounting for covariance structure.

### Example 13: Correlation and Eigenvalues

Correlation matrix: $ R = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.8 \\ 0.8 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$

Find eigenvalues.

**Solution:**

$$ \det(R - \lambda I) = (1-\lambda)^2 - 0.64 = \lambda^2 - 2\lambda + 0.36 = 0$$

$$ \lambda = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 1.44}}{2} = \frac{2 \pm 1.6}{2}$$

$\lambda_1 = 1.8$, $\lambda_2 = 0.2$

**Answer:** Eigenvalues are 1.8 and 0.2 (both positive → positive definite).

High correlation (0.8) means large difference in eigenvalues (data mostly along one direction).

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
$\vec{u}_i\vec{u}_i^T$ is outer product of unit vector with itself.
Creates rank-1 projection matrix onto span$\{\vec{u}_i\}$.
$P^2 = P$ (idempotent), $P^T = P$ (symmetric).

**Problem 2: B**
Principal components = eigenvectors of covariance matrix.
Eigenvalues = variances along principal component directions.
First PC = direction of maximum variance.

**Problem 3: C**
Critical point is minimum iff Hessian is positive definite.
For $f = \vec{x}^TA\vec{x}$, Hessian is $2A$.
Positive definite iff all eigenvalues positive.

**Problem 4: D**
Rayleigh quotient: maximum of $\vec{x}^TA\vec{x}$ over unit vectors.
Achieved at eigenvector for largest eigenvalue.
Maximum value = $\lambda_{\max}$.

**Problem 5: C**
Laplacian always has $\vec{1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ \vdots \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ as eigenvector with $\lambda = 0$.
Number of zero eigenvalues = number of connected components.
One zero eigenvalue iff connected.
