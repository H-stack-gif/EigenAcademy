# Linear Algebra Topic 2.4: Coordinate Systems

On a city street grid, you might say "3 blocks east, 2 blocks north" to describe a location. Change the reference point, and the numbers change—but the location doesn't. This is the essence of **coordinates**: representing the same mathematical object using different reference systems.

In linear algebra, coordinates mean expressing vectors as linear combinations of basis vectors. The standard basis gives us familiar numerical coordinates, but we can use any basis. Different bases give different coordinate representations of the same vector, just like different map projections give different coordinates for the same geographical point.

Why does this matter? Sometimes a problem is hard in one coordinate system but easy in another. Diagonalizing a matrix means finding coordinates where the transformation is simple. Principal component analysis finds coordinates that reveal data structure. Understanding coordinate systems unlocks the ability to choose the best perspective for any problem.

## Coordinates Relative to a Basis

Let $\mathcal{B} = \{\vec{b}_1, \vec{b}_2, \ldots, \vec{b}_n\}$ be a basis for vector space $V$.

For any $\vec{v} \in V$, there exist unique scalars $c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_n$ such that:

$$ \vec{v} = c_1\vec{b}_1 + c_2\vec{b}_2 + \cdots + c_n\vec{b}_n$$

The **coordinate vector** of $\vec{v}$ relative to basis $\mathcal{B}$ is:

$$ [\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \vdots \\ c_n \end{bmatrix}$$

**Note**: $[\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}}$ is always in $\mathbb{R}^n$, even if $V$ isn't $\mathbb{R}^n$ (e.g., polynomial spaces, matrix spaces).

### Example 1: Finding Coordinates

Let $ \mathcal{B} = \left\{\vec{b}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \vec{b}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$ be a basis for $\mathbb{R}^2$.

Find $[\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}}$ where $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

We need $c_1, c_2$ such that:
$$ c_1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + c_2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

This gives the system:
$$ \begin{cases} c_1 + c_2 = 3 \\ c_1 - c_2 = 1 \end{cases}$$

Adding: $2c_1 = 4$, so $c_1 = 2$

Subtracting: $2c_2 = 2$, so $c_2 = 1$

**Answer:** $ [\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

Verification: $ 2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ ✓

### Example 2: Coordinates in Polynomial Space

Let $\mathcal{B} = \{1, x, x^2\}$ be the standard basis for $P_2$.

Find $[p]_{\mathcal{B}}$ for $p(x) = 3 - 5x + 2x^2$.

**Solution:**

Express $p(x)$ as a linear combination of basis elements:
$$ p(x) = 3(1) + (-5)(x) + 2(x^2)$$

The coefficients give the coordinates:

**Answer:** $ [p]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -5 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 3: Non-Standard Polynomial Basis

Let $\mathcal{B} = \{1, 1+x, 1+x+x^2\}$ be a basis for $P_2$.

Find $[p]_{\mathcal{B}}$ for $p(x) = 6 + 3x + 2x^2$.

**Solution:**

We need $c_1, c_2, c_3$ such that:
$$ c_1(1) + c_2(1+x) + c_3(1+x+x^2) = 6 + 3x + 2x^2$$

Expand the left side:
$$ c_1 + c_2 + c_2x + c_3 + c_3x + c_3x^2 = 6 + 3x + 2x^2$$
$$ (c_1 + c_2 + c_3) + (c_2 + c_3)x + c_3x^2 = 6 + 3x + 2x^2$$

Match coefficients:
- $x^2$: $c_3 = 2$
- $x^1$: $c_2 + c_3 = 3$, so $c_2 = 1$
- $x^0$: $c_1 + c_2 + c_3 = 6$, so $c_1 = 3$

**Answer:** $ [p]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$

## The Change-of-Basis Matrix

Let $P_{\mathcal{B}} = [\vec{b}_1 \ \vec{b}_2 \ \cdots \ \vec{b}_n]$ be the matrix whose columns are the basis vectors of $\mathcal{B}$.

Then:
$$ \vec{v} = P_{\mathcal{B}}[\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}}$$

This converts $\mathcal{B}$-coordinates back to standard coordinates.

Conversely:
$$ [\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = P_{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}\vec{v}$$

This converts standard coordinates to $\mathcal{B}$-coordinates.

### Example 4: Using the Change-of-Basis Matrix

For basis $ \mathcal{B} = \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$, find $P_{\mathcal{B}}$ and use it to find $ \left[\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}\right]_{\mathcal{B}}$.

**Solution:**

$$ P_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

Find $P_{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}$:
$\det(P_{\mathcal{B}}) = 6 - 1 = 5$

$$ P_{\mathcal{B}}^{-1} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Compute coordinates:
$$ \left[\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}\right]_{\mathcal{B}} = P_{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 15 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ \left[\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}\right]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$

Verify: $ 2\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 3\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}$ ✓

### Example 5: Converting Between Coordinate Systems

Given $ [\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}$ where $ \mathcal{B} = \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$, find $\vec{v}$ in standard coordinates.

**Solution:**

$$ P_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \vec{v} = P_{\mathcal{B}}[\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 - 6 \\ 8 - 10 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}$ in standard coordinates.

## Change of Basis Between Non-Standard Bases

To convert from $\mathcal{B}$-coordinates to $\mathcal{C}$-coordinates:

$$ [\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{C}} = P_{\mathcal{C}}^{-1}P_{\mathcal{B}}[\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}}$$

The matrix $P_{\mathcal{C} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}} = P_{\mathcal{C}}^{-1}P_{\mathcal{B}}$ is called the **change-of-basis matrix** from $\mathcal{B}$ to $\mathcal{C}$.

### Example 6: Change of Basis in $\mathbb{R}^2$

Let $ \mathcal{B} = \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$ and $ \mathcal{C} = \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$ (standard basis).

Find the change-of-basis matrix from $\mathcal{B}$ to $\mathcal{C}$.

**Solution:**

$$ P_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad P_{\mathcal{C}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I$$

$$ P_{\mathcal{C} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}} = P_{\mathcal{C}}^{-1}P_{\mathcal{B}} = I \cdot P_{\mathcal{B}} = P_{\mathcal{B}}$$

Actually, we want $P_{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}$ since we're going from $\mathcal{B}$ to standard:

$\det(P_{\mathcal{B}}) = -1 - 1 = -2$

$$ P_{\mathcal{B}}^{-1} = \frac{1}{-2}\begin{bmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & -1/2 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ P_{\mathcal{C} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & -1/2 \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 7: Applying Change of Basis
Using Example 6, if $ [\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$, find $[\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{C}}$ (standard coordinates).
**Solution:**


$$[\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{C}} = P_{\mathcal{B}}[\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ [\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{C}} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$

## Isomorphism Between Vector Spaces

The coordinate mapping $\vec{v} \mapsto [\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}}$ is a one-to-one correspondence (isomorphism) between $V$ and $\mathbb{R}^n$ (when dim$(V) = n$).

This means:
- $[\vec{u} + \vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = [\vec{u}]_{\mathcal{B}} + [\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}}$
- $[c\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = c[\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}}$

**Consequence**: Every $n$-dimensional vector space "behaves like" $\mathbb{R}^n$ once we choose a basis.

### Example 8: Linearity of Coordinates

For basis $\mathcal{B} = \{1, x, x^2\}$ of $P_2$, verify that $[p + q]_{\mathcal{B}} = [p]_{\mathcal{B}} + [q]_{\mathcal{B}}$ for $p(x) = 1 + 2x$ and $q(x) = 3 - x + x^2$.

**Solution:**

$$ [p]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad [q]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ [p]_{\mathcal{B}} + [q]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Now: $(p + q)(x) = (1 + 2x) + (3 - x + x^2) = 4 + x + x^2$

$$ [p + q]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** Verified: $[p + q]_{\mathcal{B}} = [p]_{\mathcal{B}} + [q]_{\mathcal{B}}$ ✓

### Example 9: Matrix Coordinates

For standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}$:
$$ \mathcal{B} = \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$$

Find $[A]_{\mathcal{B}}$ for $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

$$ A = 3\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + (-2)\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + 1\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + 5\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ [A]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -2 \\ 1 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$

(This "vectorizes" the matrix.)

### Example 10: Application - Diagonalization Preview

If $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis of eigenvectors for matrix $A$, then the $\mathcal{B}$-coordinate representation of the linear transformation $\vec{x} \mapsto A\vec{x}$ is diagonal.

Given eigenvectors $ \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ (eigenvalue 5) and $ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$ (eigenvalue 3) for matrix $A$, what is the $\mathcal{B}$-coordinate matrix where $\mathcal{B} = \{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2\}$?

**Solution:**

In $\mathcal{B}$-coordinates:
$$ A\vec{v}_1 = 5\vec{v}_1 \Rightarrow [A\vec{v}_1]_{\mathcal{B}} = 5[\vec{v}_1]_{\mathcal{B}} = 5\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ A\vec{v}_2 = 3\vec{v}_2 \Rightarrow [A\vec{v}_2]_{\mathcal{B}} = 3[\vec{v}_2]_{\mathcal{B}} = 3\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** In $\mathcal{B}$-coordinates, $A$ is represented by $ D = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ (diagonal!).

This is why diagonalization is so powerful—it finds coordinates where transformations are simple.

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
$ \vec{v} = P_{\mathcal{B}}[\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3+2 \\ 0+2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$
Verify: $ 3\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + 2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ ✓

**Problem 2: B**
$p(x) = 2(1) + (-3)(x) + 5(x^2)$
Coordinates are the coefficients in order: $ [p]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$

**Problem 3: A**
$\vec{v} = P_{\mathcal{B}}[\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}}$ means $P_{\mathcal{B}}$ goes from $\mathcal{B}$-coordinates to standard.
$[\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = P_{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}\vec{v}$ means $P_{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}$ goes from standard to $\mathcal{B}$-coordinates.

**Problem 4: C**
The coordinate mapping is a linear transformation (isomorphism).
$[\vec{u} + \vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = [\vec{u}]_{\mathcal{B}} + [\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}}$ and $[c\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = c[\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}}$
Both operations are preserved.

**Problem 5: D**
Coordinate vectors relative to a basis in an $n$-dimensional space are always in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
If dim$(V) = 4$, then $[\vec{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} \in \mathbb{R}^4$ for any basis $\mathcal{B}$ of $V$.
