# Linear Algebra Topic 5.2: Diagonalization

Computing $A^{100}$ by multiplying a matrix by itself 100 times is computationally intractable. But if we could write $A = PDP^{-1}$ where $D$ is diagonal, then $A^{100} = PD^{100}P^{-1}$—and raising a diagonal matrix to a power just means raising each diagonal entry to that power. One matrix multiplication versus 100: that's the power of **diagonalization**.

Diagonalization transforms matrices into their simplest possible form while preserving their essential behavior. A diagonal matrix is easy to work with—powers, exponentials, and functions of diagonal matrices are trivial to compute. Not all matrices are diagonalizable, but those that are reveal deep structural properties through their eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

This topic connects eigenvalues and eigenvectors to practical computation. We'll learn which matrices can be diagonalized, how to construct the diagonalization explicitly, and how to exploit diagonal form for efficient computation. These techniques are fundamental in differential equations, quantum mechanics, and analyzing iterative processes.

## Definition of Diagonalization

An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is **diagonalizable** if there exists an invertible matrix $P$ and a diagonal matrix $D$ such that:
$$ A = PDP^{-1}$$

Equivalently: $P^{-1}AP = D$ (or "$A$ is similar to $D$").

**Key theorem**: Matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors.

**Construction**: If $A$ is diagonalizable:
- $P = [\vec{v}_1 \ \vec{v}_2 \ \cdots \ \vec{v}_n]$ (columns are eigenvectors)
- $D = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ldots, \lambda_n)$ (diagonal entries are corresponding eigenvalues)

### Example 1: Verifying Diagonalization

Verify that $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}^{-1}$.

**Solution:**

First find $P^{-1}$:
$$ P^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Compute $PDP^{-1}$:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & -2 \\ 5 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 5 & -2 \\ 5 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 12 & 3 \\ 6 & 9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** Verified ✓

### Example 2: Geometric Meaning

Explain why $A = PDP^{-1}$ means "change basis, apply diagonal transformation, change back."

**Solution:**

Think of $P$ as a change-of-basis matrix. The columns of $P$ are eigenvectors, forming a new basis.

For vector $\vec{x}$:
1. $P^{-1}\vec{x}$ converts to the eigenvector basis
2. $D$ scales each coordinate (diagonal action in eigenbasis)
3. $P$ converts back to standard basis

So $A = PDP^{-1}$ means: $A$ acts like a simple diagonal transformation when viewed in the eigenvector basis.

**Answer:** $A$ is diagonal in the eigenvector coordinate system.

## Diagonalizing a Matrix

**Procedure to diagonalize $A$**:
1. Find eigenvalues by solving $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$
2. For each eigenvalue, find eigenvectors by solving $(A - \lambda I)\vec{v} = \vec{0}$
3. Check: Do you have $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors? If not, $A$ is not diagonalizable.
4. Form $P$ with eigenvectors as columns
5. Form $D$ with corresponding eigenvalues on diagonal

### Example 3: Diagonalizing a $2 \times 2$ Matrix

Diagonalize $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

**Step 1: Eigenvalues**
$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = (1-\lambda)(4-\lambda) - 4 = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda = \lambda(\lambda - 5)$$

Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 0$, $\lambda_2 = 5$

**Step 2: Eigenvectors**

For $\lambda_1 = 0$:
$$ (A - 0I)\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Row reduce: $v_1 + 2v_2 = 0$, so $v_1 = -2v_2$

Eigenvector: $ \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

For $\lambda_2 = 5$:
$$ (A - 5I)\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -4 & 2 \\ 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Row reduce: $-4v_1 + 2v_2 = 0$, so $v_2 = 2v_1$

Eigenvector: $ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$

**Step 3: Form $P$ and $D$**

$$ P = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad D = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ A = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}^{-1}$

### Example 4: Diagonalizing a $3 \times 3$ Matrix

Diagonalize $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

**Step 1: Eigenvalues**

Upper triangular, so eigenvalues are diagonal entries: $\lambda_1 = 1$, $\lambda_2 = 2$ (multiplicity 2)

**Step 2: Eigenvectors**

For $\lambda_1 = 1$:
$$ (A - I)\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ v_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

From row 3: $v_3 = 0$
From row 2: $v_2 + v_3 = 0$, so $v_2 = 0$
Free variable: $v_1$

Eigenvector: $ \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$

For $\lambda_2 = 2$:
$$ (A - 2I)\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ v_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

From row 1: $v_1 = 0$
From row 2: $v_3 = 0$
Free variable: $v_2$

Only one independent eigenvector for $\lambda = 2$: $ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$

**Step 3: Check**

We have only 2 independent eigenvectors (need 3 for $3 \times 3$ matrix).

**Answer:** $A$ is NOT diagonalizable. The eigenvalue $\lambda = 2$ has geometric multiplicity 1 but algebraic multiplicity 2.

### Example 5: When Is a Matrix Diagonalizable?

State conditions under which an $n \times n$ matrix is guaranteed to be diagonalizable.

**Solution:**

**Sufficient conditions**:
1. $A$ has $n$ distinct eigenvalues
2. $A$ is symmetric (for real matrices)

**Necessary and sufficient condition**:
- For each eigenvalue $\lambda$: geometric multiplicity = algebraic multiplicity
- Equivalently: $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors

**Answer:** If all eigenvalues are distinct, $A$ is definitely diagonalizable. Repeated eigenvalues might work if they have enough independent eigenvectors.

## Powers of Matrices

If $A = PDP^{-1}$, then:
$$ A^k = (PDP^{-1})^k = PD^kP^{-1}$$

And for diagonal $D = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)$:
$$ D^k = \text{diag}(\lambda_1^k, \ldots, \lambda_n^k)$$

### Example 6: Computing High Powers

If $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}^{-1}$, find $A^{10}$.

**Solution:**

$$ A^{10} = PD^{10}P^{-1}$$

$$ D^{10} = \begin{bmatrix} 0^{10} & 0 \\ 0 & 5^{10} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 9765625 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ P^{-1} = \frac{1}{-5}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & -2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2/5 & 1/5 \\ 1/5 & 2/5 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ A^{10} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 9765625 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} -2/5 & 1/5 \\ 1/5 & 2/5 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 9765625 \\ 0 & 19531250 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} -2/5 & 1/5 \\ 1/5 & 2/5 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = \begin{bmatrix} 1953125 & 3906250 \\ 3906250 & 7812500 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ A^{10} = \begin{bmatrix} 1953125 & 3906250 \\ 3906250 & 7812500 \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 7: Pattern in Powers

If $A$ has eigenvalues 0.5 and 2, what happens to $A^k$ as $k \to \infty$?

**Solution:**

$A = PDP^{-1}$ where $D = \text{diag}(0.5, 2)$

$$ D^k = \text{diag}(0.5^k, 2^k)$$

As $k \to \infty$:
- $0.5^k \to 0$ (decays)
- $2^k \to \infty$ (grows)

**Answer:** $A^k$ will grow without bound (one eigenvalue > 1). The dominant eigenvalue determines long-term behavior.

## Similar Matrices

Matrices $A$ and $B$ are **similar** if there exists invertible $P$ such that:
$$ B = P^{-1}AP$$

**Key fact**: Similar matrices have the same eigenvalues (but different eigenvectors).

### Example 8: Similar Matrices Share Eigenvalues

If $B = P^{-1}AP$ and $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$, show $\lambda$ is also an eigenvalue of $B$.

**Solution:**

If $A\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v}$, then:
$$ B(P^{-1}\vec{v}) = P^{-1}AP(P^{-1}\vec{v}) = P^{-1}A\vec{v} = P^{-1}(\lambda\vec{v}) = \lambda(P^{-1}\vec{v})$$

So $P^{-1}\vec{v}$ is an eigenvector of $B$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$.

**Answer:** Similar matrices share eigenvalues ✓

### Example 9: Checking Similarity

Are $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and $ B = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ similar?

**Solution:**

$B = 3I$ is a scalar multiple of identity.

If $B = P^{-1}AP$, then $3I = P^{-1}AP$, giving $3P = AP$, so $A = 3I$.

But $A \neq 3I$, so they're not similar.

Alternatively: $A$ is not diagonalizable (only one independent eigenvector for $\lambda = 3$), but $B$ is already diagonal. Not similar.

**Answer:** Not similar.

## Symmetric Matrices

**Spectral Theorem**: Every symmetric matrix is diagonalizable with **orthogonal** eigenvectors.

For symmetric $A$:
- All eigenvalues are real
- Eigenvectors from different eigenvalues are orthogonal
- Can write $A = PDP^T$ where $P$ has orthonormal columns (so $P^{-1} = P^T$)

### Example 10: Symmetric Matrix

Find eigenvalues of $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ (symmetric).

**Solution:**

$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = (2-\lambda)^2 - 1 = \lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = (\lambda - 3)(\lambda - 1)$$

**Answer:** Eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 3$ and $\lambda_2 = 1$ (both real, as guaranteed for symmetric matrices).

### Example 11: Orthogonal Eigenvectors

For the matrix in Example 10, verify eigenvectors are orthogonal.

**Solution:**

For $\lambda_1 = 3$:
$$ (A - 3I)\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Eigenvector: $ \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

For $\lambda_2 = 1$:
$$ (A - I)\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Eigenvector: $ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$

Dot product: $\vec{v}_1 \cdot \vec{v}_2 = (1)(1) + (1)(-1) = 0$ ✓

**Answer:** Orthogonal, as expected for symmetric matrix.

### Example 12: Orthogonal Diagonalization

Orthogonally diagonalize $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 4$, $\lambda_2 = 2$ (can verify)

Eigenvectors (unnormalized):
- $\lambda_1 = 4$: $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
- $\lambda_2 = 2$: $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$

Normalize:
$$ \vec{u}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \vec{u}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ A = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$

(Note: $P^{-1} = P^T$ for orthonormal columns)

### Example 13: Non-Diagonalizable Example

Why isn't $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ diagonalizable?

**Solution:**

Eigenvalues: $\det(A - \lambda I) = \lambda^2 = 0$, so $\lambda = 0$ (multiplicity 2)

Eigenvectors for $\lambda = 0$:
$$ A\vec{v} = \vec{0} \implies \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

This gives $v_2 = 0$, so eigenvectors are $ \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

Only one independent eigenvector: $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$

**Answer:** Not diagonalizable. Algebraic multiplicity (2) $>$ geometric multiplicity (1).

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
$(PDP^{-1})^5 = (PDP^{-1})(PDP^{-1})(PDP^{-1})(PDP^{-1})(PDP^{-1})$
$= PD(P^{-1}P)D(P^{-1}P)D(P^{-1}P)D(P^{-1}P)DP^{-1}$
$= PDDDDDP^{-1} = PD^5P^{-1}$

**Problem 2: C**
Repeated eigenvalue (3 with multiplicity 2) might or might not have 2 independent eigenvectors.
Need to check if geometric multiplicity = algebraic multiplicity for eigenvalue 3.
If yes, diagonalizable; if no, not diagonalizable.

**Problem 3: C**
Dominant eigenvalue is 1.2 > 1
$A^k = PD^kP^{-1}$ where $D^k = \text{diag}(0.5^k, 0.8^k, 1.2^k)$
As $k \to \infty$: $1.2^k \to \infty$, so $A^k$ grows without bound.

**Problem 4: C**
$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$
Eigenvalues: $\det\begin{bmatrix} 1-\lambda & 1 \\ 0 & 1-\lambda \end{bmatrix} = (1-\lambda)^2 = 0$
So $\lambda = 1$ (multiplicity 2)
Eigenvectors: $(A-I)\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\vec{v} = \vec{0}$ gives only $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$
Only 1 independent eigenvector for $2 \times 2$ matrix → not diagonalizable

**Problem 5: B**
Spectral theorem: symmetric matrices have orthogonal eigenvectors from different eigenspaces.
