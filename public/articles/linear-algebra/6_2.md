# Linear Algebra Topic 6.2: Orthogonal Projections

When you cast a shadow, you're projecting a three-dimensional object onto a two-dimensional plane. Mathematically, **projection** means finding the closest point in a subspace to a given vector. This "closest point" problem appears everywhere: fitting lines to data, solving inconsistent systems, compressing signals, and decomposing vectors into components.

The power of orthogonal projection lies in a simple geometric fact: the shortest path from a point to a plane is perpendicular to that plane. This means the projection $\hat{\vec{y}}$ of $\vec{y}$ onto subspace $W$ satisfies $\vec{y} - \hat{\vec{y}} \perp W$. This orthogonality condition gives us a formula: when $W$ has orthogonal basis $\{\vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_p\}$, we simply add up projections onto each basis vector.

Projections decompose vectors into "signal" and "noise," or "trend" and "fluctuation." In least squares regression, we project data onto the column space of a design matrix. In Fourier analysis, we project functions onto spaces of trigonometric polynomials. Understanding projection formulas unlocks these applications and makes abstract orthogonality computationally useful.

## Projection onto a Line

For nonzero vector $\vec{u}$, the **orthogonal projection** of $\vec{y}$ onto $\text{Span}\{\vec{u}\}$ is:
$$ \hat{\vec{y}} = \text{proj}_{\vec{u}}(\vec{y}) = \frac{\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}}{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{u}}\vec{u}$$

**Alternative formula** when $\vec{u}$ is a unit vector:
$$ \hat{\vec{y}} = (\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u})\vec{u}$$

The **component orthogonal to $\vec{u}$** is:
$$ \vec{z} = \vec{y} - \hat{\vec{y}}$$

This satisfies $\vec{z} \perp \vec{u}$.

### Example 1: Basic Projection

Find the projection of $ \vec{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$ onto $ \vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

$$ \vec{y} \cdot \vec{u} = (7)(4) + (6)(2) = 28 + 12 = 40$$

$$ \vec{u} \cdot \vec{u} = 4^2 + 2^2 = 16 + 4 = 20$$

$$ \hat{\vec{y}} = \frac{40}{20}\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = 2\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ \text{proj}_{\vec{u}}(\vec{y}) = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 2: Finding Perpendicular Component

For Example 1, find the component of $\vec{y}$ orthogonal to $\vec{u}$.

**Solution:**

$$ \vec{z} = \vec{y} - \hat{\vec{y}} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Verify orthogonality: $ \vec{z} \cdot \vec{u} = (-1)(4) + (2)(2) = -4 + 4 = 0$ ✓

**Answer:** $ \vec{z} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$

Also verify: $\vec{y} = \hat{\vec{y}} + \vec{z} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$ ✓

### Example 3: Geometric Interpretation

Why is $\hat{\vec{y}}$ the closest point in $\text{Span}\{\vec{u}\}$ to $\vec{y}$?

**Solution:**

Any point in $\text{Span}\{\vec{u}\}$ has form $c\vec{u}$.

Distance from $\vec{y}$ to $c\vec{u}$: $\|\vec{y} - c\vec{u}\|$

This is minimized when $\vec{y} - c\vec{u} \perp \vec{u}$:
$$ (\vec{y} - c\vec{u}) \cdot \vec{u} = 0$$
$$ \vec{y} \cdot \vec{u} - c(\vec{u} \cdot \vec{u}) = 0$$
$$ c = \frac{\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}}{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{u}}$$

This is exactly our projection formula!

**Answer:** The projection minimizes distance because the error vector $\vec{y} - \hat{\vec{y}}$ is perpendicular to the subspace.

## Projection onto a Subspace

For subspace $W$ with orthogonal basis $\{\vec{u}_1, \vec{u}_2, \ldots, \vec{u}_p\}$:
$$ \hat{\vec{y}} = \text{proj}_W(\vec{y}) = \frac{\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_1}{\vec{u}_1 \cdot \vec{u}_1}\vec{u}_1 + \frac{\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_2}{\vec{u}_2 \cdot \vec{u}_2}\vec{u}_2 + \cdots + \frac{\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_p}{\vec{u}_p \cdot \vec{u}_p}\vec{u}_p$$

**Key point**: Add up individual projections onto each orthogonal basis vector.

If the basis is **orthonormal** ($\|\vec{u}_i\| = 1$ for all $i$):
$$ \hat{\vec{y}} = (\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_1)\vec{u}_1 + (\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_2)\vec{u}_2 + \cdots + (\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_p)\vec{u}_p$$

### Example 4: Projection onto a Plane

Let $ W = \text{Span}\left\{\vec{u}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \vec{u}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$ (the $xy$-plane).

Find projection of $ \vec{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$ onto $W$.

**Solution:**

The basis is orthonormal, so:

$$ \hat{\vec{y}} = (\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_1)\vec{u}_1 + (\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_2)\vec{u}_2$$

$$ = 3\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + 4\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ \hat{\vec{y}} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ (just zeros out the $z$-component)

### Example 5: Using Orthogonal Basis

Find projection of $ \vec{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ onto $ W = \text{Span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$.

**Solution:**

Check orthogonality: $(1)(1) + (1)(-1) + (0)(2) = 0$ ✓

$$ \hat{\vec{y}} = \frac{\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_1}{\vec{u}_1 \cdot \vec{u}_1}\vec{u}_1 + \frac{\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_2}{\vec{u}_2 \cdot \vec{u}_2}\vec{u}_2$$

$$ \vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_1 = 6 + 3 + 0 = 9, \quad \vec{u}_1 \cdot \vec{u}_1 = 1 + 1 + 0 = 2$$

$$ \vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_2 = 6 - 3 + 4 = 7, \quad \vec{u}_2 \cdot \vec{u}_2 = 1 + 1 + 4 = 6$$

$$ \hat{\vec{y}} = \frac{9}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \frac{7}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = \begin{bmatrix} 9/2 \\ 9/2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 7/6 \\ -7/6 \\ 14/6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 27/6 + 7/6 \\ 27/6 - 7/6 \\ 14/6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 34/6 \\ 20/6 \\ 7/3 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ \hat{\vec{y}} = \begin{bmatrix} 17/3 \\ 10/3 \\ 7/3 \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 6: Projection Matrix

For $W = \text{Span}\{\vec{u}\}$ where $\vec{u}$ is unit vector, the projection can be written as:
$$ \hat{\vec{y}} = (\vec{u}\vec{u}^T)\vec{y}$$

The matrix $P = \vec{u}\vec{u}^T$ is the **projection matrix** onto $\text{Span}\{\vec{u}\}$.

Find the projection matrix onto $ \text{Span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}\right\}$.

**Solution:**

$$ P = \vec{u}\vec{u}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & 1/2 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ P = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & 1/2 \end{bmatrix}$

Verify: $ P\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (x+y)/2 \\ (x+y)/2 \end{bmatrix}$ (projects onto line $y = x$) ✓

## Properties of Projections

**Key properties**:
1. $\hat{\vec{y}} \in W$ (projection is in the subspace)
2. $\vec{y} - \hat{\vec{y}} \perp W$ (error is orthogonal to subspace)
3. $\hat{\vec{y}}$ is the unique closest point in $W$ to $\vec{y}$
4. If $\vec{y} \in W$, then $\hat{\vec{y}} = \vec{y}$ (projecting onto subspace you're already in gives yourself)
5. For projection matrix $P$: $P^2 = P$ (projecting twice = projecting once)

### Example 7: Idempotent Property

Verify that $ P = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & 1/2 \end{bmatrix}$ satisfies $P^2 = P$.

**Solution:**

$$ P^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & 1/2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & 1/2 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = \begin{bmatrix} 1/4 + 1/4 & 1/4 + 1/4 \\ 1/4 + 1/4 & 1/4 + 1/4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & 1/2 \end{bmatrix} = P$$

**Answer:** Verified $P^2 = P$ ✓

**Interpretation**: Projecting an already-projected vector doesn't change it.

### Example 8: Distance to Subspace

The **distance from $\vec{y}$ to subspace $W$** is:
$$ \text{dist}(\vec{y}, W) = \|\vec{y} - \hat{\vec{y}}\|$$

Find distance from $ \vec{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$ to the $xy$-plane.

**Solution:**

Projection onto $xy$-plane: $ \hat{\vec{y}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$

$$ \vec{y} - \hat{\vec{y}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \text{dist}(\vec{y}, W) = \left\|\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\right\| = 3$$

**Answer:** Distance = 3 (just the $z$-coordinate)

## The Best Approximation Theorem

**Theorem**: Let $W$ be a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$, and let $\vec{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Then $\hat{\vec{y}} = \text{proj}_W(\vec{y})$ is the unique vector in $W$ such that:
$$ \|\vec{y} - \hat{\vec{y}}\| < \|\vec{y} - \vec{v}\|$$
for all $\vec{v} \in W$ with $\vec{v} \neq \hat{\vec{y}}$.

**In other words**: The projection is the best approximation to $\vec{y}$ from $W$.

### Example 9: Best Approximation

Among all vectors in $ W = \text{Span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$ ($xy$-plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$), which is closest to $ \vec{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$?

**Solution:**

The closest point is the projection:
$$ \hat{\vec{y}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Distance: $ \|\vec{y} - \hat{\vec{y}}\| = \left\|\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}\right\| = 5$$

Any other point $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \in W$ has distance:
$$ \|\vec{y} - \vec{v}\| = \sqrt{(2-a)^2 + (3-b)^2 + 25} \geq 5$$

with equality iff $a = 2, b = 3$.

**Answer:** $ \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ is the unique closest point.

## Orthogonal Decomposition

**Orthogonal Decomposition Theorem**: Let $W$ be a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Then each $\vec{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ can be written uniquely as:
$$ \vec{y} = \hat{\vec{y}} + \vec{z}$$

where $\hat{\vec{y}} \in W$ and $\vec{z} \in W^{\perp}$.

This decomposition: $\mathbb{R}^n = W \oplus W^{\perp}$ (direct sum).

### Example 10: Computing Orthogonal Decomposition

Decompose $ \vec{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 8 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ into components in $W$ and $W^{\perp}$ where $ W = \text{Span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$.

**Solution:**

$$ \vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \hat{\vec{y}} = \frac{\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}}{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{u}}\vec{u} = \frac{4 + 16 + 2}{1 + 4 + 4}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{22}{9}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 22/9 \\ 44/9 \\ 44/9 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \vec{z} = \vec{y} - \hat{\vec{y}} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 8 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 22/9 \\ 44/9 \\ 44/9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 14/9 \\ 28/9 \\ -35/9 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ \vec{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 22/9 \\ 44/9 \\ 44/9 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 14/9 \\ 28/9 \\ -35/9 \end{bmatrix}$ where first term $\in W$, second $\in W^{\perp}$.

### Example 11: Projection onto Column Space

For matrix $A$ with orthonormal columns $\{\vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_p\}$:
$$ \text{proj}_{\text{Col}(A)}(\vec{y}) = (AA^T)\vec{y}$$

where $A = [\vec{u}_1 \ \cdots \ \vec{u}_p]$.

Find projection of $\vec{y}$ onto column space of $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

Columns are orthonormal: $\vec{u}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $\vec{u}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$

$$ AA^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** Projection matrix $ P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ (projects onto $xy$-plane)

### Example 12: Application to Least Squares

The normal equation $A^TA\hat{\vec{x}} = A^T\vec{b}$ finds the vector $\hat{\vec{x}}$ that makes $A\hat{\vec{x}}$ the projection of $\vec{b}$ onto $\text{Col}(A)$.

This minimizes $\|\vec{b} - A\vec{x}\|^2$ (least squares solution).

**Preview**: If columns of $A$ are orthogonal, $A^TA$ is diagonal, making the system easy to solve!

### Example 13: Projection in Function Spaces

The inner product on $C[0, 1]$ is $ \langle f, g \rangle = \int_0^1 f(x)g(x)\,dx$.

To project $f(x) = x$ onto $W = \text{Span}\{1\}$:
$$ \hat{f} = \frac{\langle f, 1 \rangle}{\langle 1, 1 \rangle} \cdot 1 = \frac{\int_0^1 x\,dx}{\int_0^1 1\,dx} \cdot 1 = \frac{1/2}{1} = \frac{1}{2}$$

**Answer:** The constant function $\hat{f}(x) = 1/2$ is the projection (it's the average value).

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
$\vec{y} \cdot \vec{u} = 18 + 32 = 50$
$\vec{u} \cdot \vec{u} = 9 + 16 = 25$
$ \hat{\vec{y}} = \frac{50}{25}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = 2\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix}$
$\vec{y}$ is already a scalar multiple of $\vec{u}$, so projection = itself.

**Problem 2: B**
The error vector $\vec{y} - \hat{\vec{y}}$ is always orthogonal to the subspace $W$.
This is the defining property of projection.

**Problem 3: B**
Projection matrices are idempotent: $P^2 = P$
Projecting twice = projecting once.
(A) False: projection matrices usually aren't invertible
(C) False: projection matrices are symmetric: $P^T = P$
(D) False: $\det(P) = 0$ if projecting onto proper subspace

**Problem 4: A**
Projection onto $xy$-plane: $ \hat{\vec{y}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$
Distance $ = \left\|\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\| = 1$

**Problem 5: B**
If $\vec{y}$ is already in $W$, its projection onto $W$ is itself.
Projecting onto a subspace you're already in gives you back.
