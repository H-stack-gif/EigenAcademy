# Linear Algebra Topic 7.2: Quadratic Forms

Quadratic expressions appear everywhere: kinetic energy $\frac{1}{2}m(v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2)$, least squares error $\sum(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$, variance $\sigma^2 = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$. A **quadratic form** generalizes this to $Q(\vec{x}) = ax_1^2 + bx_1x_2 + cx_2^2$, which we can write as $Q(\vec{x}) = \vec{x}^TA\vec{x}$ for symmetric matrix $A$.

Why does this matter? Quadratic forms determine the shape of optimization landscapes. Is a critical point a minimum (bowl-shaped), maximum (hill-shaped), or saddle (mountain pass)? The eigenvalues of the associated matrix $A$ answer this: all positive means minimum, all negative means maximum, mixed signs mean saddle point.

Understanding quadratic forms means understanding curvature, stability, and optimization. When you fit a least squares regression, you're minimizing a quadratic form. When a bridge oscillates, the energy is a quadratic form in displacement coordinates. The **principal axes theorem** shows that every quadratic form becomes sum of squares $\lambda_1 y_1^2 + \cdots + \lambda_n y_n^2$ in the right coordinate system—revealing its intrinsic geometry.

## Definition of Quadratic Forms

A **quadratic form** on $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a function:
$$ Q(\vec{x}) = \vec{x}^TA\vec{x}$$

where $A$ is a symmetric $n \times n$ matrix.

**Expanded form** (for $n = 2$):
$$ Q(x_1, x_2) = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & x_2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = ax_1^2 + 2bx_1x_2 + cx_2^2$$

**Key observation**: The matrix $A$ must be symmetric (we can always make it so by setting $a_{ij} = a_{ji} = (a_{ij} + a_{ji})/2$).

### Example 1: Computing Quadratic Form

For $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$, find $Q(\vec{x})$ in expanded form.

**Solution:**

$$ Q(\vec{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & x_2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & x_2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2x_1 - x_2 \\ -x_1 + 3x_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = x_1(2x_1 - x_2) + x_2(-x_1 + 3x_2)$$

$$ = 2x_1^2 - x_1x_2 - x_1x_2 + 3x_2^2$$

$$ = 2x_1^2 - 2x_1x_2 + 3x_2^2$$

**Answer:** $Q(x_1, x_2) = 2x_1^2 - 2x_1x_2 + 3x_2^2$

### Example 2: Matrix from Quadratic Form

Find the matrix $A$ for $Q(x_1, x_2) = 3x_1^2 + 4x_1x_2 + 5x_2^2$.

**Solution:**

Match with $ax_1^2 + 2bx_1x_2 + cx_2^2$:
- Coefficient of $x_1^2$: $a = 3$
- Coefficient of $x_2^2$: $c = 5$
- Coefficient of $x_1x_2$: $2b = 4$, so $b = 2$

**Answer:** $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 3: Evaluating Quadratic Form

For $Q(x_1, x_2) = 2x_1^2 - 2x_1x_2 + 3x_2^2$, evaluate $Q(1, 2)$.

**Solution:**

$$ Q(1, 2) = 2(1)^2 - 2(1)(2) + 3(2)^2$$

$$ = 2 - 4 + 12 = 10$$

Alternatively using matrix form:
$$ Q\begin{pmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = 10$$

**Answer:** $Q(1, 2) = 10$

## Classification of Quadratic Forms

Based on the sign of $Q(\vec{x})$ for $\vec{x} \neq \vec{0}$:

- **Positive definite**: $Q(\vec{x}) > 0$ for all $\vec{x} \neq \vec{0}$
- **Positive semidefinite**: $Q(\vec{x}) \geq 0$ for all $\vec{x} \neq \vec{0}$
- **Negative definite**: $Q(\vec{x}) < 0$ for all $\vec{x} \neq \vec{0}$
- **Negative semidefinite**: $Q(\vec{x}) \leq 0$ for all $\vec{x} \neq \vec{0}$
- **Indefinite**: $Q$ takes both positive and negative values

### Example 4: Positive Definite Form

Show that $Q(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + x_2^2$ is positive definite.

**Solution:**

For any $ \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} \neq \vec{0}$:

$$ Q(\vec{x}) = x_1^2 + x_2^2 \geq 0$$

with equality only when $x_1 = x_2 = 0$.

So for $\vec{x} \neq \vec{0}$, at least one of $x_1, x_2$ is nonzero, making $Q(\vec{x}) > 0$.

**Answer:** Positive definite (represents squared distance from origin).

Matrix: $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I$

### Example 5: Indefinite Form

Classify $Q(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 - x_2^2$.

**Solution:**

Test vectors:
- At $\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$: $Q = 1 > 0$
- At $\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$: $Q = -1 < 0$

**Answer:** Indefinite (takes both positive and negative values).

This is a **saddle** shape (hyperbolic paraboloid).

## Eigenvalues and Classification

**Theorem**: For quadratic form $Q(\vec{x}) = \vec{x}^TA\vec{x}$ with symmetric $A$:

- **Positive definite** iff all eigenvalues of $A$ are positive
- **Positive semidefinite** iff all eigenvalues are nonnegative
- **Negative definite** iff all eigenvalues are negative
- **Negative semidefinite** iff all eigenvalues are nonpositive
- **Indefinite** iff $A$ has both positive and negative eigenvalues

### Example 6: Classification via Eigenvalues

Classify $Q$ for $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

Find eigenvalues:
$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = (2-\lambda)(3-\lambda) - 1 = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 5 = 0$$

$$ \lambda = \frac{5 \pm \sqrt{25 - 20}}{2} = \frac{5 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2}$$

$$ \lambda_1 = \frac{5 + \sqrt{5}}{2} \approx 3.62 > 0$$

$$ \lambda_2 = \frac{5 - \sqrt{5}}{2} \approx 1.38 > 0$$

Both eigenvalues positive.

**Answer:** Positive definite (bowl-shaped graph, minimum at origin).

### Example 7: Saddle Point

Classify $Q$ for $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

Diagonal matrix has eigenvalues on diagonal: $\lambda_1 = 1 > 0$, $\lambda_2 = -1 < 0$.

Mixed signs.

**Answer:** Indefinite (saddle point).

$Q(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 - x_2^2$ (hyperbolic paraboloid).

### Example 8: Semidefinite Example

Classify $Q$ for $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = (1-\lambda)^2 - 1 = \lambda^2 - 2\lambda = \lambda(\lambda - 2) = 0$$

Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 2 > 0$, $\lambda_2 = 0$

One zero eigenvalue.

**Answer:** Positive semidefinite.

$Q(\vec{x}) \geq 0$ for all $\vec{x}$, with $Q(\vec{x}) = 0$ along the eigenspace for $\lambda = 0$.

## Principal Axes Theorem

**Theorem**: For quadratic form $Q(\vec{x}) = \vec{x}^TA\vec{x}$ with symmetric $A$:

There exists orthogonal change of variables $\vec{x} = P\vec{y}$ (where $P$ has orthonormal eigenvectors) such that:
$$ Q(\vec{x}) = \lambda_1 y_1^2 + \lambda_2 y_2^2 + \cdots + \lambda_n y_n^2$$

**No cross terms!** Just sum of squares with eigenvalues as coefficients.

The axes defined by eigenvectors are the **principal axes**.

### Example 9: Principal Axes

Find principal axes form for $Q(x_1, x_2) = 2x_1^2 - 2x_1x_2 + 3x_2^2$.

**Solution:**

Matrix: $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$

From Example 6: eigenvalues $ \lambda_1 = \frac{5 + \sqrt{5}}{2}$, $ \lambda_2 = \frac{5 - \sqrt{5}}{2}$

Find eigenvectors (calculation omitted): normalize to get orthonormal set, form $P$.

After change of variables $\vec{x} = P\vec{y}$:
$$ Q = \frac{5 + \sqrt{5}}{2}y_1^2 + \frac{5 - \sqrt{5}}{2}y_2^2$$

**Answer:** Principal axes form eliminates cross term $x_1x_2$.

### Example 10: Geometric Meaning

For $Q(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + 4x_2^2$, describe level curves $Q = c$.

**Solution:**

Level curve: $x_1^2 + 4x_2^2 = c$

Divide by $c$: $ \frac{x_1^2}{c} + \frac{x_2^2}{c/4} = 1$

This is an ellipse with semi-axes $\sqrt{c}$ (horizontal) and $\sqrt{c/4} = \sqrt{c}/2$ (vertical).

Matrix: $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$

Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 1$, $\lambda_2 = 4$

**Answer:** Level curves are ellipses aligned with coordinate axes. Larger eigenvalue (4) corresponds to direction of greater curvature ($x_2$-direction).

## Applications to Optimization

At critical point $\vec{x}_0$ where $\nabla f = \vec{0}$, the **Hessian matrix** $H$ of second partial derivatives determines the nature of the critical point:

$$ H = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1^2} & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1 \partial x_2} & \cdots \\ \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_2 \partial x_1} & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_2^2} & \cdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{bmatrix}$$

- If $H$ is positive definite → local minimum
- If $H$ is negative definite → local maximum
- If $H$ is indefinite → saddle point

### Example 11: Second Derivative Test

For $f(x, y) = x^2 + 2xy + 3y^2$, classify the critical point at origin.

**Solution:**

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x + 2y, \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 2x + 6y$$

Critical point: $2x + 2y = 0$ and $2x + 6y = 0$ gives $(0, 0)$ ✓

Hessian:
$$ H = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$$

Eigenvalues: $\det(H - \lambda I) = (2-\lambda)(6-\lambda) - 4 = \lambda^2 - 8\lambda + 8 = 0$

$$ \lambda = \frac{8 \pm \sqrt{64-32}}{2} = \frac{8 \pm \sqrt{32}}{2} = 4 \pm 2\sqrt{2}$$

Both eigenvalues positive (since $4 - 2\sqrt{2} \approx 1.17 > 0$).

**Answer:** Local minimum at origin.

### Example 12: Saddle Point

For $f(x, y) = x^2 - y^2$, classify the origin.

**Solution:**

$$ \nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} 2x \\ -2y \end{bmatrix} = \vec{0}$$ at origin.

Hessian:
$$ H = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 2 > 0$, $\lambda_2 = -2 < 0$ (mixed signs).

**Answer:** Saddle point at origin (indefinite Hessian).

## Completing the Square

An alternative to diagonalization: **complete the square** to eliminate cross terms.

### Example 13: Completing the Square

Write $Q(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + 4x_1x_2 + 3x_2^2$ in principal axes form by completing the square.

**Solution:**

Group terms with $x_1$:
$$ Q = x_1^2 + 4x_1x_2 + 3x_2^2$$

$$ = (x_1^2 + 4x_1x_2 + 4x_2^2) - 4x_2^2 + 3x_2^2$$

$$ = (x_1 + 2x_2)^2 - x_2^2$$

Let $y_1 = x_1 + 2x_2$ and $y_2 = x_2$:

$$ Q = y_1^2 - y_2^2$$

**Answer:** $Q = y_1^2 - y_2^2$ (indefinite, eigenvalues 1 and -1).

Note: The change of variables $\begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}$ is not orthogonal, but achieves diagonal form.

## Practice Problems

**Problem 1:** The quadratic form $Q(\vec{x}) = \vec{x}^TA\vec{x}$ requires:

A) $A$ to be diagonal
B) $A$ to be symmetric
C) $A$ to be invertible
D) $A$ to have positive determinant
E) $A$ to be orthogonal

**Problem 2:** If all eigenvalues of $A$ are positive, then $Q(\vec{x}) = \vec{x}^TA\vec{x}$ is:

A) Indefinite
B) Negative definite
C) Positive semidefinite
D) Positive definite
E) Zero

**Problem 3:** The matrix for $Q(x_1, x_2) = 2x_1^2 + 6x_1x_2 + x_2^2$ is:

A) $ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 6 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$

B) $ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$

C) $ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$

D) $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$

E) $ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$

**Problem 4:** Principal axes theorem says every quadratic form can be written as:

A) Product of linear forms
B) Sum of squares with no cross terms
C) Difference of matrices
D) Ratio of polynomials
E) Determinant of a matrix

**Problem 5:** If Hessian matrix at critical point has eigenvalues 3, 5, -1, the point is:

A) Local minimum
B) Local maximum
C) Saddle point
D) Global minimum
E) Inflection point

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
Quadratic form matrix must be symmetric.
Can always make symmetric by $a_{ij} = (a_{ij} + a_{ji})/2$.
Doesn't need to be diagonal, invertible, or orthogonal.

**Problem 2: D**
All eigenvalues positive → positive definite.
$Q(\vec{x}) > 0$ for all $\vec{x} \neq \vec{0}$.
(Positive semidefinite allows zero eigenvalues)

**Problem 3: B**
$Q = 2x_1^2 + 6x_1x_2 + x_2^2 = ax_1^2 + 2bx_1x_2 + cx_2^2$
$a = 2$, $2b = 6$ so $b = 3$, $c = 1$
$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$

**Problem 4: B**
Principal axes: $Q = \lambda_1 y_1^2 + \lambda_2 y_2^2 + \cdots + \lambda_n y_n^2$
Sum of squares, no cross terms like $y_iy_j$.
Eigenvalues are coefficients.

**Problem 5: C**
Mixed eigenvalue signs (positive and negative) → indefinite → saddle point.
All positive → minimum.
All negative → maximum.
