# Linear Algebra Topic 1.4: Matrix Inverses

Division isn't defined for matrices. If you want to solve $ax = b$ for real numbers, you divide: $x = b/a$. But what's $\vec{x}$ in the matrix equation $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$? We can't "divide by $A$."

Instead, we use the **matrix inverse**. When $A$ has an inverse $A^{-1}$, we can multiply both sides: $A^{-1}A\vec{x} = A^{-1}\vec{b}$, giving $\vec{x} = A^{-1}\vec{b}$. The inverse "undoes" what $A$ does, just like reciprocals undo multiplication for numbers.

But not every matrix has an inverse—just as not every number has a reciprocal (zero doesn't). Understanding which matrices are invertible and how to compute inverses is fundamental. Invertibility connects to uniqueness of solutions, linear independence, determinants, and the geometry of linear transformations. In this topic, we'll develop both computational techniques and theoretical understanding of this crucial concept.

## Definition of Matrix Inverse

A square $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is **invertible** (or **nonsingular**) if there exists a matrix $A^{-1}$ such that:

$$ AA^{-1} = I \quad \text{and} \quad A^{-1}A = I$$

where $I$ is the $n \times n$ identity matrix.

If $A$ is not invertible, we call it **singular**.

**Key facts:**
- Only square matrices can have inverses
- If $A^{-1}$ exists, it's unique
- $(A^{-1})^{-1} = A$ (inverse of inverse gives back original)
- If $A$ and $B$ are invertible, then $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ (note the reversal!)

### Example 1: Verifying an Inverse

Verify that $ B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -5 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ is the inverse of $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

Compute $AB$:
$$ AB = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -5 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I$$

Compute $BA$:
$$ BA = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -5 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I$$

Both products give $I$.

**Answer:** Yes, $B = A^{-1}$.

## Inverse of a $2 \times 2$ Matrix

For a $2 \times 2$ matrix, there's a formula:

If $ A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$, then:

$$ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}$$

provided $ad - bc \neq 0$.

The quantity $ad - bc$ is the **determinant** of $A$, written $\det(A)$ or $|A|$.

**Rule**: $A$ is invertible if and only if $\det(A) \neq 0$.

### Example 2: Computing $2 \times 2$ Inverse

Find the inverse of $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 7 \\ 2 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

$\det(A) = (4)(6) - (7)(2) = 24 - 14 = 10 \neq 0$

So $A$ is invertible:
$$ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{10}\begin{bmatrix} 6 & -7 \\ -2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 & -0.7 \\ -0.2 & 0.4 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{3}{5} & -\frac{7}{10} \\ -\frac{1}{5} & \frac{2}{5} \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 3: Singular Matrix

Determine if $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ is invertible.

**Solution:**

$\det(A) = (2)(2) - (4)(1) = 4 - 4 = 0$

Since $\det(A) = 0$, $A$ is singular (not invertible).

**Answer:** Not invertible. (Notice the second row is half the first row—the rows are linearly dependent.)

## Computing Inverses by Row Reduction

For larger matrices, we use row reduction. The algorithm:

**To find $A^{-1}$:**
1. Form the augmented matrix $[A | I]$
2. Row reduce to $[I | B]$ (if possible)
3. Then $B = A^{-1}$

If row reduction produces a row of zeros on the left side, then $A$ is singular (no inverse).

**Why this works**: Elementary row operations on $[A | I]$ perform the same operations to both parts. If we can transform $A$ into $I$, we're effectively multiplying $A$ by its inverse. Those same operations applied to $I$ give us $A^{-1}$.

### Example 4: Finding Inverse by Row Reduction

Find the inverse of $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \\ 5 & 6 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

Form $[A | I]$:
$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 5 & 6 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right]$$

$R_3 - 5R_1 \to R_3$:
$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -4 & -15 & -5 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right]$$

$R_3 + 4R_2 \to R_3$:
$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -5 & 4 & 1 \end{array}\right]$$

Back-substitute to get RREF:

$R_2 - 4R_3 \to R_2$:
$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 20 & -15 & -4 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -5 & 4 & 1 \end{array}\right]$$

$R_1 - 3R_3 \to R_1$:
$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 0 & 16 & -12 & -3 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 20 & -15 & -4 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -5 & 4 & 1 \end{array}\right]$$

$R_1 - 2R_2 \to R_1$:
$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 0 & 0 & -24 & 18 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 20 & -15 & -4 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -5 & 4 & 1 \end{array}\right]$$

**Answer:** $ A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} -24 & 18 & 5 \\ 20 & -15 & -4 \\ -5 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 5: Detecting Singular Matrices

Attempt to find the inverse of $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

Form $[A | I]$:
$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right]$$

$R_2 - 2R_1 \to R_2$ and $R_3 - R_1 \to R_3$:
$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 & -1 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right]$$

Row 2 is all zeros in the left part.

**Answer:** $A$ is singular (not invertible). The second row is twice the first row.

## Solving Systems with Inverses

If $A$ is invertible, the equation $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ has the unique solution:

$$ \vec{x} = A^{-1}\vec{b}$$

**Proof**: Multiply both sides by $A^{-1}$ on the left:
$$ A^{-1}(A\vec{x}) = A^{-1}\vec{b}$$
$$ (A^{-1}A)\vec{x} = A^{-1}\vec{b}$$
$$ I\vec{x} = A^{-1}\vec{b}$$
$$ \vec{x} = A^{-1}\vec{b}$$

### Example 6: Solving with Matrix Inverse

Solve $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ where $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $ \vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 12 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Given:** From Example 1, $ A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -5 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$

**Solution:**

$$ \vec{x} = A^{-1}\vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -5 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 12 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 14 - 12 \\ -35 + 36 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

Verify: $ \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 12 \end{bmatrix}$ ✓

### Example 7: Multiple Right-Hand Sides

Solve both $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}_1$ and $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}_2$ where:
$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \vec{b}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \vec{b}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Solution:**

First find $A^{-1}$:
$\det(A) = 2 - 1 = 1$

$$ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{1}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

For $\vec{b}_1$:
$$ \vec{x}_1 = A^{-1}\vec{b}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

For $\vec{b}_2$:
$$ \vec{x}_2 = A^{-1}\vec{b}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ \vec{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $ \vec{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$

## Properties of Invertible Matrices

If $A$ and $B$ are invertible $n \times n$ matrices:

1. $(A^{-1})^{-1} = A$
2. $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ (reversal rule)
3. $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T$ (written $A^{-T}$)
4. $(cA)^{-1} = \frac{1}{c}A^{-1}$ for $c \neq 0$
5. $A^k$ is invertible and $(A^k)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^k = A^{-k}$

### Example 8: Product of Inverses

If $ A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $ B^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, find $(AB)^{-1}$.

**Solution:**

Using $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ (note the order):

$$ (AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ (AB)^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 9: Transpose of Inverse

If $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, find $(A^T)^{-1}$ and $(A^{-1})^T$, and verify they're equal.

**Solution:**

First find $A^{-1}$:
$\det(A) = 1$
$$ A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Then:
$$ (A^{-1})^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Now find $A^T$ and its inverse:
$$ A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$\det(A^T) = 1$
$$ (A^T)^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T =  \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ ✓

## Invertible Matrix Theorem (Preview)

For an $n \times n$ matrix $A$, the following are equivalent (all true or all false):

1. $A$ is invertible
2. $A$ is row equivalent to $I_n$
3. $A$ has $n$ pivot positions
4. $A\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ has only the trivial solution
5. The columns of $A$ are linearly independent
6. $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ has a unique solution for every $\vec{b}$
7. The columns of $A$ span $\mathbb{R}^n$
8. $\det(A) \neq 0$

This powerful theorem connects many concepts. We'll explore these connections more in future units.

### Example 10: Using the Invertible Matrix Theorem

Show that $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$ is invertible by showing it has 3 pivot positions.

**Solution:**

Row reduce:
$R_3 - R_1 \to R_3$:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

$R_3 - R_2 \to R_3$:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Only 2 pivot positions, not 3.

**Answer:** $A$ is NOT invertible (singular). We found only 2 pivots in the $3 \times 3$ matrix.

### Example 11: Application to Cryptography

A simple encryption uses invertible matrix $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ to encode message vectors. If encrypted message is $ \vec{c} = \begin{bmatrix} 9 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}$, decode it.

**Solution:**

Decoding means solving $A\vec{m} = \vec{c}$, so $\vec{m} = A^{-1}\vec{c}$.

Find $A^{-1}$:
$\det(A) = 3 - 2 = 1$

$$ A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Decode:
$$ \vec{m} = A^{-1}\vec{c} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 9 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** Original message vector: $ \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ (perhaps representing letters E and B)

### Example 12: Population Model

A population model has transition matrix $ P = \begin{bmatrix} 0.8 & 0.3 \\ 0.2 & 0.7 \end{bmatrix}$. If current populations are $ \vec{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 100 \\ 50 \end{bmatrix}$, what were the populations one time step earlier?

**Solution:**

We know $P\vec{x}_0 = \vec{x}_1$, so $\vec{x}_0 = P^{-1}\vec{x}_1$.

Find $P^{-1}$:
$\det(P) = 0.56 - 0.06 = 0.5$

$$ P^{-1} = \frac{1}{0.5}\begin{bmatrix} 0.7 & -0.3 \\ -0.2 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.4 & -0.6 \\ -0.4 & 1.6 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \vec{x}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 1.4 & -0.6 \\ -0.4 & 1.6 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 100 \\ 50 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 110 \\ 40 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** Previous populations were 110 and 40.

### Example 13: Solving Matrix Equations

Solve for $X$ in: $AX + B = C$ where:
$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad C = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Solution:**

Rearrange: $AX = C - B$

$$ C - B = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

So $AX =  \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$

From Example 7, $ A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$

$$ X = A^{-1}(C - B) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ X = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
$\det\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 6 \end{bmatrix} = 12 - 12 = 0$
Determinant is zero, so not invertible.
Second row is twice the first row.

**Problem 2: A**
$\det(A) = 6 - 5 = 1$
$ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{1}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -5 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -5 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$

**Problem 3: E**
$(A^2B)^{-1} = B^{-1}(A^2)^{-1} = B^{-1}(A^{-1})^2$
Also equals $B^{-1}A^{-1}A^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-2}$
Both B and C are correct.

**Problem 4: B**
Only 2 pivot positions in a $3 \times 3$ matrix.
Doesn't reduce to identity.
Not invertible.

**Problem 5: A**
$(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T$
Transpose of $ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ is $ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$
