# Linear Algebra Topic 2.2: Linear Independence

Three vectors lie in a plane. Intuitively, you know that one of them is "redundant"—it can be expressed using the other two. But how do we formalize this? How do we determine whether a set of vectors carries truly independent information, or whether some vectors are just rehashing what others already tell us?

This is the question of **linear independence**. It's not just abstract theory—independence determines whether a system has a unique solution, whether a set of vectors can serve as coordinates, whether a transformation is invertible. In data science, independent features provide new information; dependent features are redundant. In physics, independent modes of vibration represent distinct oscillations.

The concept is deceptively simple: vectors are linearly independent if none can be written as a combination of the others. But the computational and theoretical implications run deep. Understanding independence unlocks the concept of dimension, the foundation of bases, and the structure of solution spaces. Let's make this precise.

## Definition of Linear Independence

Vectors $\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, \ldots, \vec{v}_p$ in vector space $V$ are **linearly independent** if the only solution to:

$$ c_1\vec{v}_1 + c_2\vec{v}_2 + \cdots + c_p\vec{v}_p = \vec{0}$$

is the **trivial solution**: $c_1 = c_2 = \cdots = c_p = 0$.

If a nontrivial solution exists (some $c_i \neq 0$), the vectors are **linearly dependent**.

**Geometric intuition**:
- In $\mathbb{R}^2$: Two vectors are dependent iff they lie on the same line through the origin
- In $\mathbb{R}^3$: Three vectors are dependent iff they lie in the same plane through the origin

### Example 1: Testing Independence in $\mathbb{R}^3$

Determine if $ \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$, $ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$, $ \vec{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 8 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix}$ are linearly independent.

**Solution:**

Form the equation $c_1\vec{v}_1 + c_2\vec{v}_2 + c_3\vec{v}_3 = \vec{0}$:

$$ c_1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} + c_2\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} + c_3\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 8 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

This gives the system:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 & 7 \\ 2 & 5 & 8 \\ 3 & 6 & 9 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ c_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Row reduce:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 & 7 \\ 0 & -3 & -6 \\ 0 & -6 & -12 \end{bmatrix} \to \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 & 7 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Free variable $c_3 = t$. Then $c_2 = -2t$ and $c_1 = t$.

Nontrivial solution: $(c_1, c_2, c_3) = (1, -2, 1)$ with $t = 1$.

**Answer:** Linearly dependent. In fact, $\vec{v}_1 - 2\vec{v}_2 + \vec{v}_3 = \vec{0}$, or $\vec{v}_3 = 2\vec{v}_2 - \vec{v}_1$.

### Example 2: Independence in $\mathbb{R}^2$

Are $ \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}$ linearly independent?

**Solution:**

Notice $\vec{v}_2 = 2\vec{v}_1$.

So $2\vec{v}_1 - \vec{v}_2 = \vec{0}$ with coefficients $(2, -1) \neq (0, 0)$.

Nontrivial solution exists.

**Answer:** Linearly dependent. One vector is a scalar multiple of the other.

### Example 3: Standard Basis Vectors

Show that the standard basis vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ are linearly independent:
$$ \vec{e}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \vec{e}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \vec{e}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Solution:**

Consider $c_1\vec{e}_1 + c_2\vec{e}_2 + c_3\vec{e}_3 = \vec{0}$:

$$ c_1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + c_2\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + c_3\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ c_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

This immediately gives $c_1 = c_2 = c_3 = 0$.

**Answer:** Linearly independent. Only the trivial solution exists.

## Matrix Columns and Independence

For matrix $A = [\vec{a}_1 \ \vec{a}_2 \ \cdots \ \vec{a}_n]$, the columns are linearly independent if and only if $A\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ has only the trivial solution.

**Equivalently**: The columns of $A$ are linearly independent iff Nul$(A) = \{\vec{0}\}$ iff every column is a pivot column.

### Example 4: Using Matrix Form

Determine if the columns of $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ are linearly independent.

**Solution:**

This matrix is already in echelon form. Columns 1 and 2 are pivot columns, but column 3 is not.

There's a free variable, so nontrivial solutions to $A\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ exist.

Specifically, with $x_3 = t$: $x_1 = -2t$, $x_2 = -3t$

So $-2\vec{a}_1 - 3\vec{a}_2 + \vec{a}_3 = \vec{0}$, giving $\vec{a}_3 = 2\vec{a}_1 + 3\vec{a}_2$.

**Answer:** Linearly dependent. The third column is a combination of the first two.

### Example 5: Quick Test for Independence

Without computation, determine if these five vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ are linearly independent:
$$ \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \ \vec{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \ \vec{v}_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \ \vec{v}_5 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Solution:**

We have 5 vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

**Theorem**: If $p > n$ (more vectors than dimensions), the vectors must be linearly dependent.

Here $p = 5 > 3 = n$, so the vectors are dependent.

Intuitively: Can't have more than $n$ independent vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

**Answer:** Linearly dependent (by dimension argument).

## Characterization of Linear Dependence

Vectors $\vec{v}_1, \ldots, \vec{v}_p$ are linearly dependent if and only if at least one vector can be written as a linear combination of the others.

**More precisely**: If dependent, then some $\vec{v}_j$ (with $j > 1$) is a linear combination of the preceding vectors $\vec{v}_1, \ldots, \vec{v}_{j-1}$.

### Example 6: Expressing Dependent Vector

Given that $\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, \vec{v}_3$ are dependent with $2\vec{v}_1 - 3\vec{v}_2 + \vec{v}_3 = \vec{0}$, express one vector in terms of the others.

**Solution:**

From $2\vec{v}_1 - 3\vec{v}_2 + \vec{v}_3 = \vec{0}$:

Solve for $\vec{v}_3$: $\vec{v}_3 = 3\vec{v}_2 - 2\vec{v}_1$

Or solve for $\vec{v}_2$: $3\vec{v}_2 = 2\vec{v}_1 + \vec{v}_3$, so $\vec{v}_2 = \frac{2}{3}\vec{v}_1 + \frac{1}{3}\vec{v}_3$

Or solve for $\vec{v}_1$: $\vec{v}_1 = \frac{3}{2}\vec{v}_2 - \frac{1}{2}\vec{v}_3$

**Answer:** $\vec{v}_3 = -2\vec{v}_1 + 3\vec{v}_2$ (or any of the other forms).

### Example 7: Zero Vector in Set

Show that any set containing the zero vector is linearly dependent.

**Solution:**

Suppose $\vec{v}_1 = \vec{0}$ and the set is $\{\vec{0}, \vec{v}_2, \ldots, \vec{v}_p\}$.

Consider: $1 \cdot \vec{0} + 0 \cdot \vec{v}_2 + \cdots + 0 \cdot \vec{v}_p = \vec{0}$

This is a nontrivial solution (coefficient of $\vec{0}$ is 1, not 0).

**Answer:** Any set containing $\vec{0}$ is linearly dependent.

### Example 8: Two Vectors

When are two vectors $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ linearly dependent?

**Solution:**

$\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ are dependent iff $c_1\vec{u} + c_2\vec{v} = \vec{0}$ has a nontrivial solution.

**Case 1**: If $\vec{u} = \vec{0}$ or $\vec{v} = \vec{0}$, they're dependent (previous example).

**Case 2**: If both are nonzero, dependent means one is a scalar multiple of the other.

If $\vec{v} = k\vec{u}$, then $\vec{u} - k^{-1}\vec{v} = \vec{0}$ (nontrivial).

Conversely, if $c_1\vec{u} + c_2\vec{v} = \vec{0}$ with, say, $c_1 \neq 0$, then $\vec{u} = -\frac{c_2}{c_1}\vec{v}$.

**Answer:** Two vectors are dependent iff one is a scalar multiple of the other (including when one is $\vec{0}$).

## Independence in Function Spaces

Linear independence applies to any vector space, not just $\mathbb{R}^n$.

### Example 9: Polynomial Independence

Are $p_1(x) = 1$, $p_2(x) = x$, $p_3(x) = x^2$ linearly independent in $P_2$?

**Solution:**

Consider $c_1(1) + c_2(x) + c_3(x^2) = 0$ (zero polynomial).

This equation must hold for all $x$, meaning:
$$ c_1 + c_2x + c_3x^2 = 0 \text{ for all } x$$

The only way a polynomial is identically zero is if all coefficients are zero.

So $c_1 = 0$, $c_2 = 0$, $c_3 = 0$.

**Answer:** Linearly independent. These form the standard basis for $P_2$.

### Example 10: Function Independence

Are $f_1(x) = e^x$, $f_2(x) = e^{2x}$, $f_3(x) = e^{3x}$ linearly independent?

**Solution:**

Suppose $c_1e^x + c_2e^{2x} + c_3e^{3x} = 0$ for all $x$.

Try specific values:
- $x = 0$: $c_1 + c_2 + c_3 = 0$
- $x = 1$: $c_1e + c_2e^2 + c_3e^3 = 0$
- $x = -1$: $c_1e^{-1} + c_2e^{-2} + c_3e^{-3} = 0$

This gives three equations in three unknowns. The system has a unique solution only if $c_1 = c_2 = c_3 = 0$.

(Can verify determinant is nonzero, or use Wronskian from differential equations.)

**Answer:** Linearly independent.

## Properties of Linear Independence

**Property 1**: A single nonzero vector is linearly independent.

**Property 2**: If a set contains more vectors than the dimension of the space, it's dependent.

**Property 3**: If $\{\vec{v}_1, \ldots, \vec{v}_p\}$ is independent, then so is any subset.

**Property 4**: If $\{\vec{v}_1, \ldots, \vec{v}_p\}$ is dependent, then so is any superset.

### Example 11: Using Properties

Given that $\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, \vec{v}_3\}$ is linearly independent, what can we say about $\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2\}$?

**Solution:**

By Property 3, any subset of an independent set is independent.

$\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2\} \subset \{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, \vec{v}_3\}$

**Answer:** $\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2\}$ is linearly independent.

### Example 12: Maximum Independent Set

In $\mathbb{R}^4$, what is the maximum number of linearly independent vectors?

**Solution:**

By Property 2, if we have more than 4 vectors in $\mathbb{R}^4$, they must be dependent.

Can we find 4 independent vectors? Yes—the standard basis $\{\vec{e}_1, \vec{e}_2, \vec{e}_3, \vec{e}_4\}$.

**Answer:** Maximum is 4 (which equals the dimension of the space).

## Relationship to Spanning

If $\{\vec{v}_1, \ldots, \vec{v}_p\}$ spans $V$ and is linearly independent, it's called a **basis** for $V$ (Topic 2.3).

If the set spans but is dependent, we can remove vectors and still span.

If the set is independent but doesn't span, we can add vectors to make it span.

### Example 13: Checking Span and Independence

Do the vectors $ \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ span $\mathbb{R}^3$? Are they independent?

**Solution:**

**Independence**: The equation $c_1\vec{v}_1 + c_2\vec{v}_2 = \vec{0}$ gives:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

So $c_1 = c_2 = 0$. Independent ✓

**Spanning**: Can we write $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ as $c_1\vec{v}_1 + c_2\vec{v}_2$?

$$ c_1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + c_2\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

The third component is always 0, so we can't get $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Answer:** Independent but don't span $\mathbb{R}^3$. They span a 2D subspace (the $xy$-plane).

## Practice Problems

**Problem 1:** Which set of vectors is linearly independent?

A) $ \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$

B) $ \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$

C) $ \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 8 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$

D) $ \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$

E) $ \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$

**Problem 2:** The columns of matrix $A$ are linearly independent if and only if:

A) $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ is always consistent
B) $A\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ has only the trivial solution
C) $A$ is square
D) $\text{Col}(A) = \mathbb{R}^m$
E) $A$ is invertible

**Problem 3:** If vectors $\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, \vec{v}_3, \vec{v}_4$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ are linearly dependent, which must be true?

A) All four vectors are zero
B) At least one vector is zero
C) At least one vector can be written as a combination of the others
D) All vectors are scalar multiples of each other
E) No two vectors are parallel

**Problem 4:** How many vectors in a linearly independent set in $\mathbb{R}^5$ can there be at most?

A) 4
B) 5
C) 6
D) 10
E) Infinitely many

**Problem 5:** If $\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, \vec{v}_3\}$ is linearly independent and $\vec{v}_4 = 2\vec{v}_1 - \vec{v}_2$, what can we conclude about $\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, \vec{v}_3, \vec{v}_4\}$?

A) It's linearly independent
B) It's linearly dependent
C) It depends on $\vec{v}_3$
D) It's independent iff $\vec{v}_3 = \vec{0}$
E) Cannot be determined

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: D**
(A) Second vector is $2 \times$ first; dependent
(B) Three vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$; must be dependent (more vectors than dimensions)
(C) Third row minus twice second row equals first row; dependent
(D) Two standard basis vectors; independent ✓
(E) Contains zero vector; dependent

**Problem 2: B**
Linear independence means $c_1\vec{a}_1 + \cdots + c_n\vec{a}_n = \vec{0}$ only when all $c_i = 0$.
This is exactly $A\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ has only trivial solution.
(A), (D), (E) require more conditions than just independence.

**Problem 3: C**
Dependence means nontrivial solution to $c_1\vec{v}_1 + c_2\vec{v}_2 + c_3\vec{v}_3 + c_4\vec{v}_4 = \vec{0}$.
Some $c_i \neq 0$; solve for that $\vec{v}_i$ in terms of others.
(A), (B), (D) are too strong.
By dimension argument: 4 vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ must be dependent.

**Problem 4: B**
Maximum number of independent vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is $n$.
In $\mathbb{R}^5$, maximum is 5.
More than 5 vectors in $\mathbb{R}^5$ must be dependent.

**Problem 5: B**
$\vec{v}_4 = 2\vec{v}_1 - \vec{v}_2$ means:
$2\vec{v}_1 - \vec{v}_2 + 0\vec{v}_3 - \vec{v}_4 = \vec{0}$
This is a nontrivial linear combination equaling zero.
The set is dependent.
