
# Linear Algebra Topic 3.1: Introduction to Linear Transformations

When you rotate an image on your phone, scale a CAD drawing, or apply a filter to a photograph, you're using linear transformations. These operations take input vectors and produce output vectors in a way that preserves the fundamental structure of vector spaces. A linear transformation doesn't just arbitrarily scramble data—it respects addition and scalar multiplication, the two operations that define vector spaces.

This seemingly simple requirement—preserving vector space operations—has profound consequences. Linear transformations can be represented by matrices, making abstract mappings concrete and computable. They connect geometry to algebra, allowing us to analyze rotations, reflections, projections, and dilations using matrix multiplication. In data science, linear transformations reduce dimensions while preserving relationships. In physics, they describe coordinate changes and symmetries.

Understanding linear transformations is the bridge from computational linear algebra to its applications. We'll see how functions between vector spaces can be characterized by simple algebraic properties, how matrices encode these functions completely, and how transformation composition works. This perspective transforms matrices from arrays of numbers into operators that act on vector spaces.

## Definition of Linear Transformation

A function $T: V \to W$ between vector spaces $V$ and $W$ is a **linear transformation** if for all vectors $\vec{u}, \vec{v} \in V$ and all scalars $c$:

1. **Preserves addition**: $T(\vec{u} + \vec{v}) = T(\vec{u}) + T(\vec{v})$
2. **Preserves scalar multiplication**: $T(c\vec{v}) = cT(\vec{v})$

These two properties can be combined into a single statement:
$$ T(c\vec{u} + d\vec{v}) = cT(\vec{u}) + dT(\vec{v})$$

for all vectors $\vec{u}, \vec{v}$ and scalars $c, d$.

**Important consequence**: Every linear transformation satisfies $T(\vec{0}) = \vec{0}$.

**Proof**: $T(\vec{0}) = T(0\vec{v}) = 0T(\vec{v}) = \vec{0}$ ✓

### Example 1: Verifying Linearity

Determine if $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ defined by $ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 2x + y \\ 3x - y \end{bmatrix}$ is linear.

**Solution:**

**Check property 1 (addition)**:
$$ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ y_1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} x_2 \\ y_2 \end{bmatrix}\right) = T\left(\begin{bmatrix} x_1 + x_2 \\ y_1 + y_2 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 2(x_1 + x_2) + (y_1 + y_2) \\ 3(x_1 + x_2) - (y_1 + y_2) \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = \begin{bmatrix} 2x_1 + y_1 + 2x_2 + y_2 \\ 3x_1 - y_1 + 3x_2 - y_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2x_1 + y_1 \\ 3x_1 - y_1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 2x_2 + y_2 \\ 3x_2 - y_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = T\left(\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ y_1 \end{bmatrix}\right) + T\left(\begin{bmatrix} x_2 \\ y_2 \end{bmatrix}\right)$$ ✓

**Check property 2 (scalar multiplication)**:
$$ T\left(c\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}\right) = T\left(\begin{bmatrix} cx \\ cy \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 2cx + cy \\ 3cx - cy \end{bmatrix} = c\begin{bmatrix} 2x + y \\ 3x - y \end{bmatrix} = cT\left(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}\right)$$ ✓

**Answer:** Yes, $T$ is a linear transformation.

### Example 2: Non-Linear Transformation (Addition Fails)

Show that $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ defined by $ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} x + 1 \\ y \end{bmatrix}$ is NOT linear.

**Solution:**

Check if $T(\vec{0}) = \vec{0}$:
$$ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 + 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \neq \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Since $T(\vec{0}) \neq \vec{0}$, $T$ is not linear.

Alternatively, check addition directly:
$$ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right) + T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Since these don't match, addition is not preserved.

**Answer:** Not linear. The "+1" term breaks linearity (it's an **affine transformation**, not linear).

### Example 3: Non-Linear Transformation (Scalar Multiplication Fails)

Show that $T: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ defined by $T(x) = x^2$ is NOT linear.

**Solution:**

Check scalar multiplication:
$$ T(2x) = (2x)^2 = 4x^2$$
$$ 2T(x) = 2x^2$$

These are not equal in general. For instance, with $x = 1$:
$$ T(2 \cdot 1) = T(2) = 4$$
$$ 2T(1) = 2(1) = 2$$

Since $4 \neq 2$, scalar multiplication is not preserved.

**Answer:** Not linear. Squaring is a nonlinear operation.

## Matrix Representations

Every linear transformation $T: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ can be represented as matrix multiplication.

If $T: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is linear, there exists a unique $m \times n$ matrix $A$ such that:
$$ T(\vec{x}) = A\vec{x}$$

for all $\vec{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$.

**Finding the standard matrix**: The columns of $A$ are the images of the standard basis vectors:
$$ A = [T(\vec{e}_1) \ T(\vec{e}_2) \ \cdots \ T(\vec{e}_n)]$$

where $\vec{e}_1, \ldots, \vec{e}_n$ are the standard basis vectors of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

### Example 4: Finding the Standard Matrix

Find the standard matrix for the linear transformation $T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ defined by:
$$ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} x - 2y + 3z \\ 4x + 5y - z \end{bmatrix}$$

**Solution:**

Apply $T$ to each standard basis vector:

$$ T(\vec{e}_1) = T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - 0 + 0 \\ 4 + 0 - 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ T(\vec{e}_2) = T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 - 2 + 0 \\ 0 + 5 - 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ T(\vec{e}_3) = T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 - 0 + 3 \\ 0 + 0 - 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$

Verify: $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x - 2y + 3z \\ 4x + 5y - z \end{bmatrix}$ ✓

### Example 5: From Matrix to Transformation

Given matrix $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$, describe the linear transformation $T(\vec{x}) = A\vec{x}$ explicitly.

**Solution:**

$$ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2x + y \\ -x + 3y \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $T$ takes input $(x, y)$ and outputs $(2x + y, -x + 3y)$.

## Geometric Linear Transformations

Many geometric operations are linear transformations.

**Common transformations in $\mathbb{R}^2$**:

**Rotation by angle $\theta$ counterclockwise**:
$$ R_{\theta} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$$

**Reflection across the $x$-axis**:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Horizontal scaling by factor $k$**:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} k & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Projection onto the $x$-axis**:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Shear (horizontal)**:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & k \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

### Example 6: Rotation Matrix

Find the matrix for rotation by $90°$ counterclockwise in $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**Solution:**

Use $\theta = 90° = \frac{\pi}{2}$:
$$ R_{90°} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(90°) & -\sin(90°) \\ \sin(90°) & \cos(90°) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$

Verify: $ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ (rotates $(1,0)$ to $(0,1)$) ✓

### Example 7: Reflection Matrix

Find the matrix for reflection across the line $y = x$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**Solution:**

Reflection across $y = x$ swaps the coordinates.

See where basis vectors go:
- $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ reflects to $ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
- $ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ reflects to $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$

**Answer:** $ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$

Verify: $ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} y \\ x \end{bmatrix}$ (swaps coordinates) ✓

### Example 8: Projection Matrix

Find the matrix for orthogonal projection onto the $x$-axis in $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**Solution:**

Projection onto $x$-axis keeps $x$-coordinate, sets $y$-coordinate to 0.

Standard basis images:
- $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ projects to $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$
- $ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ projects to $ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$

**Answer:** $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$

Verify: $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ ✓

## Linear Transformations in Other Spaces

Linear transformations aren't limited to $\mathbb{R}^n$.

### Example 9: Differentiation Operator

Show that the differentiation operator $D: P_2 \to P_1$ defined by $D(p) = p'$ (derivative) is linear.

**Solution:**

**Check addition**:
$$ D(p + q) = (p + q)' = p' + q' = D(p) + D(q)$$ ✓

**Check scalar multiplication**:
$$ D(cp) = (cp)' = cp' = cD(p)$$ ✓

**Answer:** $D$ is linear. Differentiation preserves vector space operations.

### Example 10: Integration Operator

Show that the definite integral operator $I: C[0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ defined by $ I(f) = \int_0^1 f(x)\,dx$ is linear.

**Solution:**

**Check addition**:
$$ I(f + g) = \int_0^1 [f(x) + g(x)]\,dx = \int_0^1 f(x)\,dx + \int_0^1 g(x)\,dx = I(f) + I(g)$$ ✓

**Check scalar multiplication**:
$$ I(cf) = \int_0^1 cf(x)\,dx = c\int_0^1 f(x)\,dx = cI(f)$$ ✓

**Answer:** $I$ is linear. Integration is a linear operation.

### Example 11: Matrix Transformation

Define $T: \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2} \to \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}$ by $T(A) = A^T$ (transpose). Is $T$ linear?

**Solution:**

**Check addition**:
$$ T(A + B) = (A + B)^T = A^T + B^T = T(A) + T(B)$$ ✓

**Check scalar multiplication**:
$$ T(cA) = (cA)^T = cA^T = cT(A)$$ ✓

**Answer:** Yes, transpose is a linear transformation.

## Properties of Linear Transformations

If $T$ is linear, then:
1. $T(\vec{0}) = \vec{0}$
2. $T(-\vec{v}) = -T(\vec{v})$
3. $T(c_1\vec{v}_1 + \cdots + c_k\vec{v}_k) = c_1T(\vec{v}_1) + \cdots + c_kT(\vec{v}_k)$ (linearity of combinations)

**Property 3** says: The image of a linear combination is the linear combination of images.

### Example 12: Using Linearity

If $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ is linear with $ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and $ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 4 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}$, find $ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}\right)$.

**Solution:**

Express the input as a linear combination of basis vectors:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} = 3\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + (-2)\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Apply linearity:
$$ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}\right) = 3T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right) + (-2)T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right)$$

$$ = 3\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} + (-2)\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 4 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ -3 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -8 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -11 \\ 13 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -11 \\ 13 \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 13: Determining if Transformation Exists

Can there exist a linear transformation $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ with:
$$ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Solution:**

By linearity:
$$ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\right) = T\left(2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = 2T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = 2\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$$

But we're told $ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$.

Since $ \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} \neq \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$, this is a contradiction.

**Answer:** No such linear transformation exists. The given conditions violate linearity.
