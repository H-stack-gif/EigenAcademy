# Linear Algebra Topic 8.1: Complex Vector Spaces

Real numbers aren't enough. When you solve $x^2 + 1 = 0$, you need complex numbers: $x = \pm i$ where $i = \sqrt{-1}$. Similarly, when you find eigenvalues of a rotation matrix, you get complex numbers. A 90° rotation has no real eigenvectors—every vector gets rotated, not just stretched. But in the **complex plane**, rotation matrices have eigenvectors, and the eigenvalues $e^{i\theta}$ encode the rotation angle.

Complex eigenvalues aren't exotic exceptions—they're fundamental to understanding oscillations, waves, and rotations. In quantum mechanics, state vectors live in complex vector spaces. In signal processing, the Fourier transform uses complex exponentials $e^{i\omega t}$. In control theory, system stability depends on whether complex eigenvalues have negative real parts.

Working with complex numbers requires new inner products, new notions of length, and conjugate transposes. But the payoff is enormous: **every** matrix has eigenvalues (over $\mathbb{C}$), rotations become simple, and oscillatory phenomena become elegant. This article explores how linear algebra extends to complex vector spaces, where rotation and oscillation become as natural as stretching.

## Complex Numbers Review

A **complex number** has form $z = a + bi$ where $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ and $i^2 = -1$.

**Real part**: $\text{Re}(z) = a$

**Imaginary part**: $\text{Im}(z) = b$

**Complex conjugate**: $\bar{z} = a - bi$

**Modulus**: $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \sqrt{z\bar{z}}$

**Polar form**: $z = r e^{i\theta}$ where $r = |z|$ and $\theta = \arg(z)$

**Euler's formula**: $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$

### Example 1: Complex Arithmetic

Compute $(2 + 3i)(1 - i)$.

**Solution:**

$$ (2 + 3i)(1 - i) = 2 - 2i + 3i - 3i^2$$

$$ = 2 + i - 3(-1) = 2 + i + 3 = 5 + i$$

**Answer:** $5 + i$

### Example 2: Modulus and Conjugate

For $z = 3 + 4i$, find $|z|$ and $\bar{z}$.

**Solution:**

$$ |z| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$$

$$ \bar{z} = 3 - 4i$$

Verify: $z\bar{z} = (3 + 4i)(3 - 4i) = 9 - 12i + 12i - 16i^2 = 9 + 16 = 25 = |z|^2$ ✓

**Answer:** $|z| = 5$, $\bar{z} = 3 - 4i$

### Example 3: Polar Form

Write $z = 1 + i$ in polar form.

**Solution:**

$$ r = |z| = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}$$

$$ \theta = \arctan\left(\frac{1}{1}\right) = \frac{\pi}{4}$$

$$ z = \sqrt{2}e^{i\pi/4} = \sqrt{2}\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{4} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\right)$$

**Answer:** $ z = \sqrt{2}e^{i\pi/4}$

## Complex Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

For real matrix $A$, eigenvalues may be complex (occurring in conjugate pairs).

If $\lambda = a + bi$ is an eigenvalue with eigenvector $\vec{v}$, then $\bar{\lambda} = a - bi$ is also an eigenvalue with eigenvector $\bar{\vec{v}}$ (component-wise conjugate).

### Example 4: Rotation Matrix Eigenvalues

Find eigenvalues of $ R = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$ (rotation by angle $\theta$).

**Solution:**

For $\theta = 90°$: $ R = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$

Characteristic equation:
$$ \det(R - \lambda I) = \det\begin{bmatrix} -\lambda & -1 \\ 1 & -\lambda \end{bmatrix} = \lambda^2 + 1 = 0$$

$$ \lambda^2 = -1$$

$$ \lambda = \pm i$$

**Answer:** Eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = i$ and $\lambda_2 = -i$ (complex conjugate pair).

**Geometric meaning**: Rotation has no real eigenvectors (every vector rotates), but complex eigenvectors exist.

### Example 5: Complex Eigenvector

For $ R = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ with $\lambda = i$, find eigenvector.

**Solution:**

$$ (R - iI)\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -i & -1 \\ 1 & -i \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \vec{0}$$

From row 1: $-iv_1 - v_2 = 0$, so $v_2 = -iv_1$

Choose $v_1 = 1$: $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{bmatrix}$

Verify: $ R\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} i \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = i\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{bmatrix}$ ✓

**Answer:** $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 6: Conjugate Pairs

Verify that $\lambda_2 = -i$ has eigenvector $ \bar{\vec{v}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

$$ R\bar{\vec{v}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -i \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = -i\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{bmatrix}$$ ✓

**Answer:** Complex eigenvalues/eigenvectors occur in conjugate pairs for real matrices.

## Complex Vector Spaces

A **complex vector space** $\mathbb{C}^n$ consists of vectors with complex entries.

Addition and scalar multiplication work component-wise with complex scalars.

**Example**: $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 + i \\ 2 - 3i \\ i \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{C}^3$

### Example 7: Complex Linear Combinations

Express $ \vec{w} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 + 2i \\ 1 - i \end{bmatrix}$ as a linear combination of $ \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{bmatrix}$ and $ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} i \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

Need $c_1\vec{v}_1 + c_2\vec{v}_2 = \vec{w}$:

$$ c_1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{bmatrix} + c_2\begin{bmatrix} i \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 + 2i \\ 1 - i \end{bmatrix}$$

From first component: $c_1 + ic_2 = 3 + 2i$

From second component: $ic_1 + c_2 = 1 - i$

Multiply second equation by $-i$: $c_1 - ic_2 = -i(1-i) = -i - 1$

Add to first: $2c_1 = (3+2i) + (-1-i) = 2 + i$, so $c_1 = 1 + \frac{i}{2}$

Back-substitute: $ic_2 = 3 + 2i - c_1 = 2 + \frac{3i}{2}$, so $c_2 = \frac{2 + \frac{3i}{2}}{i} = \frac{3}{2} - 2i$

**Answer:** $ \vec{w} = \left(1 + \frac{i}{2}\right)\vec{v}_1 + \left(\frac{3}{2} - 2i\right)\vec{v}_2$

## Hermitian Inner Product

For complex vectors, the standard dot product doesn't work (it wouldn't satisfy $\vec{v} \cdot \vec{v} > 0$ for $\vec{v} \neq \vec{0}$).

**Hermitian inner product** (or **complex inner product**):
$$ \langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle = \bar{u}_1v_1 + \bar{u}_2v_2 + \cdots + \bar{u}_nv_n = \bar{\vec{u}}^T\vec{v}$$

**Properties**:
1. $\langle \vec{v}, \vec{v} \rangle \geq 0$ with equality iff $\vec{v} = \vec{0}$
2. $\langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle = \overline{\langle \vec{v}, \vec{u} \rangle}$ (conjugate symmetry)
3. $\langle \vec{u}, c\vec{v} \rangle = c\langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle$ (linear in second argument)
4. $\langle c\vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle = \bar{c}\langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle$ (conjugate linear in first)

**Length**: $ \|\vec{v}\| = \sqrt{\langle \vec{v}, \vec{v} \rangle}$

### Example 8: Hermitian Inner Product

Compute $\langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle$ for $ \vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 + i \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - i \\ i \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

$$ \langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle = (1-i)(1-i) + 2 \cdot i$$

$$ = 1 - 2i + i^2 + 2i = 1 - 1 = 0$$

**Answer:** $\langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle = 0$ (orthogonal complex vectors)

### Example 9: Length of Complex Vector

Find $ \left\|\begin{bmatrix} 1 + i \\ 2i \\ 1 - i \end{bmatrix}\right\|$.

**Solution:**

$$ \|\vec{v}\|^2 = |1+i|^2 + |2i|^2 + |1-i|^2$$

$$ = (1^2 + 1^2) + (2^2) + (1^2 + 1^2) = 2 + 4 + 2 = 8$$

$$ \|\vec{v}\| = \sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}$$

**Answer:** $\|\vec{v}\| = 2\sqrt{2}$

## Conjugate Transpose

The **conjugate transpose** (or **Hermitian transpose**) $A^*$ is:
$$ A^* = \bar{A}^T$$

Take transpose then conjugate (or vice versa—same result).

**Properties**:
- $(A^*)^* = A$
- $(A + B)^* = A^* + B^*$
- $(cA)^* = \bar{c}A^*$
- $(AB)^* = B^*A^*$

For **real** matrices: $A^* = A^T$ (conjugate transpose reduces to transpose).

### Example 10: Computing Conjugate Transpose

Find $A^*$ for $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 + i & 2 \\ i & 3 - i \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

$$ A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 + i & i \\ 2 & 3 - i \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ A^* = \bar{A}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - i & -i \\ 2 & 3 + i \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ A^* = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - i & -i \\ 2 & 3 + i \end{bmatrix}$

## Hermitian and Unitary Matrices

**Hermitian matrix**: $A^* = A$ (complex analog of symmetric)

**Properties**:
- All eigenvalues are real
- Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal (under Hermitian inner product)

**Unitary matrix**: $U^*U = I$ (complex analog of orthogonal)

Equivalently: $U^* = U^{-1}$

**Properties**:
- Preserves Hermitian inner product: $\langle U\vec{u}, U\vec{v} \rangle = \langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle$
- All eigenvalues have $|\lambda| = 1$ (lie on unit circle)

### Example 11: Hermitian Matrix

Verify that $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 + i \\ 1 - i & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ is Hermitian.

**Solution:**

$$ A^* = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 - i \\ 1 + i & 3 \end{bmatrix}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 + i \\ 1 - i & 3 \end{bmatrix} = A$$ ✓

**Answer:** Hermitian. Note: diagonal entries must be real, off-diagonal entries are conjugates.

### Example 12: Unitary Matrix

Show that $ U = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & i \\ i & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ is unitary.

**Solution:**

$$ U^* = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -i \\ -i & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ U^*U = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -i \\ -i & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & i \\ i & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 + 1 & i - i \\ -i + i & 1 + 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$ ✓

**Answer:** Unitary.

## Applications to Differential Equations

System $ \frac{d\vec{x}}{dt} = A\vec{x}$ with complex eigenvalues $\lambda = a \pm bi$ has solutions involving oscillations.

If $\lambda = a + bi$, then:
$$ \vec{x}(t) = e^{at}(\cos(bt) + i\sin(bt))\vec{v}$$

Real part and imaginary part are real solutions.

### Example 13: Oscillatory System

For $ \frac{d\vec{x}}{dt} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x}$ (harmonic oscillator), describe solutions.

**Solution:**

From Example 4, eigenvalues are $\lambda = \pm i$ (pure imaginary).

General solution:
$$ \vec{x}(t) = c_1e^{it}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{bmatrix} + c_2e^{-it}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{bmatrix}$$

Using $e^{it} = \cos t + i\sin t$:
$$ \vec{x}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} C_1\cos t + C_2\sin t \\ -C_1\sin t + C_2\cos t \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** Periodic solutions with period $2\pi$ (undamped oscillation).

Pure imaginary eigenvalues → oscillation without growth/decay.

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
$|z| = \sqrt{3^2 + (-4)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$
Modulus is always real and nonnegative.

**Problem 2: A**
Complex eigenvalues of real matrices occur in conjugate pairs.
If $\lambda = 2 + 3i$, then $\bar{\lambda} = 2 - 3i$ is also an eigenvalue.

**Problem 3: B**
Hermitian inner product: $\langle \vec{u}, \vec{v} \rangle = \bar{\vec{u}}^T\vec{v}$
Conjugate first vector, transpose, then multiply.
This ensures $\langle \vec{v}, \vec{v} \rangle > 0$ for $\vec{v} \neq \vec{0}$.

**Problem 4: B**
Hermitian: $A^* = A$ (conjugate transpose equals itself)
Complex analog of symmetric ($A^T = A$ for real matrices).
Diagonal entries must be real.

**Problem 5: B**
Unitary: $U^*U = I$
Complex analog of orthogonal ($Q^TQ = I$ for real matrices).
Means $U^{-1} = U^*$ (conjugate transpose is inverse).
