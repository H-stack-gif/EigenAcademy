# Linear Algebra Topic 5.1: Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

When you apply a linear transformation to most vectors, they change direction and magnitude. But certain special vectors—**eigenvectors**—only change in magnitude, not direction. They get stretched or compressed along their original line. The stretch factor is the **eigenvalue**. This simple idea has extraordinary consequences.

Eigenvectors reveal the natural "axes" of a transformation. In physics, they identify principal directions of stress in materials and axes of rotation for rigid bodies. In data science, they underpin principal component analysis (PCA), which reduces high-dimensional data to essential features. In differential equations, they transform coupled systems into independent equations. Google's PageRank algorithm finds the dominant eigenvector of a massive web connectivity matrix.

Understanding eigenvalues and eigenvectors requires connecting algebra (solving polynomial equations) with geometry (finding invariant directions). We'll learn to compute them systematically, understand their geometric meaning, and recognize their theoretical importance. These concepts are among the most powerful tools in all of linear algebra.

## Definition of Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix. A nonzero vector $\vec{v}$ is an **eigenvector** of $A$ if:
$$ A\vec{v} = \lambda \vec{v}$$

for some scalar $\lambda$. The scalar $\lambda$ is the **eigenvalue** corresponding to eigenvector $\vec{v}$.

**Key observations**:
- Eigenvectors must be nonzero (by definition)
- Eigenvalues can be zero, positive, negative, or even complex
- If $\vec{v}$ is an eigenvector, so is $c\vec{v}$ for any nonzero scalar $c$
- The equation says: "$A$ transforms $\vec{v}$ into a scalar multiple of itself"

**Geometric interpretation**: $A\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v}$ means $A$ stretches $\vec{v}$ by factor $|\lambda|$ (and reverses direction if $\lambda < 0$).

### Example 1: Verifying Eigenvectors

Verify that $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ is an eigenvector of $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and find its eigenvalue.

**Solution:**

$$ A\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 + 2 \\ 2 + 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$$

Check if this is a scalar multiple of $\vec{v}$:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} \stackrel{?}{=} \lambda\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

This would require $\lambda = 5$ and $2\lambda = 6$, giving $\lambda = 5$ and $\lambda = 3$ (contradiction).

**Answer:** $\vec{v}$ is NOT an eigenvector of $A$.

### Example 2: Finding Eigenvalue from Eigenvector

If $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ is an eigenvector of $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, find the eigenvalue.

**Solution:**

$$ A\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

We need $ \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \lambda\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

This doesn't work — $\begin{bmatrix}7 \\\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$ is not a scalar multiple of $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, so $\vec{v}$ is not an eigenvector. Using $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ instead:

$$ A\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 12 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} = 6\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** For eigenvector $ \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, eigenvalue $\lambda = 6$.

## The Characteristic Equation

To find eigenvalues, we solve:
$$ A\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v}$$
$$ A\vec{v} - \lambda\vec{v} = \vec{0}$$
$$ (A - \lambda I)\vec{v} = \vec{0}$$

This homogeneous system has nontrivial solutions iff $A - \lambda I$ is singular:
$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = 0$$

This is the **characteristic equation**. Its solutions are the eigenvalues.

The **characteristic polynomial** is:
$$ p(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I)$$

For $n \times n$ matrix, this is a degree-$n$ polynomial in $\lambda$.

### Example 3: Finding Eigenvalues of $2 \times 2$ Matrix

Find the eigenvalues of $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

$$ A - \lambda I = \begin{bmatrix} 4 - \lambda & 1 \\ 2 & 3 - \lambda \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = (4-\lambda)(3-\lambda) - (1)(2)$$
$$ = 12 - 4\lambda - 3\lambda + \lambda^2 - 2$$
$$ = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10$$

Solve: $\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = 0$

Factor: $(\lambda - 5)(\lambda - 2) = 0$

**Answer:** Eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 5$ and $\lambda_2 = 2$.

### Example 4: Finding Eigenvalues of $3 \times 3$ Matrix

Find the eigenvalues of $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

$$ A - \lambda I = \begin{bmatrix} 2-\lambda & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 2-\lambda & 1 \\ -1 & 0 & 1-\lambda \end{bmatrix}$$

Expand along row 1 (has two zeros):
$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = (2-\lambda)\begin{vmatrix} 2-\lambda & 1 \\ 0 & 1-\lambda \end{vmatrix}$$

$$ = (2-\lambda)[(2-\lambda)(1-\lambda) - 0]$$
$$ = (2-\lambda)(2-\lambda)(1-\lambda)$$
$$ = (2-\lambda)^2(1-\lambda)$$

**Answer:** Eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 2$ (with multiplicity 2) and $\lambda_2 = 1$.

### Example 5: Triangular Matrix Eigenvalues

Find eigenvalues of $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 5 & 2 \\ 0 & -1 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

$$ A - \lambda I = \begin{bmatrix} 3-\lambda & 5 & 2 \\ 0 & -1-\lambda & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 2-\lambda \end{bmatrix}$$

Upper triangular, so $\det(A - \lambda I) = (3-\lambda)(-1-\lambda)(2-\lambda)$

Set equal to zero: $(3-\lambda)(-1-\lambda)(2-\lambda) = 0$

**Answer:** Eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 3$, $\lambda_2 = -1$, $\lambda_3 = 2$.

**Key fact**: Eigenvalues of triangular matrices are the diagonal entries.

## Finding Eigenvectors

Once we have eigenvalue $\lambda$, find eigenvectors by solving:
$$ (A - \lambda I)\vec{v} = \vec{0}$$

This is a homogeneous system. The eigenvectors are the nontrivial solutions.

### Example 6: Finding Eigenvectors

For $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 5$ and $\lambda_2 = 2$ (from Example 3), find eigenvectors.

**Solution:**

**For $\lambda_1 = 5$:**
$$ (A - 5I)\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Row reduce:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \to \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Equation: $v_1 - v_2 = 0$, so $v_1 = v_2$

General solution: $ \vec{v} = v_2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

Eigenvector: $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ (or any nonzero multiple)

**For $\lambda_2 = 2$:**
$$ (A - 2I)\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Row reduce:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \to \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Equation: $v_1 + \frac{1}{2}v_2 = 0$, so $v_1 = -\frac{1}{2}v_2$

General solution: $ \vec{v} = v_2\begin{bmatrix} -1/2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

Eigenvector: $ \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ (multiplying by 2 to clear fraction)

**Answer:** For $\lambda_1 = 5$: $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$; For $\lambda_2 = 2$: $ \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 7: Repeated Eigenvalue

For $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 2$ (multiplicity 2) and $\lambda_2 = 1$ (from Example 4), find all eigenvectors.

**Solution:**

**For $\lambda = 2$:**
$$ (A - 2I)\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ v_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Row reduce:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Equation: $v_1 + v_3 = 0$, so $v_1 = -v_3$

Free variables: $v_2 = s$, $v_3 = t$

$$ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -t \\ s \\ t \end{bmatrix} = s\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + t\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Two linearly independent eigenvectors: $ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $ \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

**For $\lambda = 1$:**
$$ (A - I)\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ v_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

From row 1: $v_1 = 0$
From row 2 with $v_1 = 0$: $v_2 + v_3 = 0$, so $v_2 = -v_3$

$$ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -v_3 \\ v_3 \end{bmatrix} = v_3\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** For $\lambda = 2$: $ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$; For $\lambda = 1$: $ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

## Eigenspaces

The **eigenspace** $E_{\lambda}$ corresponding to eigenvalue $\lambda$ is:
$$ E_{\lambda} = \text{Nul}(A - \lambda I) = \{\vec{v} : (A - \lambda I)\vec{v} = \vec{0}\}$$

This is the set of all eigenvectors for $\lambda$, plus the zero vector.

**Key facts**:
- $E_{\lambda}$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$
- $\dim(E_{\lambda}) \geq 1$ for each eigenvalue
- $\dim(E_{\lambda}) \leq$ algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda$

### Example 8: Finding Eigenspace Dimension

For the matrix in Example 7, what are $\dim(E_2)$ and $\dim(E_1)$?

**Solution:**

From Example 7:
- For $\lambda = 2$: two independent eigenvectors
- For $\lambda = 1$: one independent eigenvector

**Answer:** $\dim(E_2) = 2$ and $\dim(E_1) = 1$

Note: $\lambda = 2$ has algebraic multiplicity 2 and geometric multiplicity 2.

### Example 9: Complex Eigenvalues

Find eigenvalues of $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ (90° rotation).

**Solution:**

$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = \det\begin{bmatrix} -\lambda & -1 \\ 1 & -\lambda \end{bmatrix} = \lambda^2 + 1$$

Set to zero: $\lambda^2 + 1 = 0$, giving $\lambda^2 = -1$

**Answer:** $\lambda_1 = i$ and $\lambda_2 = -i$ (complex eigenvalues)

**Interpretation**: Rotation has no real eigenvectors (no vector stays on its own line when rotated 90°).

## Properties of Eigenvalues

**For $n \times n$ matrix $A$**:
1. Sum of eigenvalues = trace$(A)$ (sum of diagonal entries)
2. Product of eigenvalues = $\det(A)$
3. $A$ is invertible iff all eigenvalues are nonzero
4. Eigenvalues of $A^T$ equal eigenvalues of $A$
5. Eigenvalues of $kA$ are $k\lambda_1, k\lambda_2, \ldots, k\lambda_n$

### Example 10: Using Eigenvalue Properties

If $A$ is $3 \times 3$ with eigenvalues 2, 3, and 5, find $\det(A)$ and trace$(A)$.

**Solution:**

Product of eigenvalues: $\det(A) = (2)(3)(5) = 30$

Sum of eigenvalues: trace$(A) = 2 + 3 + 5 = 10$

**Answer:** $\det(A) = 30$, trace$(A) = 10$

### Example 11: Eigenvalues of Scalar Multiple

If $A$ has eigenvalues 1, 2, 3, what are eigenvalues of $3A$?

**Solution:**

If $A\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v}$, then:
$$ (3A)\vec{v} = 3(A\vec{v}) = 3(\lambda\vec{v}) = (3\lambda)\vec{v}$$

Each eigenvalue is multiplied by 3.

**Answer:** Eigenvalues of $3A$ are 3, 6, 9.

### Example 12: Zero Eigenvalue

If $A$ is $4 \times 4$ with eigenvalues 1, 2, 3, and 0, is $A$ invertible?

**Solution:**

$A$ is invertible iff all eigenvalues are nonzero.

Since 0 is an eigenvalue, $A$ is singular.

Alternatively: $\det(A) = (1)(2)(3)(0) = 0$, so not invertible.

**Answer:** No, $A$ is not invertible.

### Example 13: Finding Missing Eigenvalue

A $3 \times 3$ matrix has trace 9 and two eigenvalues are 2 and 4. Find the third eigenvalue.

**Solution:**

Let the third eigenvalue be $\lambda_3$.

Sum of eigenvalues = trace: $2 + 4 + \lambda_3 = 9$

Solve: $\lambda_3 = 3$

**Answer:** The third eigenvalue is 3.

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: A**
$A\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v} = 3\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$

**Problem 2: B**
For triangular matrix, $\det(A - \lambda I)$ is product of diagonal entries of $(A - \lambda I)$
Setting equal to zero gives diagonal entries of $A$ as eigenvalues.

**Problem 3: B**
$\det(A) = $ product of eigenvalues $= (-1)(2)(3) = -6$

**Problem 4: C**
Check determinants:
(A) $\det = 2 \neq 0$
(B) $\det = 12 \neq 0$
(C) $\det = 2 - 2 = 0$ ✓
(D) $\det = 5 \neq 0$
(E) $\det = 1 \neq 0$
Matrix with $\det = 0$ has eigenvalue 0.

**Problem 5: E**
Sum of eigenvalues = trace
$1 + 2 + 3 = 6$ ✓
This is consistent: the trace is 6 and the eigenvalues sum to 6. Also $\det(A) = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 = 6$. Both B and C are true, and B is the more fundamental reason. Answer: B
