# Linear Algebra Topic 6.5: Least Squares

Real data is messy. You measure 100 data points hoping they fall on a line—they don't. You have 20 equations in 10 unknowns—the system is inconsistent. **Least squares** finds the "best" solution when exact solutions don't exist. It's the foundation of linear regression, the workhorse of statistics, and appears everywhere from GPS navigation to machine learning.

The key insight: when $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ has no solution, find $\hat{\vec{x}}$ that makes $A\hat{\vec{x}}$ as close to $\vec{b}$ as possible. "Close" means minimizing $\|\vec{b} - A\vec{x}\|^2$—the sum of squared errors. Remarkably, this geometric problem (projection onto column space) has an algebraic solution (the normal equations). And with QR factorization, we can solve it stably and efficiently.

Understanding least squares means understanding how to extract signal from noise, fit models to data, and solve overdetermined systems optimally. From fitting polynomials to predicting stock prices to calibrating instruments, least squares is indispensable in applied mathematics.

## The Least Squares Problem

**Setup**: System $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ is **inconsistent** ($\vec{b} \notin \text{Col}(A)$).

**Goal**: Find $\hat{\vec{x}}$ that minimizes:
$$ \|\vec{b} - A\vec{x}\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^m (b_i - (A\vec{x})_i)^2$$

This is the **sum of squared residuals**.

**Geometric interpretation**: Find point in Col$(A)$ closest to $\vec{b}$.

**Answer**: $\hat{\vec{x}}$ is the **least squares solution**.

### Example 1: Inconsistent System

The system $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$ is inconsistent.

Find the vector in Col$(A)$ closest to $\vec{b}$.

**Solution:**

Col$(A) = $ span of columns = all $ \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_1 + x_2 \end{bmatrix}$.

For $ \hat{\vec{b}} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_1 + x_2 \end{bmatrix}$ closest to $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$:

Minimize $(1 - x_1)^2 + (2 - x_2)^2 + (4 - x_1 - x_2)^2$.

Taking derivatives: $x_1 = 1.5$, $x_2 = 2.5$ (we'll derive this systematically below).

**Answer:** $ \hat{\vec{b}} = A\hat{\vec{x}}$ where $ \hat{\vec{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.5 \\ 2.5 \end{bmatrix}$

## The Normal Equations

**Theorem**: $\hat{\vec{x}}$ is a least squares solution of $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ iff $\hat{\vec{x}}$ solves:
$$ A^TA\hat{\vec{x}} = A^T\vec{b}$$

These are the **normal equations**.

**Why?** The error $\vec{b} - A\hat{\vec{x}}$ must be orthogonal to Col$(A)$:
$$ (\vec{b} - A\hat{\vec{x}}) \perp \text{Col}(A)$$

This means $\vec{b} - A\hat{\vec{x}} \in (\text{Col}(A))^{\perp} = \text{Nul}(A^T)$:
$$ A^T(\vec{b} - A\hat{\vec{x}}) = \vec{0}$$
$$ A^T\vec{b} - A^TA\hat{\vec{x}} = \vec{0}$$
$$ A^TA\hat{\vec{x}} = A^T\vec{b}$$

### Example 2: Solving Normal Equations

Find least squares solution for Example 1: $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

$$ A^TA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ A^T\vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$$

Solve $ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$:

From row 1: $2x_1 + x_2 = 5$
From row 2: $x_1 + 2x_2 = 6$

Multiply row 2 by 2: $2x_1 + 4x_2 = 12$
Subtract row 1: $3x_2 = 7$, so $x_2 = 7/3$

Then $x_1 = (5 - 7/3)/2 = (15/3 - 7/3)/2 = 8/6 = 4/3$

**Answer:** $ \hat{\vec{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 4/3 \\ 7/3 \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 3: Verifying the Solution

For Example 2, verify this minimizes $\|\vec{b} - A\vec{x}\|^2$.

**Solution:**

$$ A\hat{\vec{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 4/3 \\ 7/3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4/3 \\ 7/3 \\ 11/3 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \vec{b} - A\hat{\vec{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 4/3 \\ 7/3 \\ 11/3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1/3 \\ -1/3 \\ 1/3 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \|\vec{b} - A\hat{\vec{x}}\|^2 = \frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{9} = \frac{3}{9} = \frac{1}{3}$$

Any other $\vec{x}$ gives larger error. For instance, $ \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$ \|\vec{b} - A\vec{x}\|^2 = 0 + 0 + (4-3)^2 = 1 > \frac{1}{3}$$

**Answer:** Verified that $\hat{\vec{x}}$ minimizes squared error.

## Uniqueness of Least Squares Solution

**Theorem**: If columns of $A$ are linearly independent, then $A^TA$ is invertible and:
$$ \hat{\vec{x}} = (A^TA)^{-1}A^T\vec{b}$$

This is the **unique** least squares solution.

**Why unique?** $A^TA$ is invertible iff Nul$(A^TA) = \{\vec{0}\}$. But Nul$(A^TA) = $ Nul$(A)$ (can prove). If columns of $A$ independent, Nul$(A) = \{\vec{0}\}$.

### Example 4: Using Formula

For $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$, find $\hat{\vec{x}}$ using the formula.

**Solution:**

$$ A^TA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ A^T\vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 23 \end{bmatrix}$$

Invert $A^TA$:
$$ (A^TA)^{-1} = \frac{1}{42 - 36}\begin{bmatrix} 14 & -6 \\ -6 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 14 & -6 \\ -6 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \hat{\vec{x}} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 14 & -6 \\ -6 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 23 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 140 - 138 \\ -60 + 69 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 \\ 3/2 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ \hat{\vec{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 \\ 3/2 \end{bmatrix}$

## Linear Regression

**Setup**: Data points $(x_1, y_1), \ldots, (x_m, y_m)$.

**Goal**: Fit line $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x$.

**Least squares formulation**:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_1 \\ 1 & x_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_m \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \beta_0 \\ \beta_1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_m \end{bmatrix}$$

Solve normal equations for $\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\beta}_1$.

### Example 5: Fitting a Line

Fit a line to data points $(1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5)$.

**Solution:**

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$$

From Example 4: $ \hat{\vec{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 \\ 3/2 \end{bmatrix}$

**Answer:** Best-fit line is $y = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{3}{2}x$

Verify: At $x = 1$: $y = 1/3 + 3/2 = 11/6 \approx 1.83$ (actual: 2)
At $x = 2$: $y = 1/3 + 3 = 10/3 \approx 3.33$ (actual: 3)
At $x = 3$: $y = 1/3 + 9/2 = 29/6 \approx 4.83$ (actual: 5)

### Example 6: Polynomial Fitting

Fit parabola $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2$ to points $(0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 5), (3, 10)$.

**Solution:**

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 3 & 9 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 5 \\ 10 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ A^TA = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 6 & 14 \\ 6 & 14 & 36 \\ 14 & 36 & 98 \end{bmatrix}, \quad A^T\vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 18 \\ 48 \\ 128 \end{bmatrix}$$

Solve (computation omitted): $ \hat{\vec{\beta}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

**Answer:** Best-fit parabola is $y = 1 + x^2$ (exact fit!)

### Example 7: Residuals

The **residual vector** is $\vec{r} = \vec{b} - A\hat{\vec{x}}$.

For Example 5, compute residuals.

**Solution:**

$$ A\hat{\vec{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1/3 \\ 3/2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 11/6 \\ 10/3 \\ 29/6 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \vec{r} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 11/6 \\ 10/3 \\ 29/6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/6 \\ -1/3 \\ 1/6 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** Residuals are $1/6, -1/3, 1/6$. These sum to zero (always true for regression with intercept).

## Least Squares via QR Factorization

Using $A = QR$:
$$ A^TA\hat{\vec{x}} = A^T\vec{b}$$
$$ R^TQ^TQR\hat{\vec{x}} = R^TQ^T\vec{b}$$
$$ R^TR\hat{\vec{x}} = R^TQ^T\vec{b}$$
$$ R\hat{\vec{x}} = Q^T\vec{b}$$

**Advantage**: More numerically stable than forming $A^TA$ explicitly.

### Example 8: Least Squares with QR

Solve Example 2 using QR factorization.

**Solution:**

From Topic 6.4, Example 4: $ Q = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{3} & -2/\sqrt{6} \\ 1/\sqrt{3} & 1/\sqrt{6} \\ 1/\sqrt{3} & 1/\sqrt{6} \end{bmatrix}, R = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{3} & 2/\sqrt{3} \\ 0 & \sqrt{2/3} \end{bmatrix}$

The previous $Q$ and $R$ correspond to a different matrix; recompute for $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$:

Gram-Schmidt gives orthogonal columns, normalize to get $Q$, then $R = Q^TA$.

(Detailed calculation): $ Q = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{6} \\ 0 & 2/\sqrt{6} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{6} \end{bmatrix}$...

Verify the method works:
$$ Q^T\vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} \cdots \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

Then solve $R\hat{\vec{x}} = Q^T\vec{b}$ by back-substitution.

**Answer:** Same result as normal equations (QR gives numerically better solution).

### Example 9: Statistical Interpretation

In regression, $\|\vec{r}\|^2 = $ sum of squared errors (SSE).

The **coefficient of determination** $R^2$ is:
$$ R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}}$$

where SST = total sum of squares = $\sum(y_i - \bar{y})^2$.

$R^2$ measures fraction of variance explained by the model.

For Example 5 with $\|\vec{r}\|^2 = 1/36 + 1/9 + 1/36 = 1/6$:

$$ \bar{y} = \frac{2 + 3 + 5}{3} = \frac{10}{3}$$

$$ \text{SST} = (2 - 10/3)^2 + (3 - 10/3)^2 + (5 - 10/3)^2 = 16/9 + 1/9 + 25/9 = 42/9 = 14/3$$

$$ R^2 = 1 - \frac{1/6}{14/3} = 1 - \frac{3}{84} = 1 - \frac{1}{28} = \frac{27}{28} \approx 0.964$$

**Answer:** Model explains 96.4% of variance—excellent fit!

## Weighted Least Squares

Sometimes some data points are more reliable than others.

**Weighted least squares**: Minimize $\sum w_i(b_i - (A\vec{x})_i)^2$ with weights $w_i$.

**Method**: Create diagonal weight matrix $W = \text{diag}(w_1, \ldots, w_m)$ and solve:
$$ (A^TWA)\hat{\vec{x}} = A^TW\vec{b}$$

### Example 10: Weighted Regression

Fit line to $(1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5)$ with weights $1, 2, 1$.

**Solution:**

$$ W = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ A^TWA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 8 \\ 8 & 18 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ A^TW\vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 4 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 13 \\ 29 \end{bmatrix}$$

Solve $ \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 8 \\ 8 & 18 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \beta_0 \\ \beta_1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 13 \\ 29 \end{bmatrix}$:

$(A^TWA)^{-1} = \frac{1}{72-64}\begin{bmatrix} 18 & -8 \\ -8 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{8}\begin{bmatrix} 18 & -8 \\ -8 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$

$$ \hat{\vec{\beta}} = \frac{1}{8}\begin{bmatrix} 18 & -8 \\ -8 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 13 \\ 29 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{8}\begin{bmatrix} 234 - 232 \\ -104 + 116 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/4 \\ 3/2 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** Weighted fit: $y = 1/4 + (3/2)x$ (gives more weight to middle point).

### Example 11: Application - GPS

GPS receivers solve overdetermined system using least squares:
- Position $(x, y, z)$ and clock offset $\delta t$ (4 unknowns)
- Receive signals from $n \geq 4$ satellites (n equations)
- Least squares handles extra satellites and measurement errors

Each satellite equation: $ \sqrt{(x - x_i)^2 + (y - y_i)^2 + (z - z_i)^2} = c(t_i - \delta t)$

Linearize and solve via least squares.

**Answer:** More satellites = better position accuracy through least squares.

### Example 12: Condition Number

The **condition number** $\kappa(A^TA)$ measures sensitivity to errors.

Large condition number → small errors in data cause large errors in solution.

**Better**: Use QR factorization. Since $\kappa(R) = \kappa(A)$ but $\kappa(A^TA) = [\kappa(A)]^2$, QR is more stable.

Why is $\kappa(A^TA) = [\kappa(A)]^2$?

**Solution:**

Condition number relates to ratio of largest to smallest singular values.

For $A^TA$: eigenvalues are squares of singular values of $A$.

So condition number is squared.

**Answer:** Normal equations square the condition number, amplifying numerical errors.

### Example 13: Regularization

When $A^TA$ is nearly singular (ill-conditioned), add regularization:
$$ (A^TA + \lambda I)\hat{\vec{x}} = A^T\vec{b}$$

where $\lambda > 0$ is small.

This is **ridge regression** or **Tikhonov regularization**.

**Effect**: Trades exact fit for stability and smoothness.

**Answer:** Small $\lambda$ stabilizes solution at cost of slight bias.

A) In Col$(A)$
B) Orthogonal to Col$(A)$
C) Equal to $\vec{b}$
D) Always zero
E) Equal to $\hat{\vec{x}}$

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
Normal equations: $A^TA\hat{\vec{x}} = A^T\vec{b}$
Derived from orthogonality condition: error perpendicular to Col$(A)$.

**Problem 2: B**
If columns independent, then $A^TA$ is invertible.
$\hat{\vec{x}} = (A^TA)^{-1}A^T\vec{b}$ is unique.

**Problem 3: B**
For $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x$:
Design matrix $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_1 \\ 1 & x_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_m \end{bmatrix}$
First column of 1's for intercept, second column for slope.

**Problem 4: C**
From $A = QR$: $QR\hat{\vec{x}} = \vec{b}$
Multiply by $Q^T$: $R\hat{\vec{x}} = Q^T\vec{b}$
More stable than normal equations.

**Problem 5: B**
The error $\vec{r} = \vec{b} - A\hat{\vec{x}}$ is orthogonal to Col$(A)$.
This is the defining property of least squares solution.
Geometrically: error perpendicular to subspace.
