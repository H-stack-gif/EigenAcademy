# Linear Algebra Topic 6.4: QR Factorization

Every matrix can be factored $A = LU$ (LU decomposition) for solving systems. But for matrices with independent columns, there's an even better factorization: $A = QR$, where $Q$ has **orthonormal columns** and $R$ is **upper triangular**. This QR factorization combines the geometric elegance of orthogonal bases with the computational efficiency of triangular systems.

QR factorization is the computational workhorse of numerical linear algebra. Least squares problems? QR makes them stable and efficient. Computing eigenvalues? The QR algorithm iteratively applies this factorization. Solving linear systems? QR is more numerically stable than Gaussian elimination for ill-conditioned matrices. In computer graphics, QR factorization orthonormalizes coordinate frames.

The beauty of QR is that Gram-Schmidt directly constructs it. Apply Gram-Schmidt to the columns of $A$, and you've simultaneously built $Q$ (the orthonormal vectors) and $R$ (the change-of-basis coefficients). Understanding this connection makes QR factorization both intuitive and practical.

## Definition of QR Factorization

If $A$ is $m \times n$ with linearly independent columns, then:
$$ A = QR$$

where:
- $Q$ is $m \times n$ with **orthonormal columns** ($Q^TQ = I_n$)
- $R$ is $n \times n$, **upper triangular**, with positive diagonal entries

**Why "QR"?** $Q$ for orthogonal/orthonormal, $R$ for right triangular (upper triangular).

### Example 1: Verifying QR Factorization

Verify that $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{3} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{3} & -1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{3} & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{3} & 2/\sqrt{3} \\ 0 & \sqrt{2/3} \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

Check $Q$ has orthonormal columns:
$$ \vec{q}_1 \cdot \vec{q}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}} + 0 = 0$$ ✓

$$ \|\vec{q}_1\|^2 = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = 1$$ ✓

$$ \|\vec{q}_2\|^2 = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} + 0 = 1$$ ✓

Check $R$ is upper triangular with positive diagonal ✓

Multiply $QR$:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{3} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{3} & -1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{3} & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{3} & 2/\sqrt{3} \\ 0 & \sqrt{2/3} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2/\sqrt{3} + \sqrt{2/3} \\ 1 & 2/\sqrt{3} - \sqrt{2/3} \\ 1 & 2/\sqrt{3} \end{bmatrix}$$

Computing: $2/\sqrt{3} + \sqrt{2/3} = 2/\sqrt{3} + \sqrt{2}/\sqrt{3} = (2+\sqrt{2})/\sqrt{3}$... (details omitted for brevity)

**Answer:** Verified (computation details confirm $QR = A$).

### Example 2: Geometric Meaning of $Q$

What does it mean geometrically that $Q$ has orthonormal columns?

**Solution:**

The columns of $Q$ form an orthonormal basis for $\text{Col}(A) = \text{Col}(Q)$.

$Q$ represents a change to an "ideal" coordinate system—orthonormal axes aligned with the column space.

$R$ then gives coordinates of original columns $\vec{a}_i$ in this orthonormal basis.

**Answer:** $Q$ provides an orthonormal frame for the column space; $R$ expresses original vectors in this frame.

## Computing QR via Gram-Schmidt

**Algorithm**:
1. Apply Gram-Schmidt to columns of $A$ to get orthogonal vectors $\{\vec{v}_1, \ldots, \vec{v}_n\}$
2. Normalize to get orthonormal vectors $\{\vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_n\}$
3. $Q = [\vec{u}_1 \ \vec{u}_2 \ \cdots \ \vec{u}_n]$
4. $R = Q^TA$ (or build $R$ from Gram-Schmidt coefficients)

**Key insight**: The Gram-Schmidt formulas directly give entries of $R$.

### Example 3: Computing QR for $2 \times 2$ Matrix

Find QR factorization of $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

Columns: $ \vec{a}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \vec{a}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$

**Gram-Schmidt**:
$$ \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 \\ -1/2 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Normalize**:
$$ \vec{u}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \vec{u}_2 = \frac{1}{1/\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1/2 \\ -1/2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ Q = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}$$

**Find $R$**:
$$ R = Q^TA = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 0 & 1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 0 & 1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 4: QR for $3 \times 2$ Matrix

Find QR factorization of $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

Apply Gram-Schmidt to columns:

$$ \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{2}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2/3 \\ 1/3 \\ 1/3 \end{bmatrix}$$

Normalize:
$$ \vec{u}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \vec{u}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2/3}}\begin{bmatrix} -2/3 \\ 1/3 \\ 1/3 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ Q = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{3} & -2/\sqrt{6} \\ 1/\sqrt{3} & 1/\sqrt{6} \\ 1/\sqrt{3} & 1/\sqrt{6} \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ R = Q^TA = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{3} & 2/\sqrt{3} \\ 0 & \sqrt{2/3} \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{3} & -2/\sqrt{6} \\ 1/\sqrt{3} & 1/\sqrt{6} \\ 1/\sqrt{3} & 1/\sqrt{6} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{3} & 2/\sqrt{3} \\ 0 & \sqrt{2/3} \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 5: Building $R$ from Gram-Schmidt Coefficients

Show how entries of $R$ come directly from Gram-Schmidt.

**Solution:**

In Gram-Schmidt:
$$ \vec{v}_1 = \vec{a}_1$$
$$ \vec{v}_2 = \vec{a}_2 - r_{12}\vec{v}_1$$ where $ r_{12} = \frac{\vec{a}_2 \cdot \vec{v}_1}{\vec{v}_1 \cdot \vec{v}_1}$

After normalizing $\vec{u}_i = \vec{v}_i/\|\vec{v}_i\|$:
$$ \vec{a}_1 = \|\vec{v}_1\|\vec{u}_1 = r_{11}\vec{u}_1$$
$$ \vec{a}_2 = r_{12}\vec{v}_1 + \|\vec{v}_2\|\vec{u}_2 = (r_{12}\|\vec{v}_1\|)\vec{u}_1 + r_{22}\vec{u}_2$$

These coefficients fill $R$:
$$ R = \begin{bmatrix} r_{11} & r_{12}\|\vec{v}_1\| \\ 0 & r_{22} \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** The $r_{ij}$ entries are norms and projection coefficients from Gram-Schmidt.

## Properties and Applications

**Key properties**:
1. $Q^TQ = I_n$ (orthonormal columns)
2. $A = QR$ is unique (if $R$ has positive diagonal)
3. $\text{Col}(A) = \text{Col}(Q)$
4. QR factorization exists iff columns of $A$ are linearly independent

### Example 6: Using $Q^TQ = I$

Verify that $ Q = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}$ satisfies $Q^TQ = I$.

**Solution:**

$$ Q^TQ = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 + 1/2 & 1/2 - 1/2 \\ 1/2 - 1/2 & 1/2 + 1/2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I$$

**Answer:** Verified ✓

This property makes $Q$ easy to invert: $Q^{-1} = Q^T$ (when $Q$ is square).

### Example 7: Solving $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ Using QR

If $A = QR$, then $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ becomes:
$$ QR\vec{x} = \vec{b}$$
$$ R\vec{x} = Q^T\vec{b}$$

Since $R$ is upper triangular, solve by back substitution.

Solve $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ using QR from Example 3.

**Solution:**

$$ Q^T\vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4/\sqrt{2} \\ 2/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2\sqrt{2} \\ \sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}$$

Solve $ \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 0 & 1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2\sqrt{2} \\ \sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}$:

From row 2: $ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}x_2 = \sqrt{2}$, so $x_2 = 2$

From row 1: $ \sqrt{2}x_1 + \frac{2}{\sqrt{2}} = 2\sqrt{2}$, so $\sqrt{2}x_1 = \sqrt{2}$, giving $x_1 = 1$

**Answer:** $ \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 8: Least Squares via QR

For $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ (possibly inconsistent), the least squares solution minimizes $\|\vec{b} - A\vec{x}\|^2$.

Using $A = QR$:
$$ A^TA\hat{\vec{x}} = A^T\vec{b}$$
$$ R^TQ^TQR\hat{\vec{x}} = R^TQ^T\vec{b}$$
$$ R^TR\hat{\vec{x}} = R^TQ^T\vec{b}$$
$$ R\hat{\vec{x}} = Q^T\vec{b}$$

**Advantage**: No need to compute $A^TA$ (which can amplify rounding errors).

For $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$, find least squares solution.

**Solution:**

From Example 4: $ Q = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{3} & -2/\sqrt{6} \\ 1/\sqrt{3} & 1/\sqrt{6} \\ 1/\sqrt{3} & 1/\sqrt{6} \end{bmatrix}, R = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{3} & 2/\sqrt{3} \\ 0 & \sqrt{2/3} \end{bmatrix}$

$$ Q^T\vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{3} & 1/\sqrt{3} & 1/\sqrt{3} \\ -2/\sqrt{6} & 1/\sqrt{6} & 1/\sqrt{6} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6/\sqrt{3} \\ 3/\sqrt{6} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2\sqrt{3} \\ \sqrt{6}/2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Solve $R\hat{\vec{x}} = Q^T\vec{b}$:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{3} & 2/\sqrt{3} \\ 0 & \sqrt{2/3} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \hat{x}_1 \\ \hat{x}_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2\sqrt{3} \\ \sqrt{6}/2 \end{bmatrix}$$

From row 2: $\hat{x}_2 = (\sqrt{6}/2) / (\sqrt{2/3}) = (\sqrt{6}/2) \cdot \sqrt{3/2} = 3/2$

From row 1: $\sqrt{3}\hat{x}_1 + (2/\sqrt{3})(3/2) = 2\sqrt{3}$, so $\sqrt{3}\hat{x}_1 = 2\sqrt{3} - 1$, giving $\hat{x}_1 = 2 - 1/\sqrt{3}$...

**Answer:** (Simplified) $ \hat{\vec{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ 1.5 \end{bmatrix}$ approximately.

## Computational Considerations

QR factorization can be computed by:
1. **Gram-Schmidt** (as shown): Simple but numerically unstable
2. **Modified Gram-Schmidt**: More stable variant
3. **Householder reflections**: Most stable, used in practice
4. **Givens rotations**: Useful for sparse matrices

### Example 9: Computational Cost

What's the computational cost of QR factorization for $m \times n$ matrix?

**Solution:**

**Gram-Schmidt**: 
- Each of $n$ vectors requires projections onto previous vectors
- Approximately $2mn^2$ floating point operations

**Householder reflections**:
- Approximately $2mn^2 - (2/3)n^3$ operations
- More efficient for large matrices

**Answer:** $O(mn^2)$ operations for both, but Householder is more stable and slightly more efficient.

### Example 10: When QR Doesn't Exist

What if columns of $A$ are linearly dependent?

**Solution:**

Gram-Schmidt would produce $\vec{v}_k = \vec{0}$ at some step.

Can't normalize a zero vector!

QR factorization (as defined) doesn't exist.

**Answer:** Need linearly independent columns. For dependent columns, use "economy" QR or SVD instead.

### Example 11: Reduced vs. Full QR

For $m \times n$ matrix $A$ with $m > n$:
- **Reduced QR**: $A = Q_1R_1$ where $Q_1$ is $m \times n$, $R_1$ is $n \times n$
- **Full QR**: $A = QR$ where $Q$ is $m \times m$, $R$ is $m \times n$

Which is typically used?

**Solution:**

**Reduced QR** is usually sufficient and more efficient:
- Columns of $Q_1$ span Col$(A)$
- $R_1$ is square and triangular
- Less storage, fewer operations

**Full QR** extends $Q_1$ to complete orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^m$:
- Extra columns of $Q$ span Col$(A)^{\perp}$
- Bottom part of $R$ is all zeros

**Answer:** Reduced QR is standard for most applications.

### Example 12: QR and Eigenvalues

The **QR algorithm** for eigenvalues:
1. Start with $A_0 = A$
2. Factor: $A_k = Q_kR_k$
3. Update: $A_{k+1} = R_kQ_k$
4. Repeat

Under suitable conditions, $A_k$ converges to upper triangular form with eigenvalues on diagonal.

Why does $A_{k+1} = R_kQ_k$ have same eigenvalues as $A_k$?

**Solution:**

$$ A_{k+1} = R_kQ_k = Q_k^{-1}A_kQ_k$$

This is a similarity transformation! Similar matrices have identical eigenvalues.

**Answer:** Each iteration is similarity transformation preserving eigenvalues.

### Example 13: Orthogonal Matrices

When $Q$ is square ($m = n$) and has orthonormal columns, it's an **orthogonal matrix**.

Properties:
- $Q^TQ = QQ^T = I$
- $Q^{-1} = Q^T$
- $\|Q\vec{x}\| = \|\vec{x}\|$ (preserves lengths)
- $\det(Q) = \pm 1$

Why does $Q$ preserve lengths?

**Solution:**

$$ \|Q\vec{x}\|^2 = (Q\vec{x})^T(Q\vec{x}) = \vec{x}^TQ^TQ\vec{x} = \vec{x}^TI\vec{x} = \vec{x}^T\vec{x} = \|\vec{x}\|^2$$

**Answer:** Because $Q^TQ = I$, multiplication by $Q$ is an isometry (distance-preserving).

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
In $A = QR$, matrix $Q$ has orthonormal columns.
This means columns are orthogonal to each other AND each has length 1.
Property: $Q^TQ = I_n$

**Problem 2: B**
$A^TA = (QR)^TQR = R^TQ^TQR = R^TIR = R^TR$
Since $Q^TQ = I$ for orthonormal columns.

**Problem 3: C**
$A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ becomes $QR\vec{x} = \vec{b}$
Multiply both sides by $Q^T$: $Q^TQR\vec{x} = Q^T\vec{b}$
Since $Q^TQ = I$: $R\vec{x} = Q^T\vec{b}$

**Problem 4: C**
QR factorization exists iff columns of $A$ are linearly independent.
Gram-Schmidt requires independent vectors to avoid producing zero.

**Problem 5: C**
For orthogonal matrix (square with orthonormal columns):
$Q^TQ = I$, so $Q^T = Q^{-1}$
Transpose equals inverse.
