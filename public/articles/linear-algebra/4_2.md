# Linear Algebra Topic 4.2: Properties of Determinants

Determinants aren't just computational tools—they're algebraic objects with elegant properties that make complex calculations manageable. Want to find the determinant of a product? Multiply the determinants. Need the determinant of an inverse? Take the reciprocal. These properties transform tedious computations into simple manipulations.

Beyond computational convenience, determinant properties reveal deep mathematical structure. The fact that $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$ shows that determinants respect matrix multiplication in a fundamental way. The connection between determinants and invertibility—$\det(A) \neq 0$ if and only if $A$ is invertible—unifies algebraic and geometric perspectives.

Understanding these properties lets us work efficiently with determinants without computing them from scratch every time. We'll see how determinants behave under matrix operations, how they relate to invertibility, and how they connect to the structure of solutions to linear systems. These tools are essential for eigenvalue problems, differential equations, and theoretical linear algebra.

## Determinant of a Product

**Fundamental theorem**: For $n \times n$ matrices $A$ and $B$:
$$ \det(AB) = \det(A) \cdot \det(B)$$

This remarkable property says determinants turn matrix multiplication into ordinary multiplication of numbers.

**Important**: This does NOT mean $\det(A + B) = \det(A) + \det(B)$. Determinants are multiplicative, not additive.

### Example 1: Verifying the Product Property

Verify $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$ for $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and $ B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

$$ \det(A) = (2)(3) - (1)(1) = 5$$
$$ \det(B) = (1)(4) - (2)(3) = -2$$
$$ \det(A)\det(B) = (5)(-2) = -10$$

$$ AB = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 8 \\ 10 & 14 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \det(AB) = (5)(14) - (8)(10) = 70 - 80 = -10$$

**Answer:** Verified: $\det(AB) = -10 = \det(A)\det(B)$ ✓

### Example 2: Using Product Property

If $\det(A) = 3$ and $\det(B) = -2$, find $\det(AB)$ and $\det(A^2B)$.

**Solution:**

$$ \det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B) = (3)(-2) = -6$$

$$ \det(A^2B) = \det(A^2)\det(B) = \det(A)^2 \det(B) = 3^2(-2) = -18$$

(Note: $\det(A^2) = \det(AA) = \det(A)\det(A) = [\det(A)]^2$)

**Answer:** $\det(AB) = -6$, $\det(A^2B) = -18$

### Example 3: Multiple Matrix Products

If $\det(A) = 2$, $\det(B) = 3$, and $\det(C) = 5$ (all $n \times n$), find $\det(ABC)$.

**Solution:**

$$ \det(ABC) = \det(A)\det(B)\det(C) = (2)(3)(5) = 30$$

**Answer:** $\det(ABC) = 30$

## Determinant of Transpose

**Theorem**: For any square matrix $A$:
$$ \det(A^T) = \det(A)$$

The determinant is unchanged by transposition.

**Consequence**: All properties that hold for rows also hold for columns (e.g., we can expand along rows OR columns).

### Example 4: Transpose Determinant

Verify $\det(A^T) = \det(A)$ for $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 4 & 5 \\ 0 & 0 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

Upper triangular: $\det(A) = (1)(4)(6) = 24$

$$ A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 4 & 0 \\ 3 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$$

Lower triangular: $\det(A^T) = (1)(4)(6) = 24$

**Answer:** Verified: $\det(A^T) = 24 = \det(A)$ ✓

## Determinant of Inverse

**Theorem**: If $A$ is invertible:
$$ \det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det(A)}$$

**Proof**: Since $AA^{-1} = I$:
$$ \det(AA^{-1}) = \det(I)$$
$$ \det(A)\det(A^{-1}) = 1$$
$$ \det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det(A)}$$ ✓

### Example 5: Determinant of Inverse

If $\det(A) = 4$, find $\det(A^{-1})$.

**Solution:**

$$ \det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det(A)} = \frac{1}{4}$$

**Answer:** $ \det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{4}$

### Example 6: Combined Properties

If $A$ and $B$ are invertible with $\det(A) = 3$ and $\det(B) = 2$, find $\det((AB)^{-1})$.

**Solution:**

Method 1: $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$

$$ \det((AB)^{-1}) = \det(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = \det(B^{-1})\det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{6}$$

Method 2: Direct formula

$$ \det((AB)^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det(AB)} = \frac{1}{\det(A)\det(B)} = \frac{1}{(3)(2)} = \frac{1}{6}$$

**Answer:** $ \det((AB)^{-1}) = \frac{1}{6}$

## Scalar Multiplication

**Theorem**: For $n \times n$ matrix $A$ and scalar $k$:
$$ \det(kA) = k^n \det(A)$$

Each of the $n$ rows contributes one factor of $k$.

**Warning**: $\det(kA) \neq k\det(A)$ (common mistake!)

### Example 7: Scalar Multiple Determinant

If $A$ is $3 \times 3$ with $\det(A) = 5$, find $\det(3A)$.

**Solution:**

$$ \det(3A) = 3^3 \det(A) = 27(5) = 135$$

**Answer:** $\det(3A) = 135$

### Example 8: Negative of a Matrix

Find $\det(-A)$ where $A$ is $4 \times 4$ with $\det(A) = 2$.

**Solution:**

$$ \det(-A) = \det((-1)A) = (-1)^4 \det(A) = 1 \cdot 2 = 2$$

**Answer:** $\det(-A) = 2$

Note: $\det(-A) = (-1)^n\det(A)$ where $n$ is the size. For even $n$, $\det(-A) = \det(A)$.

## Invertibility and Determinants

**Fundamental theorem**: Square matrix $A$ is invertible if and only if $\det(A) \neq 0$.

**Equivalent statements**:
- $A$ is invertible
- $\det(A) \neq 0$
- $A$ is nonsingular
- $A\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ has only trivial solution
- Columns of $A$ are linearly independent
- $A$ has rank $n$ (full rank)

**If $\det(A) = 0$**: $A$ is singular (not invertible)

### Example 9: Testing Invertibility

Determine if $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$ is invertible.

**Solution:**

Notice column 2 is twice column 1 through the first two rows. Let's compute:

Expand along row 1:
$$ \det(A) = 1\begin{vmatrix} 4 & 5 \\ 6 & 7 \end{vmatrix} - 2\begin{vmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 7 \end{vmatrix} + 3\begin{vmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 6 \end{vmatrix}$$

$$ = 1(28-30) - 2(14-15) + 3(12-12) = -2 - 2(-1) + 0 = -2 + 2 = 0$$

Since $\det(A) = 0$, $A$ is not invertible.

**Answer:** Not invertible (singular).

### Example 10: Invertibility of Products

If $A$ and $B$ are $n \times n$ matrices and $\det(A) = 0$, is $AB$ invertible?

**Solution:**

$$ \det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B) = 0 \cdot \det(B) = 0$$

Since $\det(AB) = 0$, $AB$ is not invertible.

**Answer:** No, $AB$ is singular. If either factor is singular, the product is singular.

## Row Operation Effects

We saw these in Topic 4.1, but let's formalize:

**Theorem**: Let $A'$ be the matrix obtained from $A$ by:
1. **Swapping two rows**: $\det(A') = -\det(A)$
2. **Multiplying a row by $k$**: $\det(A') = k\det(A)$
3. **Adding $k$ times one row to another**: $\det(A') = \det(A)$

### Example 11: Determinant After Row Operations

If $\det(A) = 8$, find the determinant after:
a) Swapping rows 1 and 2
b) Multiplying row 3 by 5
c) Adding 3 times row 1 to row 2

**Solution:**

a) $\det = -8$ (sign change from swap)

b) $\det = 5(8) = 40$ (multiply by scalar)

c) $\det = 8$ (no change from row addition)

**Answer:** (a) $-8$, (b) $40$, (c) $8$

### Example 12: Multiple Operations

Starting with $\det(A) = 6$:
- Swap rows 1 and 3
- Multiply row 2 by 2
- Add 5 times row 1 to row 3

What's the final determinant?

**Solution:**

After swap: $\det = -6$
After multiply: $\det = 2(-6) = -12$
After addition: $\det = -12$ (no change)

**Answer:** $-12$

## Special Properties

**If a matrix has**:
- Two identical rows (or columns): $\det(A) = 0$
- A row (or column) of all zeros: $\det(A) = 0$
- One row (or column) that's a multiple of another: $\det(A) = 0$

### Example 13: Identical Rows

Find $ \det\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

Rows 1 and 3 are identical.

By property: $\det(A) = 0$

Alternatively: Row 3 minus Row 1 gives a zero row, so determinant is 0.

**Answer:** $\det(A) = 0$

## Practice Problems

**Problem 1:** If $\det(A) = 5$ and $\det(B) = -3$ (both $n \times n$), what is $\det(A^2B^{-1})$?

A) $-75$
B) $ -\frac{25}{3}$
C) $15$
D) $ \frac{25}{3}$
E) $ -\frac{75}{3}$

**Problem 2:** For any $n \times n$ matrix $A$, which is always true?

A) $\det(A + I) = \det(A) + 1$
B) $\det(2A) = 2\det(A)$
C) $\det(A^T) = \det(A)$
D) $\det(A^2) = [\det(A)]^2$
E) Both C and D

**Problem 3:** If $A$ is $5 \times 5$ with $\det(A) = 2$, what is $\det(2A)$?

A) 4
B) 10
C) 32
D) 64
E) 16

**Problem 4:** If $\det(A) = 0$, which must be FALSE?

A) $A\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ has infinitely many solutions
B) The columns of $A$ are linearly dependent
C) $A$ is invertible
D) rank$(A) < n$
E) $A$ is singular

**Problem 5:** After swapping two rows and multiplying one row by 3, a matrix has determinant 12. What was the original determinant?

A) 4
B) -4
C) 36
D) -36
E) 12

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: B**
$\det(A^2B^{-1}) = \det(A^2)\det(B^{-1}) = [\det(A)]^2 \cdot \frac{1}{\det(B)}$
$= 5^2 \cdot \frac{1}{-3} = \frac{25}{-3} = -\frac{25}{3}$

**Problem 2: E**
(A) False: determinant is not additive
(B) False: $\det(2A) = 2^n\det(A)$, not $2\det(A)$
(C) True: $\det(A^T) = \det(A)$ always
(D) True: $\det(A^2) = \det(A)\det(A) = [\det(A)]^2$
Both C and D are true.

**Problem 3: D**
$\det(2A) = 2^5 \det(A) = 32(2) = 64$
For $n \times n$ matrix, factor is $2^n$.

**Problem 4: C**
If $\det(A) = 0$, then $A$ is NOT invertible (singular).
Statement C says "$A$ is invertible" which is false when $\det(A) = 0$.
All other statements are true when $\det(A) = 0$.

**Problem 5: B**
Let original determinant be $d$.
After row swap: $-d$
After multiply by 3: $3(-d) = -3d$
We're told $-3d = 12$
So $d = -4$
