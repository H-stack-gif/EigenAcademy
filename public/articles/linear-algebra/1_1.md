# Linear Algebra Topic 1.1: Systems of Linear Equations

Three variables, three equations. At first glance, solving a system like this seems straightforward—just use substitution or elimination like you did in high school algebra. But what happens when you have five variables and four equations? Or ten variables and seven equations? What if there's no solution at all, or infinitely many solutions? These questions push us beyond the algebraic tricks of the past and into the systematic world of linear algebra.

Systems of linear equations appear everywhere: in economics when balancing supply and demand across multiple markets, in engineering when analyzing electrical circuits or structural forces, in computer graphics when transforming three-dimensional objects, and in data science when fitting models to observations. The power of linear algebra lies not just in solving these systems, but in understanding their structure—recognizing when solutions exist, when they're unique, and how to express infinite solution sets elegantly.

In this topic, we'll develop a matrix-based framework for linear systems. Rather than manipulating individual equations, we'll learn to represent entire systems compactly and leverage this representation to gain geometric and algebraic insights. This foundation will serve us throughout linear algebra, as nearly every concept we encounter connects back to solving $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$.

## What Is a Linear Equation?

A **linear equation** in variables $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ has the form:

$$ a_1x_1 + a_2x_2 + \cdots + a_nx_n = b$$

where $a_1, \ldots, a_n$ and $b$ are constants. The key property: each variable appears to the first power only, with no products like $x_1x_2$, no functions like $\sin(x_1)$, and no powers like $x_1^2$.

**Examples of linear equations:**
- $3x_1 - 2x_2 + x_3 = 7$
- $x_1 + x_2 + x_3 + x_4 = 0$
- $5x = 10$

**Examples of nonlinear equations:**
- $x_1^2 + x_2 = 4$ (contains $x_1^2$)
- $x_1x_2 = 1$ (contains product $x_1x_2$)
- $\sqrt{x_1} + x_2 = 3$ (contains $\sqrt{x_1}$)

A **system of linear equations** is a collection of one or more linear equations involving the same variables. For instance:

$$ \begin{cases}
x_1 - 2x_2 + x_3 = 0 \\
2x_2 - 8x_3 = 8 \\
5x_1 - 5x_3 = 10
\end{cases}$$

A **solution** to this system is a list of numbers $(s_1, s_2, s_3)$ that makes all three equations true when we substitute $x_1 = s_1$, $x_2 = s_2$, $x_3 = s_3$.

## Matrix Representation of Linear Systems

Rather than working with individual equations, we can encode the entire system in a matrix. Consider the system:

$$ \begin{cases}
2x_1 + 3x_2 = 7 \\
x_1 - x_2 = -1
\end{cases}$$

We extract three components:
1. **Coefficient matrix** $A$: contains the coefficients of the variables
2. **Variable vector** $\vec{x}$: lists the unknowns
3. **Constant vector** $\vec{b}$: contains the right-hand sides

For our system:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$$

The system becomes the compact matrix equation:

$$ A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$$

Here, $A\vec{x}$ represents matrix-vector multiplication (which we'll explore more in Topic 1.3). For now, understand that multiplying the matrix $A$ by the vector $\vec{x}$ produces a new vector, and we're setting that equal to $\vec{b}$.

The **augmented matrix** $[A|\vec{b}]$ combines the coefficient matrix and constant vector:

$$ [A|\vec{b}] = \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 2 & 3 & 7 \\ 1 & -1 & -1 \end{array}\right]$$

The vertical bar separates the coefficients from the constants, helping us track which numbers belong where.

### Example 1: Writing Systems in Matrix Form

Convert the system to matrix form and write the augmented matrix:
$$ \begin{cases}
x_1 + 2x_2 - x_3 = 4 \\
2x_1 - x_2 + 3x_3 = 1 \\
-x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = -2
\end{cases}$$

**Solution:**

The coefficient matrix contains the coefficients in order:
$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & -1 \\ 2 & -1 & 3 \\ -1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

The variable and constant vectors:
$$ \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** Matrix equation: $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ with the matrices above.

Augmented matrix: $ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & -1 & 4 \\ 2 & -1 & 3 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 & 1 & -2 \end{array}\right]$

### Example 2: Handling Missing Variables

Write the system in matrix form:
$$ \begin{cases}
2x_1 + x_3 = 5 \\
x_2 - 3x_3 = 2 \\
x_1 + x_2 = 1
\end{cases}$$

**Solution:**

Notice that $x_2$ is missing from the first equation, $x_1$ is missing from the second, and $x_3$ is missing from the third. We use coefficient 0 for missing variables:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -3 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 2 & 0 & 1 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 & -3 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right]$

## Consistency and Solution Types

A linear system falls into one of three categories:

1. **No solution** (inconsistent system): The equations contradict each other
2. **Exactly one solution** (consistent, independent): A unique answer exists
3. **Infinitely many solutions** (consistent, dependent): Multiple solutions exist

### Example 3: Recognizing Inconsistent Systems

Consider:
$$ \begin{cases}
x_1 + x_2 = 3 \\
x_1 + x_2 = 5
\end{cases}$$

**Solution:**

Both equations require $x_1 + x_2$ to equal something, but 3 and 5 are different numbers. No pair $(x_1, x_2)$ can satisfy both equations simultaneously.

**Answer:** This system is inconsistent (no solution).

Geometrically, each equation represents a line in the $x_1$-$x_2$ plane. These lines are parallel (same slope, different intercepts), so they never intersect.

### Example 4: System with Unique Solution

Solve by substitution:
$$ \begin{cases}
x_1 + 2x_2 = 5 \\
3x_1 - x_2 = 4
\end{cases}$$

**Solution:**

From the first equation: $x_1 = 5 - 2x_2$

Substitute into the second equation:
$$ 3(5 - 2x_2) - x_2 = 4$$
$$ 15 - 6x_2 - x_2 = 4$$
$$ -7x_2 = -11$$
$$ x_2 = \frac{11}{7}$$

Then: $ x_1 = 5 - 2\left(\frac{11}{7}\right) = 5 - \frac{22}{7} = \frac{13}{7}$

**Answer:** Unique solution: $ \left(\frac{13}{7}, \frac{11}{7}\right)$

### Example 5: System with Infinitely Many Solutions

Analyze:
$$ \begin{cases}
x_1 - 2x_2 = 3 \\
2x_1 - 4x_2 = 6
\end{cases}$$

**Solution:**

Notice the second equation is exactly twice the first equation. These are really the same constraint written twice. Any solution to the first equation automatically satisfies the second.

From $x_1 - 2x_2 = 3$, we get $x_1 = 3 + 2x_2$. 

We can choose $x_2$ freely (say, $x_2 = t$ where $t$ is any real number), and then $x_1 = 3 + 2t$.

**Answer:** Infinitely many solutions: $ \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 + 2t \\ t \end{bmatrix}$ for any real $t$.

This is called **parametric form**—we express solutions using a parameter $t$.

## Geometric Interpretation

In $\mathbb{R}^2$ (the plane), each linear equation represents a line. The solution set is where these lines intersect:
- **Two distinct lines**: intersect at one point (unique solution) or are parallel (no solution)
- **Same line**: infinitely many intersections (infinitely many solutions)

In $\mathbb{R}^3$ (three-dimensional space), each linear equation represents a plane:
- Three planes can meet at a single point (unique solution)
- Three planes can have no common point (no solution)
- Three planes can intersect along a line or lie on top of each other (infinitely many solutions)

### Example 6: Three-Variable System Interpretation

$$ \begin{cases}
x + y + z = 6 \\
2x - y + z = 3 \\
x + 2y - z = 0
\end{cases}$$

**Given:** This system (we won't solve it yet).

**Find:** Describe geometrically what we're looking for.

**Solution:**

Each equation represents a plane in three-dimensional space:
- First equation: points where coordinates sum to 6
- Second equation: points satisfying $2x - y + z = 3$
- Third equation: points satisfying $x + 2y - z = 0$

We're looking for points that lie on all three planes simultaneously—the intersection of three planes.

**Answer:** The solution is the point (or points) where all three planes meet.

## Free Variables and Parametric Solutions

When a system has infinitely many solutions, we express them using **free variables**. A free variable can take any value, and the other variables are determined by these choices.

### Example 7: Identifying Free Variables

For the system with augmented matrix (already in a nice form):
$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 3 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 5 \end{array}\right]$$

**Solution:**

Reading this augmented matrix as equations:
- Row 1: $x_1 + 3x_2 = 2$, so $x_1 = 2 - 3x_2$
- Row 2: $x_3 = 5$

Notice $x_2$ doesn't have its own equation—it can be anything. We call $x_2$ a **free variable**.

Let $x_2 = t$ (parameter). Then:
- $x_1 = 2 - 3t$
- $x_2 = t$  
- $x_3 = 5$

**Answer:** General solution in parametric form: $ \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 - 3t \\ t \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} + t\begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$

This form shows all solutions as a particular solution $ \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$ plus any multiple of the direction vector $ \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

## Homogeneous Systems

A system is **homogeneous** if all constant terms are zero: $A\vec{x} = \vec{0}$.

**Key property**: Every homogeneous system is consistent because $\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ is always a solution (called the **trivial solution**).

The interesting question: Does a homogeneous system have **nontrivial solutions** (solutions other than $\vec{0}$)?

### Example 8: Homogeneous System

Describe the solution set:
$$ \begin{cases}
x_1 + 2x_2 - x_3 = 0 \\
2x_1 + 4x_2 - 2x_3 = 0
\end{cases}$$

**Solution:**

The second equation is twice the first, so they're equivalent. We really have one equation: $x_1 + 2x_2 - x_3 = 0$

Solving for $x_1$: $x_1 = -2x_2 + x_3$

Let $x_2 = s$ and $x_3 = t$ (two free variables):

$$ \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2s + t \\ s \\ t \end{bmatrix} = s\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + t\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** Infinitely many solutions forming a plane through the origin, spanned by vectors $ \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

### Example 9: When Is the Trivial Solution the Only Solution?

For what value of $k$ does this homogeneous system have only the trivial solution?
$$ \begin{cases}
x_1 + 2x_2 = 0 \\
kx_1 - 3x_2 = 0
\end{cases}$$

**Solution:**

From the first equation: $x_1 = -2x_2$

Substitute into the second: $k(-2x_2) - 3x_2 = 0$, which gives $(-2k - 3)x_2 = 0$

If $-2k - 3 \neq 0$ (i.e., $k \neq -\frac{3}{2}$), then $x_2 = 0$, which forces $x_1 = 0$ (only trivial solution).

If $k = -\frac{3}{2}$, then $(-2k - 3)x_2 = 0$ is satisfied for any $x_2$ (infinitely many solutions).

**Answer:** $k \neq -\frac{3}{2}$ gives only the trivial solution.

### Example 10: Application - Balancing Chemical Equations

Balance the chemical equation:
$$ x_1\text{C}_3\text{H}_8 + x_2\text{O}_2 \to x_3\text{CO}_2 + x_4\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

**Solution:**

We need integer coefficients such that atoms balance. Count each element:
- Carbon: $3x_1 = x_3$
- Hydrogen: $8x_1 = 2x_4$
- Oxygen: $2x_2 = 2x_3 + x_4$

This is a homogeneous system (equals 0 when we move terms):
$$ \begin{cases}
3x_1 - x_3 = 0 \\
8x_1 - 2x_4 = 0 \\
-2x_2 + 2x_3 + x_4 = 0
\end{cases}$$

From equation 1: $x_3 = 3x_1$
From equation 2: $x_4 = 4x_1$
From equation 3: $2x_2 = 2(3x_1) + 4x_1 = 10x_1$, so $x_2 = 5x_1$

General solution: $(x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4) = (t, 5t, 3t, 4t)$ for any $t > 0$.

Choosing $t = 1$ gives integer coefficients: $(1, 5, 3, 4)$.

**Answer:** $\text{C}_3\text{H}_8 + 5\text{O}_2 \to 3\text{CO}_2 + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$

## Number of Equations vs. Number of Variables

An $m \times n$ system has $m$ equations and $n$ variables:

- If $m < n$ (fewer equations than variables): typically infinitely many solutions or no solution
- If $m = n$ (square system): may have unique solution, but not guaranteed
- If $m > n$ (more equations than variables): typically no solution (overdetermined), unless equations are compatible

These are tendencies, not rules. A system with $m < n$ could still be inconsistent (no solution) if equations contradict.

### Example 11: Two Equations, Three Variables

$$ \begin{cases}
x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 1 \\
2x_1 + 2x_2 + 2x_3 = 2
\end{cases}$$

**Solution:**

The second equation is twice the first—they're equivalent. We have essentially one equation with three variables.

From $x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 1$, let $x_2 = s$ and $x_3 = t$. Then $x_1 = 1 - s - t$.

**Answer:** Infinitely many solutions: $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 - s - t \\ s \\ t \end{bmatrix}$ for any real $s, t$.

This represents a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

## Linear Combinations Perspective

Here's another way to think about $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$. If $A$ has columns $\vec{a}_1, \ldots, \vec{a}_n$, then:

$$ A\vec{x} = x_1\vec{a}_1 + x_2\vec{a}_2 + \cdots + x_n\vec{a}_n$$

So solving $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ means: "Can we write $\vec{b}$ as a linear combination of the columns of $A$?"

- If yes, the system is consistent
- If no, the system is inconsistent

The coefficients $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ in the linear combination are the solution.

### Example 12: Linear Combination Viewpoint

Can $ \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$ be written as a linear combination of $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $ \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$?

**Solution:**

We're asking: do $x_1, x_2$ exist such that:
$$ x_1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + x_2\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

This gives the system:
$$ \begin{cases}
x_1 + 3x_2 = 7 \\
2x_1 + x_2 = 4
\end{cases}$$

From the second equation: $x_1 = \frac{4 - x_2}{2}$

Substitute: $\frac{4 - x_2}{2} + 3x_2 = 7$
$$ 4 - x_2 + 6x_2 = 14$$
$$ 5x_2 = 10$$
$$ x_2 = 2$$

Then $x_1 = \frac{4 - 2}{2} = 1$.

**Answer:** Yes, $ \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = 1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + 2\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

## Practice Problems

**Problem 1:** Which of the following is NOT a linear equation?

A) $3x_1 - 2x_2 + 5x_3 = 0$
B) $x_1 = 7$
C) $\sqrt{2}x_1 + \pi x_2 = e$
D) $x_1x_2 + x_3 = 1$
E) $x_1 - \frac{1}{3}x_2 + 2x_3 - x_4 = 10$

**Problem 2:** A system of linear equations is given by the augmented matrix $ \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 2 & 5 \\ 2 & 4 & 7 \end{array}\right]$. What can we conclude about the solution?

A) The system has a unique solution
B) The system has infinitely many solutions
C) The system has no solution
D) More information is needed
E) The system has exactly two solutions

**Problem 3:** How many free variables does the system with augmented matrix $ \left[\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 0 & 3 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 & 0 & 5 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & -3 \end{array}\right]$ have?

A) 0
B) 1
C) 2
D) 3
E) 4

**Problem 4:** For a homogeneous system $A\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ where $A$ is $3 \times 5$, which statement must be true?

A) The system has only the trivial solution
B) The system has infinitely many solutions
C) The system has exactly three solutions
D) The system could have no solution
E) The system has a unique nontrivial solution

**Problem 5:** The system $ \begin{cases} x + 2y = 3 \\ 3x + ky = 9 \end{cases}$ has infinitely many solutions. What is $k$?

A) 3
B) 6
C) 9
D) 2
E) No such value exists

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: D**
$x_1x_2 + x_3 = 1$ contains the product $x_1x_2$, violating linearity.
All other options have variables appearing only to the first power with no products or nonlinear functions.

**Problem 2: C**
Row 1: $x_1 + 2x_2 = 5$
Row 2: $2x_1 + 4x_2 = 7$
The second equation is the first multiplied by 2, which would give $2x_1 + 4x_2 = 10$.
But the augmented matrix shows $2x_1 + 4x_2 = 7$.
This is a contradiction: $10 \neq 7$.
No solution exists.

**Problem 3: B**
Variables $x_1, x_2, x_4$ are basic (have leading 1's in their columns).
Variable $x_3$ is free (no leading 1 in its column).
One free variable.

**Problem 4: B**
A is $3 \times 5$ means 3 equations, 5 variables.
With more variables than equations, there must be free variables (at least $5 - 3 = 2$ free variables).
Free variables give infinitely many solutions.
The trivial solution always exists, plus nontrivial solutions.

**Problem 5: B**
From equation 1: $x = 3 - 2y$
Substitute: $3(3 - 2y) + ky = 9$
$9 - 6y + ky = 9$
$(k - 6)y = 0$
For infinitely many solutions, this must hold for all $y$, so $k - 6 = 0$.
$k = 6$
