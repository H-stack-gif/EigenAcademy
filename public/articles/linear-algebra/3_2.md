# Linear Algebra Topic 3.2: Kernel and Range

Every function has a domain and codomain, but for transformations, we care about two other sets: which inputs map to zero, and which outputs are actually hit. These are the **kernel** and **range**, and they reveal the transformation's structure completely.

The kernel tells us about lost information—which inputs become indistinguishable after transformation. A rotation has trivial kernel (only zero maps to zero), but a projection loses an entire dimension. The range tells us about coverage—which outputs are achievable. Some transformations fill their entire codomain; others produce only a lower-dimensional subset.

These concepts connect directly to solving equations. The equation $T(\vec{x}) = \vec{b}$ has solutions if and only if $\vec{b}$ is in the range. Multiple solutions exist if the kernel is nontrivial. Understanding kernel and range means understanding when transformations are reversible, when they're one-to-one, when they're onto, and how to characterize their solution sets completely.

## The Kernel (Null Space)

The **kernel** (or **null space**) of linear transformation $T: V \to W$ is:
$$ \ker(T) = \{\vec{v} \in V : T(\vec{v}) = \vec{0}\}$$

The kernel is the set of all inputs that map to the zero vector in $W$.

**Key fact**: $\ker(T)$ is a subspace of $V$ (the domain).

**Proof of subspace**:
- Contains $\vec{0}$: $T(\vec{0}) = \vec{0}$, so $\vec{0} \in \ker(T)$ ✓
- Closed under addition: If $T(\vec{u}) = \vec{0}$ and $T(\vec{v}) = \vec{0}$, then $T(\vec{u} + \vec{v}) = T(\vec{u}) + T(\vec{v}) = \vec{0} + \vec{0} = \vec{0}$ ✓
- Closed under scalar multiplication: If $T(\vec{u}) = \vec{0}$, then $T(c\vec{u}) = cT(\vec{u}) = c\vec{0} = \vec{0}$ ✓

### Example 1: Finding the Kernel

Find $\ker(T)$ for $T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ defined by $ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} x + 2y - z \\ 2x + 4y - 2z \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

Need to solve $T(\vec{v}) = \vec{0}$:
$$ \begin{bmatrix} x + 2y - z \\ 2x + 4y - 2z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

This gives:
$$ \begin{cases} x + 2y - z = 0 \\ 2x + 4y - 2z = 0 \end{cases}$$

The second equation is twice the first, so we have one equation: $x + 2y - z = 0$, giving $x = -2y + z$.

Free variables: $y = s$, $z = t$

$$ \ker(T) = \left\{\begin{bmatrix} -2s + t \\ s \\ t \end{bmatrix} : s, t \in \mathbb{R}\right\} = \text{Span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$$

**Answer:** $\ker(T)$ is a 2-dimensional subspace (plane through origin) in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

### Example 2: Kernel of Matrix Transformation

For matrix $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & -1 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 \\ -1 & -2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, find $\ker(T)$ where $T(\vec{x}) = A\vec{x}$.

**Solution:**

$\ker(T) = \text{Nul}(A)$. Solve $A\vec{x} = \vec{0}$:

Row reduce:
$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & -1 & 0 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & -2 & 2 & 0 \end{array}\right] \to \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array}\right]$$

From row 2: $z = 0$
From row 1: $x + 2y = 0$, so $x = -2y$
Free variable: $y = t$

$$ \ker(T) = \text{Span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$$

**Answer:** $\ker(T)$ is a line through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

### Example 3: Kernel of Differentiation

Find the kernel of $D: P_3 \to P_2$ defined by $D(p) = p'$ (derivative).

**Solution:**

$\ker(D) = \{p \in P_3 : p' = 0\}$

Polynomials with zero derivative are constant polynomials.

**Answer:** $\ker(D) = \{c : c \in \mathbb{R}\} =$ the set of constant polynomials (1-dimensional subspace of $P_3$).

## The Range (Image)

The **range** (or **image**) of linear transformation $T: V \to W$ is:
$$ \text{range}(T) = \{T(\vec{v}) : \vec{v} \in V\} = \{\vec{w} \in W : \vec{w} = T(\vec{v}) \text{ for some } \vec{v} \in V\}$$

The range is the set of all possible outputs.

**Key fact**: range$(T)$ is a subspace of $W$ (the codomain).

For matrix transformation $T(\vec{x}) = A\vec{x}$: range$(T) = \text{Col}(A)$.

### Example 4: Finding the Range

Find range$(T)$ for $T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ defined by $ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} x + y \\ 2x + 2y \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

The standard matrix is:
$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

range$(T) = \text{Col}(A) = \text{Span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$

The second column equals the first, and the third is zero, so:

$$ \text{range}(T) = \text{Span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$$

**Answer:** range$(T)$ is a line through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^2$ (all vectors of form $ \begin{bmatrix} t \\ 2t \end{bmatrix}$).

### Example 5: Range of Projection

Find the range of the projection $T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ onto the $xy$-plane, defined by $ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

The standard matrix is:
$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

The range consists of all outputs $ \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, which is exactly the $xy$-plane.

**Answer:** $ \text{range}(T) = \left\{\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} : x, y \in \mathbb{R}\right\} = \text{Span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$

### Example 6: Range of Differentiation

Find the range of $D: P_3 \to P_2$ defined by $D(p) = p'$.

**Solution:**

For any polynomial $q(x) \in P_2$ (degree $\leq 2$), we can find $p \in P_3$ with $p' = q$ by integration:

If $q(x) = a + bx + cx^2$, then $p(x) = ax + \frac{b}{2}x^2 + \frac{c}{3}x^3$ (plus any constant).

Since $p \in P_3$, every polynomial in $P_2$ is the derivative of some polynomial in $P_3$.

**Answer:** range$(D) = P_2$ (all of $P_2$). The transformation is **onto**.

## One-to-One and Onto

A linear transformation $T: V \to W$ is:

**One-to-one** (injective) if $T(\vec{u}) = T(\vec{v})$ implies $\vec{u} = \vec{v}$.
- Equivalently: $\ker(T) = \{\vec{0}\}$
- No two distinct inputs map to the same output

**Onto** (surjective) if range$(T) = W$.
- Equivalently: For every $\vec{w} \in W$, there exists $\vec{v} \in V$ with $T(\vec{v}) = \vec{w}$
- Every possible output is actually achieved

### Example 7: Testing One-to-One

Determine if $T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ from Example 1 is one-to-one.

**Solution:**

From Example 1, $\ker(T)$ is a 2-dimensional subspace, not just $\{\vec{0}\}$.

Since $\ker(T) \neq \{\vec{0}\}$, the transformation is NOT one-to-one.

For instance, $ \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ are different inputs that both map to $\vec{0}$.

**Answer:** Not one-to-one.

### Example 8: Testing Onto

Determine if $T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ from Example 4 is onto.

**Solution:**

From Example 4, range$(T)$ is a 1-dimensional line in $\mathbb{R}^2$.

Since range$(T) \neq \mathbb{R}^2$ (a 1D line is not all of 2D space), $T$ is NOT onto.

For instance, $ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ is not in the range (would require $2t = 0$ and $t = 1$, contradiction).

**Answer:** Not onto.

### Example 9: One-to-One and Onto

Determine if $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ defined by $ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} x + y \\ x - y \end{bmatrix}$ is one-to-one and/or onto.

**Solution:**

Standard matrix: $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$

**One-to-one**: Check if $\ker(T) = \{\vec{0}\}$

Solve $A\vec{x} = \vec{0}$:
$$ \begin{cases} x + y = 0 \\ x - y = 0 \end{cases}$$

Adding: $2x = 0$, so $x = 0$
Then $y = 0$

Only trivial solution, so $\ker(T) = \{\vec{0}\}$. One-to-one ✓

**Onto**: Check if range$(T) = \mathbb{R}^2$

The matrix has 2 pivot columns (can verify: $\det(A) = -1 - 1 = -2 \neq 0$).

Since rank$(A) = 2$ and codomain is $\mathbb{R}^2$, we have range$(T) = \mathbb{R}^2$. Onto ✓

**Answer:** Both one-to-one and onto. $T$ is invertible.

## Rank-Nullity Theorem for Transformations

For linear transformation $T: V \to W$ where $V$ is finite-dimensional:

$$ \dim(\ker(T)) + \dim(\text{range}(T)) = \dim(V)$$

This is the same rank-nullity theorem from Unit 2, now stated for general transformations.

### Example 10: Using Rank-Nullity

If $T: \mathbb{R}^7 \to \mathbb{R}^4$ is linear with $\dim(\ker(T)) = 3$, what is $\dim(\text{range}(T))$?

**Solution:**

By rank-nullity:
$$ \dim(\ker(T)) + \dim(\text{range}(T)) = 7$$
$$ 3 + \dim(\text{range}(T)) = 7$$
$$ \dim(\text{range}(T)) = 4$$

**Answer:** $\dim(\text{range}(T)) = 4$. Since the codomain is $\mathbb{R}^4$, this means range$(T) = \mathbb{R}^4$, so $T$ is onto.

### Example 11: Implications of Dimensions

Can a linear transformation $T: \mathbb{R}^5 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ be one-to-one?

**Solution:**

If $T$ is one-to-one, then $\ker(T) = \{\vec{0}\}$, so $\dim(\ker(T)) = 0$.

By rank-nullity:
$$ 0 + \dim(\text{range}(T)) = 5$$

So $\dim(\text{range}(T)) = 5$.

But range$(T) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$, and subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^3$ have dimension at most 3.

This is a contradiction.

**Answer:** No, $T$ cannot be one-to-one. Any transformation from higher to lower dimensions must have a nontrivial kernel.

### Example 12: When Can T Be Onto?

For which values of $k$ can there exist an onto linear transformation $T: \mathbb{R}^k \to \mathbb{R}^5$?

**Solution:**

If $T$ is onto, then range$(T) = \mathbb{R}^5$, so $\dim(\text{range}(T)) = 5$.

By rank-nullity:
$$ \dim(\ker(T)) + 5 = k$$

So $\dim(\ker(T)) = k - 5$.

Since dimensions are non-negative: $k - 5 \geq 0$, giving $k \geq 5$.

**Answer:** $k \geq 5$. Need at least as many inputs as outputs for onto transformations.

## Solving $T(\vec{x}) = \vec{b}$

The equation $T(\vec{x}) = \vec{b}$ has:
- **No solution** if $\vec{b} \notin \text{range}(T)$
- **Unique solution** if $\vec{b} \in \text{range}(T)$ and $\ker(T) = \{\vec{0}\}$
- **Infinitely many solutions** if $\vec{b} \in \text{range}(T)$ and $\ker(T) \neq \{\vec{0}\}$

If $\vec{x}_p$ is a particular solution and $\ker(T) = \text{Span}\{\vec{v}_1, \ldots, \vec{v}_k\}$, then the general solution is:
$$ \vec{x} = \vec{x}_p + c_1\vec{v}_1 + \cdots + c_k\vec{v}_k$$

### Example 13: Solution Structure

For $T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ with $ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} x - y + 2z \\ 2x - 2y + 4z \end{bmatrix}$, solve $ T(\vec{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

System:
$$ \begin{cases} x - y + 2z = 3 \\ 2x - 2y + 4z = 6 \end{cases}$$

Second equation is twice the first. One equation: $x - y + 2z = 3$.

Particular solution (set $y = z = 0$): $ \vec{x}_p = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$

Find kernel (from $x - y + 2z = 0$):
$x = y - 2z$, with free variables $y = s$, $z = t$

$$ \ker(T) = \text{Span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$$

**Answer:** General solution:
$$ \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + s\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + t\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

## Practice Problems

**Problem 1:** If $T: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is one-to-one, which must be true?

A) $m = n$
B) $m > n$
C) $m \geq n$
D) $n \geq m$
E) $\ker(T) = V$

**Problem 2:** For linear transformation $T: \mathbb{R}^4 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ with $\dim(\text{range}(T)) = 2$, what is $\dim(\ker(T))$?

A) 1
B) 2
C) 3
D) 4
E) 5

**Problem 3:** The kernel of transformation $ T\left(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} x + y \\ y + z \end{bmatrix}$ is:

A) $ \text{Span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$

B) $ \text{Span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$

C) $ \text{Span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$

D) $\{\vec{0}\}$

E) All of $\mathbb{R}^3$

**Problem 4:** If $T: V \to W$ is onto, which statement must be true?

A) $\dim(V) = \dim(W)$
B) $\dim(V) \geq \dim(W)$
C) $\dim(V) \leq \dim(W)$
D) $\ker(T) = \{\vec{0}\}$
E) $T$ is one-to-one

**Problem 5:** For matrix transformation $T(\vec{x}) = A\vec{x}$ where $A$ is $3 \times 5$ with rank 3, which is true?

A) $T$ is one-to-one
B) $T$ is onto
C) $\dim(\ker(T)) = 3$
D) $\text{range}(T) = \mathbb{R}^5$
E) $\ker(T) = \mathbb{R}^3$

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
If $T$ is one-to-one, then $\ker(T) = \{\vec{0}\}$, so $\dim(\ker(T)) = 0$.
By rank-nullity: $0 + \dim(\text{range}(T)) = n$
So $\dim(\text{range}(T)) = n$
Since range$(T) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$, we need $n \leq m$, i.e., $m \geq n$.

**Problem 2: B**
Rank-nullity: $\dim(\ker(T)) + \dim(\text{range}(T)) = \dim(\mathbb{R}^4) = 4$
$\dim(\ker(T)) + 2 = 4$
$\dim(\ker(T)) = 2$

**Problem 3: B**
Solve $x + y = 0$ and $y + z = 0$:
From first: $y = -x$
From second: $z = -y = x$
So $ \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ -x \\ x \end{bmatrix} = x\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

**Problem 4: B**
If $T$ is onto, then range$(T) = W$, so $\dim(\text{range}(T)) = \dim(W)$.
By rank-nullity: $\dim(\ker(T)) + \dim(W) = \dim(V)$
So $\dim(V) = \dim(\ker(T)) + \dim(W) \geq \dim(W)$ (since $\dim(\ker(T)) \geq 0$)

**Problem 5: B**
rank$(A) = 3$ means $\dim(\text{Col}(A)) = \dim(\text{range}(T)) = 3$
Since codomain is $\mathbb{R}^3$ and range has dimension 3, range$(T) = \mathbb{R}^3$
So $T$ is onto.
(A) False: $\dim(\ker(T)) = 5 - 3 = 2 \neq 0$, not one-to-one
(C) False: $\dim(\ker(T)) = 2$
