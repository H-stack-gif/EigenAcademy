# Linear Algebra Topic 6.3: Gram-Schmidt Process

An arbitrary basis might have vectors pointing in all directions at strange angles. But an **orthogonal basis** has vectors perpendicular to each other, making calculations trivial. The **Gram-Schmidt process** is an algorithm that converts any basis into an orthogonal (or orthonormal) basis for the same subspace, preserving the span while gaining computational advantages.

The idea is brilliantly simple: take the first vector as-is, then for each subsequent vector, subtract off its projection onto the space spanned by previous vectors. What remains is perpendicular to everything before. This systematic "orthogonalization" appears in QR factorization, numerical algorithms, signal processing (creating orthogonal wavelets), and quantum mechanics (constructing orthonormal energy eigenstates).

Understanding Gram-Schmidt means understanding how to create the "ideal" coordinate system for any subspace. We'll develop the algorithm step-by-step, see how to implement it efficiently, and explore why orthogonal bases make so many problems easier. This process is fundamental to computational linear algebra and its applications.

## The Gram-Schmidt Process

**Input**: Linearly independent set $\{\vec{x}_1, \vec{x}_2, \ldots, \vec{x}_p\}$

**Output**: Orthogonal set $\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, \ldots, \vec{v}_p\}$ with same span

**Algorithm**:
$$ \vec{v}_1 = \vec{x}_1$$

$$ \vec{v}_2 = \vec{x}_2 - \frac{\vec{x}_2 \cdot \vec{v}_1}{\vec{v}_1 \cdot \vec{v}_1}\vec{v}_1$$

$$ \vec{v}_3 = \vec{x}_3 - \frac{\vec{x}_3 \cdot \vec{v}_1}{\vec{v}_1 \cdot \vec{v}_1}\vec{v}_1 - \frac{\vec{x}_3 \cdot \vec{v}_2}{\vec{v}_2 \cdot \vec{v}_2}\vec{v}_2$$

**General step**:
$$ \vec{v}_k = \vec{x}_k - \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \frac{\vec{x}_k \cdot \vec{v}_i}{\vec{v}_i \cdot \vec{v}_i}\vec{v}_i$$

**Interpretation**: At each step, subtract off the projection onto the space spanned by previous orthogonal vectors.

### Example 1: Gram-Schmidt in $\mathbb{R}^2$

Apply Gram-Schmidt to $ \left\{\vec{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \vec{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$.

**Solution:**

**Step 1**: $ \vec{v}_1 = \vec{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

**Step 2**: 
$$ \vec{v}_2 = \vec{x}_2 - \frac{\vec{x}_2 \cdot \vec{v}_1}{\vec{v}_1 \cdot \vec{v}_1}\vec{v}_1$$

$$ \vec{x}_2 \cdot \vec{v}_1 = (1)(3) + (2)(1) = 5$$

$$ \vec{v}_1 \cdot \vec{v}_1 = 9 + 1 = 10$$

$$ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{5}{10}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 1.5 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.5 \\ 1.5 \end{bmatrix}$$

Or cleaner: $ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$ (multiply by 2)

Verify orthogonality: $(3)(-1) + (1)(3) = -3 + 3 = 0$ ✓

**Answer:** Orthogonal basis: $ \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$

### Example 2: Gram-Schmidt in $\mathbb{R}^3$

Apply Gram-Schmidt to $ \left\{\vec{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \vec{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \vec{x}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$.

**Solution:**

**Step 1**: $ \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

**Step 2**:
$$ \vec{x}_2 \cdot \vec{v}_1 = 1 + 0 + 1 = 2, \quad \vec{v}_1 \cdot \vec{v}_1 = 3$$

$$ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{2}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 \\ -2/3 \\ 1/3 \end{bmatrix}$$

Multiply by 3: $ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

**Step 3**:
$$ \vec{x}_3 \cdot \vec{v}_1 = 0 + 1 + 2 = 3, \quad \vec{x}_3 \cdot \vec{v}_2 = 0 - 2 + 2 = 0$$

$$ \vec{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{3}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{0}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Verify orthogonality:
- $\vec{v}_1 \cdot \vec{v}_2 = 1 - 2 + 1 = 0$ ✓
- $\vec{v}_1 \cdot \vec{v}_3 = -1 + 0 + 1 = 0$ ✓  
- $\vec{v}_2 \cdot \vec{v}_3 = -1 + 0 + 1 = 0$ ✓

**Answer:** $ \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$

### Example 3: Why Start with $\vec{v}_1 = \vec{x}_1$?

Explain why the first step is simply $\vec{v}_1 = \vec{x}_1$.

**Solution:**

There's nothing to project onto yet! The first vector defines the first direction.

All subsequent vectors are made orthogonal to this and to each other by subtracting projections.

**Answer:** We need a starting point. The first vector becomes the first axis of our orthogonal system.

## Orthonormalization

To get an **orthonormal** basis, normalize each orthogonal vector:
$$ \vec{u}_i = \frac{\vec{v}_i}{\|\vec{v}_i\|}$$

This gives $\{\vec{u}_1, \vec{u}_2, \ldots, \vec{u}_p\}$ with:
- Orthogonal: $\vec{u}_i \cdot \vec{u}_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$
- Unit length: $\|\vec{u}_i\| = 1$ for all $i$

### Example 4: Creating Orthonormal Basis

Orthonormalize the basis from Example 1.

**Solution:**

From Example 1: $ \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$

$$ \|\vec{v}_1\| = \sqrt{9 + 1} = \sqrt{10}$$

$$ \vec{u}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3/\sqrt{10} \\ 1/\sqrt{10} \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \|\vec{v}_2\| = \sqrt{1 + 9} = \sqrt{10}$$

$$ \vec{u}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1/\sqrt{10} \\ 3/\sqrt{10} \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** Orthonormal basis: $ \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 3/\sqrt{10} \\ 1/\sqrt{10} \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -1/\sqrt{10} \\ 3/\sqrt{10} \end{bmatrix}\right\}$

### Example 5: Orthonormal Basis for $\mathbb{R}^3$

Orthonormalize the basis from Example 2.

**Solution:**

$$ \|\vec{v}_1\| = \sqrt{3}, \quad \vec{u}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \|\vec{v}_2\| = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 1} = \sqrt{6}, \quad \vec{u}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \|\vec{v}_3\| = \sqrt{1 + 0 + 1} = \sqrt{2}, \quad \vec{u}_3 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** Orthonormal basis with vectors having lengths 1.

## Gram-Schmidt for Subspaces

Gram-Schmidt constructs orthogonal basis for $W = \text{Span}\{\vec{x}_1, \ldots, \vec{x}_p\}$.

**Key property**: At each step $k$:
$$ \text{Span}\{\vec{v}_1, \ldots, \vec{v}_k\} = \text{Span}\{\vec{x}_1, \ldots, \vec{x}_k\}$$

### Example 6: Orthogonal Basis for Column Space

Find an orthogonal basis for $ \text{Col}(A)$ where $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

Columns: $ \vec{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \vec{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \vec{x}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

Apply Gram-Schmidt:

$$ \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{2}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2/3 \\ 1/3 \\ 1/3 \end{bmatrix}$$

Multiply by 3: $ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

$$ \vec{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{-1}{6}\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 \\ 1/3 \\ 1/3 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 \\ -1/6 \\ -1/6 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1/2 \\ 1/2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Multiply by 2: $ \vec{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

**Answer:** Orthogonal basis for Col$(A)$: $ \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$

### Example 7: Orthogonal Basis for Plane

Find orthogonal basis for plane $x + 2y - z = 0$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

**Solution:**

First find any basis. Set $y = 1, z = 0$: gives $x = -2$, so $ \vec{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$

Set $y = 0, z = 1$: gives $x = 1$, so $ \vec{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

Apply Gram-Schmidt:

$$ \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{-2}{5}\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -4/5 \\ 2/5 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/5 \\ 2/5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Multiply by 5: $ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$

**Answer:** $ \left\{\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$

## Modified Gram-Schmidt

The **classical Gram-Schmidt** (as described above) can have numerical stability issues.

**Modified Gram-Schmidt** recomputes projections at each step for better accuracy:
- After computing $\vec{v}_k$, update all remaining $\vec{x}_i$ by subtracting their projection onto $\vec{v}_k$
- More stable for computer implementation

### Example 8: Numerical Stability Issue

Why might classical Gram-Schmidt have numerical errors?

**Solution:**

When computing $\vec{v}_k$, we subtract many projections: $ \vec{v}_k = \vec{x}_k - \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \text{proj}_{\vec{v}_i}(\vec{x}_k)$

Each projection involves division, multiplication, and subtraction—rounding errors accumulate.

If $\vec{x}_k$ is nearly in span of $\{\vec{v}_1, \ldots, \vec{v}_{k-1}\}$, $\vec{v}_k$ becomes very small, amplifying relative error.

**Answer:** Accumulated rounding errors can make vectors that should be orthogonal slightly non-orthogonal.

Modified Gram-Schmidt mitigates this by updating vectors sequentially.

## Applying Gram-Schmidt to Functions

Inner products work for function spaces too: $ \langle f, g \rangle = \int_a^b f(x)g(x)\,dx$

Gram-Schmidt orthogonalizes functions!

### Example 9: Orthogonalizing Polynomials on $[-1, 1]$

Apply Gram-Schmidt to $\{1, x, x^2\}$ on $[-1, 1]$ with $\langle f, g \rangle = \int_{-1}^1 f(x)g(x)\,dx$.

**Solution:**

$p_0(x) = 1$

For $p_1(x)$:
$$ \langle x, 1 \rangle = \int_{-1}^1 x \cdot 1\,dx = 0$$

So $p_1(x) = x$ is already orthogonal to $p_0(x) = 1$.

For $p_2(x)$:
$$ \langle x^2, 1 \rangle = \int_{-1}^1 x^2\,dx = \frac{2}{3}$$

$$ \langle 1, 1 \rangle = \int_{-1}^1 1\,dx = 2$$

$$ \langle x^2, x \rangle = \int_{-1}^1 x^3\,dx = 0$$

$$ p_2(x) = x^2 - \frac{2/3}{2} \cdot 1 = x^2 - \frac{1}{3}$$

**Answer:** Orthogonal polynomials: $\{1, x, x^2 - 1/3\}$ (related to Legendre polynomials!)

### Example 10: Fourier Series Connection

The functions $\{1, \cos(x), \sin(x), \cos(2x), \sin(2x), \ldots\}$ are orthogonal on $[0, 2\pi]$ with inner product $\langle f, g \rangle = \int_0^{2\pi} f(x)g(x)\,dx$.

Verify: $\langle \cos(x), \sin(x) \rangle = 0$.

**Solution:**

$$ \int_0^{2\pi} \cos(x)\sin(x)\,dx = \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{1}{2}\sin(2x)\,dx = \left[-\frac{1}{4}\cos(2x)\right]_0^{2\pi} = 0$$

**Answer:** Orthogonal ✓

This orthogonality is the foundation of Fourier series—decomposing functions into orthogonal trigonometric components.

### Example 11: Why Orthogonal Bases Matter

Compare finding coordinates in a standard basis vs. orthonormal basis.

**Solution:**

**Standard basis** (may not be orthogonal):
- Solve system $A\vec{c} = \vec{y}$ where $A = [\vec{b}_1 \ \cdots \ \vec{b}_n]$
- Requires row reduction or matrix inversion: $O(n^3)$ operations

**Orthonormal basis** $\{\vec{u}_1, \ldots, \vec{u}_n\}$:
- Coordinates: $c_i = \vec{y} \cdot \vec{u}_i$
- Just $n$ dot products: $O(n^2)$ operations
- No solving systems!

**Answer:** Orthonormal bases make coordinate computation trivial—just compute dot products.

### Example 12: Uniqueness

Is the orthogonal basis produced by Gram-Schmidt unique?

**Solution:**

No! The result depends on:
1. Order of input vectors
2. Choice of scalar multiples (we can scale each $\vec{v}_i$)

But all orthogonal bases for $W$ are related by scaling and potentially reordering.

**Answer:** Not unique, but any two orthogonal bases for $W$ span the same space and have the same dimension.

### Example 13: When Gram-Schmidt Fails

What happens if the input vectors are linearly dependent?

**Solution:**

If $\vec{x}_k \in \text{Span}\{\vec{x}_1, \ldots, \vec{x}_{k-1}\}$, then:
$$ \vec{v}_k = \vec{x}_k - \text{proj}_{\text{Span}\{\vec{v}_1, \ldots, \vec{v}_{k-1}\}}(\vec{x}_k) = \vec{0}$$

Subtracting the projection removes everything!

**Answer:** Gram-Schmidt produces $\vec{0}$ when it encounters a dependent vector. This can be used to detect dependence.

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
$ \vec{v}_2 = \vec{x}_2 - \frac{\vec{x}_2 \cdot \vec{v}_1}{\vec{v}_1 \cdot \vec{v}_1}\vec{v}_1$
Subtract the projection of $\vec{x}_2$ onto $\vec{v}_1$ to make them orthogonal.

**Problem 2: B**
At each step $k$, Span$\{\vec{v}_1, \ldots, \vec{v}_k\} = $ Span$\{\vec{x}_1, \ldots, \vec{x}_k\}$
So Span$\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2\} = $ Span$\{\vec{x}_1, \vec{x}_2\}$

**Problem 3: B**
Orthogonal → Orthonormal by normalizing: $\vec{u}_i = \vec{v}_i/\|\vec{v}_i\|$
Makes each vector unit length while preserving orthogonality.

**Problem 4: B**
$ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{1}{1}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
This is orthogonal to $\vec{v}_1$ (horizontal ⊥ vertical).

**Problem 5: C**
Gram-Schmidt requires linearly independent input.
If vectors are dependent, the process produces $\vec{0}$ at some step.
(Orthogonality and normalization are outputs, not inputs)
