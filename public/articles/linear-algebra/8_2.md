# Linear Algebra Topic 8.2: Singular Value Decomposition

Every symmetric matrix can be diagonalized: $A = PDP^T$. But what about **non-square** or **non-symmetric** matrices? The **Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)** is the answer: every matrix—any size, any shape—can be factored as $A = U\Sigma V^T$ where $U$ and $V$ are orthogonal and $\Sigma$ is diagonal. This is one of the most important factorizations in all of linear algebra.

SVD reveals the fundamental structure of any linear transformation: input space has orthonormal basis (columns of $V$), output space has orthonormal basis (columns of $U$), and $A$ simply scales along these aligned directions (diagonal entries of $\Sigma$). These **singular values** measure how much $A$ stretches different directions. Large singular values indicate important directions; small ones indicate noise or redundancy.

Applications are everywhere. Netflix uses SVD to recommend movies (collaborative filtering). Google uses it for web search (latent semantic analysis). Scientists use it for image compression, noise reduction, and solving least squares. The **pseudoinverse** $A^+ = V\Sigma^+U^T$ solves inconsistent systems optimally. Understanding SVD means understanding how to decompose any matrix into its essential components.

## Definition of SVD

For $m \times n$ matrix $A$, the **singular value decomposition** is:
$$ A = U\Sigma V^T$$

where:
- $U$ is $m \times m$ **orthogonal** ($U^TU = I$): **left singular vectors**
- $\Sigma$ is $m \times n$ **diagonal** with $\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_r > 0$ on diagonal: **singular values**
- $V$ is $n \times n$ **orthogonal** ($V^TV = I$): **right singular vectors**
- $r = \text{rank}(A)$ (number of nonzero singular values)

**Key difference from eigendecomposition**: $U$ and $V$ are generally different (except for symmetric $A$).

### Example 1: SVD Structure

For $3 \times 2$ matrix, what are the dimensions of $U$, $\Sigma$, $V$?

**Solution:**

$A$ is $3 \times 2$

$U$ is $3 \times 3$ (square, orthogonal)

$\Sigma$ is $3 \times 2$ (same shape as $A$, diagonal in upper-left)

$V$ is $2 \times 2$ (square, orthogonal)

$$ \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma_2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $U$ is $3 \times 3$, $\Sigma$ is $3 \times 2$, $V$ is $2 \times 2$.

## Computing SVD via $A^TA$ and $AA^T$

**Key relationships**:
1. $V$ = eigenvectors of $A^TA$ (eigenvectors are right singular vectors)
2. $U$ = eigenvectors of $AA^T$ (eigenvectors are left singular vectors)
3. $\sigma_i^2$ = eigenvalues of $A^TA$ (or $AA^T$)
4. $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$ where $\lambda_i$ are eigenvalues of $A^TA$

**Why this works**: 
$$ A^TA = (U\Sigma V^T)^T(U\Sigma V^T) = V\Sigma^TU^TU\Sigma V^T = V(\Sigma^T\Sigma)V^T$$

Since $\Sigma^T\Sigma$ is diagonal with $\sigma_i^2$ entries, $V$ diagonalizes $A^TA$.

### Example 2: Computing SVD for $2 \times 2$

Find SVD of $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

**Step 1**: Compute $A^TA$:
$$ A^TA = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 9 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 9$, $\lambda_2 = 1$

Eigenvectors: $ \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $ \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

$$ V = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I$$

**Step 2**: Singular values:
$$ \sigma_1 = \sqrt{9} = 3, \quad \sigma_2 = \sqrt{1} = 1$$

$$ \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Step 3**: Compute $U$ from $AA^T$ (or use $\vec{u}_i = A\vec{v}_i/\sigma_i$):

$$ \vec{u}_1 = \frac{A\vec{v}_1}{\sigma_1} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \vec{u}_2 = \frac{A\vec{v}_2}{\sigma_2} = \frac{1}{1}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I$$

**Answer:** $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$

(For diagonal matrix, SVD is trivial: already in diagonal form.)

### Example 3: Non-Square Matrix SVD

Find SVD of $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Solution:**

**Step 1**: $A^TA$:
$$ A^TA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Eigenvalues: $\det(A^TA - \lambda I) = (1-\lambda)^2 - 1 = \lambda^2 - 2\lambda = \lambda(\lambda - 2) = 0$

$\lambda_1 = 2$, $\lambda_2 = 0$

For $\lambda_1 = 2$: eigenvector $ \vec{v}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

For $\lambda_2 = 0$: eigenvector $ \vec{v}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$

$$ V = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Step 2**: Singular values:
$$ \sigma_1 = \sqrt{2}, \quad \sigma_2 = 0$$

$$ \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{2} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Step 3**: Left singular vectors:
$$ \vec{u}_1 = \frac{A\vec{v}_1}{\sigma_1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

For $\sigma_2 = 0$, choose any unit vector orthogonal to $\vec{u}_1$: $ \vec{u}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

$$ U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{2} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$

## Geometric Interpretation

$A = U\Sigma V^T$ says:
1. $V^T$ rotates input space to align with principal directions
2. $\Sigma$ scales along these directions
3. $U$ rotates output space

Any linear transformation = rotate, scale, rotate.

### Example 4: Effect on Unit Circle

How does $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ transform the unit circle?

**Solution:**

SVD: $A = I \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} I$ (already diagonal)

Singular values: $\sigma_1 = 3$, $\sigma_2 = 1$

Unit circle maps to ellipse with semi-axes 3 (horizontal) and 1 (vertical).

**Answer:** Transforms circle to ellipse with semi-major axis 3, semi-minor axis 1.

The singular values are the lengths of the semi-axes!

### Example 5: Rank-1 Interpretation

Write SVD as sum of rank-1 matrices.

**Solution:**

$$ A = U\Sigma V^T = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i \vec{u}_i\vec{v}_i^T$$

Each term $\sigma_i\vec{u}_i\vec{v}_i^T$ is a rank-1 matrix (outer product).

For Example 3 with one nonzero singular value:
$$ A = \sqrt{2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** SVD expresses $A$ as weighted sum of rank-1 matrices.

## Low-Rank Approximation

**Best rank-$k$ approximation** to $A$:
$$ A_k = \sigma_1\vec{u}_1\vec{v}_1^T + \cdots + \sigma_k\vec{u}_k\vec{v}_k^T$$

Keep $k$ largest singular values, discard the rest.

**Error**: $\|A - A_k\|_F = \sqrt{\sigma_{k+1}^2 + \cdots + \sigma_r^2}$ (Frobenius norm)

This is **optimal** (Eckart-Young theorem): no other rank-$k$ matrix is closer to $A$.

### Example 6: Rank-1 Approximation

For $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ with singular values $\sqrt{2}, 0, 0$, find best rank-1 approximation.

**Solution:**

From computation (similar to Example 3):
$$ A_1 = \sqrt{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & 1/2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

This appears not to match $A$, so check the singular values: since $\sigma_2 = 0$, $A$ already has rank 1, hence $A_1 = A$.

**Answer:** $A$ is already rank-1, so best rank-1 approximation is $A$ itself (no approximation needed).

### Example 7: Image Compression

A $1000 \times 1000$ grayscale image (matrix $A$) has SVD with $\sigma_1 = 1000$, $\sigma_2 = 500$, ..., $\sigma_{100} = 10$, and $\sigma_{101}, \ldots, \sigma_{1000} < 1$.

How much compression from rank-100 approximation?

**Solution:**

**Original storage**: $1000 \times 1000 = 1,000,000$ values

**Rank-100 approximation**:
- 100 singular values: 100 values
- 100 left singular vectors ($1000 \times 1$ each): $100 \times 1000 = 100,000$ values
- 100 right singular vectors ($1000 \times 1$ each): $100 \times 1000 = 100,000$ values
- **Total**: $100 + 100,000 + 100,000 = 200,100$ values

**Compression ratio**: $1,000,000 / 200,100 \approx 5:1$

**Answer:** 80% compression (from 1M to 200K values) with minimal quality loss if small singular values represent noise.

## Pseudoinverse

For non-square or singular $A$, the **Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse** $A^+$ generalizes the inverse.

$$ A^+ = V\Sigma^+ U^T$$

where $\Sigma^+$ is $n \times m$ with entries $1/\sigma_i$ for nonzero $\sigma_i$, and 0 for zero singular values.

**Properties**:
- If $A$ is invertible, $A^+ = A^{-1}$
- $A^+$ gives least-squares solution: $\hat{\vec{x}} = A^+\vec{b}$ minimizes $\|\vec{b} - A\vec{x}\|$
- $AA^+A = A$ and $A^+AA^+ = A^+$

### Example 8: Pseudoinverse of Rank-1 Matrix

For $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ with SVD from Example 3, find $A^+$.

**Solution:**

$$ \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{2} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ \Sigma^+ = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ A^+ = V\Sigma^+ U^T = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$ = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Verify: $ AA^+ = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ (projection onto range)

**Answer:** $ A^+ = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$

### Example 9: Least Squares via Pseudoinverse

Solve $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ in least-squares sense using $A^+$.

**Solution:**

Least-squares solution: $\hat{\vec{x}} = A^+\vec{b}$

This minimizes $\|\vec{b} - A\vec{x}\|$.

For Example 8 with $ \vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$ \hat{\vec{x}} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.5 \\ 1.5 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $ \hat{\vec{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.5 \\ 1.5 \end{bmatrix}$

## Applications

**Principal Component Analysis**: SVD of centered data matrix gives principal components.

**Latent Semantic Analysis**: SVD of term-document matrix finds topics.

**Collaborative Filtering**: SVD of user-item rating matrix for recommendations.

**Total Least Squares**: Unlike ordinary least squares (which minimizes vertical errors), TLS via SVD minimizes perpendicular distances.

### Example 10: Principal Components

Data matrix (centered): $ X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$

SVD gives first PC.

**Solution:**

$$ X^TX = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 \\ 2 & -2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 4 & 8 \end{bmatrix}$$

Eigenvalue: $\lambda_1 = 10$ (with eigenvector proportional to $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$)

First PC direction: along $y = 2x$.

**Answer:** Data varies primarily along line $y = 2x$ (first principal component).

### Example 11: Condition Number

**Condition number** of $A$:
$$ \kappa(A) = \frac{\sigma_{\max}}{\sigma_{\min}} = \frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_r}$$

Large condition number → ill-conditioned (sensitive to errors).

For $ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1000 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$ \kappa(A) = \frac{1000}{1} = 1000$$

**Answer:** Highly ill-conditioned (stretches 1000× in one direction, 1× in another).

### Example 12: Null Space and Range

From SVD:
- **Range of $A$**: span of first $r$ columns of $U$ (corresponding to nonzero $\sigma_i$)
- **Null space of $A$**: span of last $n - r$ columns of $V$ (corresponding to zero $\sigma_i$)

For Example 3 with one nonzero singular value:

Range$(A)$ = span of first column of $U$ = span$\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$

Null$(A)$ = span of second column of $V$ = span$\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$

**Answer:** SVD directly reveals four fundamental subspaces.

### Example 13: Polar Decomposition

Every matrix $A$ can be written as:
$$ A = PQ$$

where $P$ is positive semidefinite and $Q$ is orthogonal.

From SVD $A = U\Sigma V^T$:
$$ A = (U\Sigma U^T)(UV^T) = PQ$$

**Answer:** Polar decomposition separates stretching ($P$) from rotation ($Q$).

## Answer Key

**Problem 1: C**
$U$ and $V$ are orthogonal matrices.
$U^TU = I$ and $V^TV = I$.
Not diagonal (unless $A$ is diagonal).
Generally $V \neq U^{-1}$.

**Problem 2: C**
$\sigma_i^2$ are eigenvalues of $A^TA$ (or $AA^T$).
$\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i(A^TA)}$
Right singular vectors = eigenvectors of $A^TA$.
Left singular vectors = eigenvectors of $AA^T$.

**Problem 3: B**
Best rank-$k$ approximation: $A_k = \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i\vec{u}_i\vec{v}_i^T$
Keep $k$ LARGEST singular values.
Discard small ones (noise/redundancy).
Eckart-Young theorem: this is optimal.

**Problem 4: C**
$A^+ = V\Sigma^+ U^T$ gives least-squares solution.
$\hat{\vec{x}} = A^+\vec{b}$ minimizes $\|\vec{b} - A\vec{x}\|$.
Not equal to $A^{-1}$ unless $A$ invertible.
$AA^+$ is projection, not identity (unless $A$ invertible).

**Problem 5: C**
Condition number measures sensitivity/ill-conditioning.
Large $\kappa$ → small errors amplified.
$\kappa = 1$ → well-conditioned (all singular values equal).
Large $\kappa$ → ill-conditioned (very different singular values).
